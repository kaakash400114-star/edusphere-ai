# Grade 5 Mathematics — Complete Knowledge Guide

> **Scope:** Aligned to Grade 5 standards covering number sense, operations, fractions, decimals, number theory, geometry, measurement, data/probability, ratios, percentages, algebra, and multi-step word problems.
>
> Each section includes **definitions**, **worked examples**, **common mistakes**, and **teaching tips**.

## 1. Place Value

### 1.1 Definitions

- **Place Value:** The value of a digit based on its position in a number. Each position is 10 times the value of the position to its right.
- **Period:** A group of three places separated by commas (e.g., ones period, thousands period, millions period, billions period).
- **Billions Period:** The fourth period from the right; includes hundred-billions, ten-billions, and billions places.
- **Expanded Form:** Writing a number as the sum of each digit multiplied by its place value.
- **Standard Form:** The usual way of writing a number (e.g., 3,456,789).
- **Word Form:** Writing a number using words.

**Place Value Chart (up to Billions)**

| Period       | Billions         | Millions          | Thousands           | Ones             |
|--------------|-------------------|-------------------|---------------------|------------------|
| Hundreds     | 100,000,000,000   | 100,000,000       | 100,000             | 100              |
| Tens         | 10,000,000,000    | 10,000,000        | 10,000              | 10               |
| Ones         | 1,000,000,000     | 1,000,000         | 1,000               | 1                |

### 1.2 Powers of 10

- **Power of 10:** A number that can be written as 10 multiplied by itself some number of times.
- 10¹ = 10
- 10² = 100
- 10³ = 1,000
- 10⁴ = 10,000
- 10⁵ = 100,000
- 10⁶ = 1,000,000
- 10⁷ = 10,000,000
- 10⁸ = 100,000,000
- 10⁹ = 1,000,000,000

**Rule:** Multiplying a whole number by a power of 10 adds that many zeros to the right.
- 345 × 10³ = 345,000 (three zeros added)

**Rule:** Dividing a whole number by a power of 10 removes that many zeros from the right (when possible).
- 45,000 ÷ 10² = 450 (two zeros removed)

### 1.3 Introduction to Exponents

- **Exponent:** A small number written above and to the right of a base number telling how many times the base is used as a factor.
- **Base:** The number being multiplied.
- **Squared:** A number raised to the power of 2 (e.g., 5² = 5 × 5 = 25).
- **Cubed:** A number raised to the power of 3 (e.g., 5³ = 5 × 5 × 5 = 125).

| Exponent | Written as           | Value   |
|----------|----------------------|---------|
| 5²       | Five squared         | 25      |
| 5³       | Five cubed           | 125     |
| 10²      | Ten squared          | 100     |
| 10³      | Ten cubed            | 1,000   |
| 2⁴       | Two to the fourth    | 16      |
| 3³       | Three cubed          | 27      |

### 1.4 Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Write 4,567,890,123 in expanded form.

4,000,000,000 + 500,000,000 + 60,000,000 + 7,000,000 + 800,000 + 90,000 + 100 + 20 + 3

**Example 2:** What is the value of the 7 in 2,734,561,089?

The 7 is in the hundred-millions place. Its value is **700,000,000**.

**Example 3:** Write 5² × 10³ in standard form.

5² = 25; 10³ = 1,000; 25 × 1,000 = **25,000**.

**Example 4:** Compare: 10⁴ ___ 999,999

10⁴ = 10,000; 10,000 < 999,999, so **10⁴ < 999,999**.

### 1.5 Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---------|------------|
| Writing 5² as 5 × 2 = 10 | 5² means 5 × 5 = 25. The exponent tells how many times to multiply the base by itself, not what to multiply by. |
| Confusing place value positions across periods | Use commas as boundaries: thousands, millions, billions. Say the full period name each time. |
| Forgetting that 10⁰ = 1 | Any number to the power of 0 equals 1. This is a key convention. |
| Misreading large numbers | Read each period separately: "four billion, five hundred sixty-seven million, eight hundred ninety thousand, one hundred twenty-three." |

### 1.6 Teaching Tips

- Use a blank place value chart and have students fill in digits of large numbers.
- Connect powers of 10 to the metric system and money (dimes, dollars).
- Use grid paper (10 × 10) to visually demonstrate 10² and 10³.
- Have students write their age or a phone number in expanded form for engagement.

## 2. Operations with Whole Numbers

### 2.1 Definitions

- **Multiplication Algorithm:** A step-by-step method for multiplying multi-digit numbers using partial products.
- **Long Division:** A step-by-step process of dividing, multiplying, subtracting, and bringing down to find a quotient.
- **Dividend:** The number being divided.
- **Divisor:** The number you divide by.
- **Quotient:** The result of division.
- **Remainder:** The amount left over after division when the dividend is not evenly divisible.
- **Partial Products:** Breaking a multiplication problem into smaller, easier products that are then added together.

### 2.2 Three-Digit × Two-Digit Multiplication

**Algorithm (Standard Method):**

Multiply by the ones digit first, then by the tens digit (shifted one place to the left), then add the partial products.

**Example:** 345 × 67

```
       345
     ×  67
     -----
      2415   ←  345 × 7
     2070    ←  345 × 60 (shifted left one place)
     -----
     23115
```

Step-by-step:
1. 345 × 7 = 2,415
2. 345 × 60 = 20,700
3. 2,415 + 20,700 = 23,115

**Area Model (Partial Products):**

Break 345 into 300 + 40 + 5 and 67 into 60 + 7.

| × | 300 | 40 | 5 |
|-----|------|-----|-----|
| 60  | 18,000 | 2,400 | 300 |
| 7   | 2,100  | 280  | 35  |

Sum: 18,000 + 2,400 + 300 + 2,100 + 280 + 35 = **23,115**

### 2.3 Four-Digit ÷ Two-Digit Long Division

**Steps:** Divide → Multiply → Subtract → Bring Down → Repeat

**Example:** 4,578 ÷ 23

```
         1 9 9 r 1
       -----------
   23 / 4 5 7 8
        2 3          ← 23 × 1
        -----
        2 2 7        ← bring down 7
        2 0 7        ← 23 × 9
        -----
           2 0 8     ← bring down 8
           2 0 7     ← 23 × 9
           -----
               1     ← remainder
```

**Answer:** 199 remainder 1, or 199 1/23.

**Check:** 199 × 23 + 1 = 4,577 + 1 = 4,578 ✓

### 2.4 Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Multiply 612 × 48.

```
       612
     ×  48
     -----
      4896   ←  612 × 8
     2448    ←  612 × 40
     -----
     29376
```

**Example 2:** Divide 8,672 ÷ 34.

```
         2 5 5
       -------
   34 / 8 6 7 2
        6 8       ← 34 × 2
        -----
        1 8 7     ← bring down 7
        1 7 0     ← 34 × 5
        -----
          1 7 2   ← bring down 2
          1 7 0   ← 34 × 5
          -----
              2   ← remainder
```

**Answer:** 255 remainder 2.

### 2.5 Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---------|------------|
| Forgetting to shift the second row in multiplication | The tens digit product is shifted one place to the left (add a zero placeholder). |
| Misplacing digits in long division columns | Use graph paper or lined paper turned sideways to keep columns aligned. |
| Subtracting wrong in long division | Check: after subtracting, the result must be less than the divisor. If it's larger, the quotient digit was too small. |
| Not checking work with multiplication | Always verify: quotient × divisor + remainder = dividend. |
| Confusing dividend and divisor | "Dividend ÷ Divisor = Quotient." The dividend goes inside the division house. |

### 2.6 Teaching Tips

- Introduce the area model before the standard algorithm so students understand *why* the algorithm works.
- For long division, teach the mnemonic **Dad, Mother, Sister, Brother, Rover** (Divide, Multiply, Subtract, Bring down, Repeat).
- Use real-world contexts (total cost of 345 items at $67 each) to make multiplication meaningful.
- Start division with no remainders, then add remainders, then quotients with zeros.

## 3. Fractions

### 3.1 Definitions

- **Numerator:** The top number of a fraction; tells how many equal parts are being considered.
- **Denominator:** The bottom number of a fraction; tells how many equal parts make up one whole.
- **Proper Fraction:** A fraction where the numerator is less than the denominator (e.g., 3/5).
- **Improper Fraction:** A fraction where the numerator is equal to or greater than the denominator (e.g., 7/4).
- **Mixed Number:** A whole number combined with a proper fraction (e.g., 1 3/4).
- **Equivalent Fractions:** Fractions that represent the same value (e.g., 2/4 = 1/2).
- **Least Common Denominator (LCD):** The smallest number that both denominators divide into evenly; also the LCM of the two denominators.
- **Reciprocal:** The "flip" of a fraction (e.g., the reciprocal of 3/4 is 4/3). Multiplying a number by its reciprocal gives 1.
- **Simplest Form:** A fraction where the numerator and denominator share no common factors other than 1.

### 3.2 Adding and Subtracting Fractions with Unlike Denominators

**Rule:** Find the LCD, rewrite each fraction with the LCD, then add or subtract numerators (keep the denominator).

**Example:** 2/5 + 1/3

Step 1 — Find the LCD: Multiples of 5 = {5, 10, 15, 20, …}; Multiples of 3 = {3, 6, 9, 12, 15, …}. LCD = **15**.

Step 2 — Rewrite:
- 2/5 = (2 × 3)/(5 × 3) = 6/15
- 1/3 = (1 × 5)/(3 × 5) = 5/15

Step 3 — Add: 6/15 + 5/15 = 11/15

**Example (Subtraction):** 7/8 − 5/6

LCD of 8 and 6: Multiples of 8 = {8, 16, 24, …}; Multiples of 6 = {6, 12, 18, 24, …}. LCD = **24**.

- 7/8 = 21/24
- 5/6 = 20/24

21/24 − 20/24 = **1/24**

### 3.3 Mixed Numbers (Addition & Subtraction)

**Method 1: Convert to Improper Fractions First**

Add 3 1/4 + 2 2/3

Step 1 — Convert:
- 3 1/4 = (3 × 4 + 1)/4 = 13/4
- 2 2/3 = (2 × 3 + 2)/3 = 8/3

Step 2 — LCD of 4 and 3 = 12.
- 13/4 = 39/12
- 8/3 = 32/12

Step 3 — Add: 39/12 + 32/12 = 71/12

Step 4 — Convert back: 71 ÷ 12 = 5 remainder 11 → **5 11/12**

**Method 2: Add Whole Numbers and Fractions Separately**

- Whole numbers: 3 + 2 = 5
- Fractions: 1/4 + 2/3 = 3/12 + 8/12 = 11/12
- Combined: 5 11/12 (when fraction sum < 1)

If the fraction sum ≥ 1, convert the improper fraction back and carry over.

**Subtracting Mixed Numbers (borrowing):**

Subtract 5 1/6 − 2 4/6

Since 1/6 < 4/6, we must borrow:
- Borrow 1 from the 5: 5 becomes 4.
- 1 whole = 6/6, so 1/6 + 6/6 = 7/6.
- Now: 4 7/6 − 2 4/6 = 2 3/6 = **2 1/2**

### 3.4 Multiplying Fractions

**Rule:** Multiply numerator × numerator and denominator × denominator. Simplify before or after.

**Example (Fraction × Fraction):** 2/3 × 4/5

(2 × 4)/(3 × 5) = 8/15

**Example (Simplify First — Cross-Cancel):** 3/8 × 4/9

Cross-cancel: 3 and 9 share a factor of 3 (3→1, 9→3). 4 and 8 share a factor of 4 (4→1, 8→2).

(1 × 1)/(2 × 3) = **1/6**

**Example (Fraction × Whole Number):** 3/5 × 8

(3 × 8)/5 = 24/5 = **4 4/5**

**Example (Mixed Number × Mixed Number):** 2 1/3 × 1 1/2

Convert to improper fractions:
- 2 1/3 = 7/3
- 1 1/2 = 3/2

(7 × 3)/(3 × 2) = 21/6 = **3 1/2** (or 7/2)

### 3.5 Dividing Fractions

**Rule:** Multiply the first fraction by the **reciprocal** of the second fraction. (Keep, Change, Flip.)

**Example:** 3/4 ÷ 2/5

Keep: 3/4
Change: ÷ → ×
Flip: 2/5 → 5/2

3/4 × 5/2 = (3 × 5)/(4 × 2) = 15/8 = **1 7/8**

**Example (Whole Number ÷ Fraction):** 6 ÷ 2/3

6 = 6/1; Reciprocal of 2/3 = 3/2.

6/1 × 3/2 = 18/2 = **9**

**Example (Fraction ÷ Whole Number):** 4/5 ÷ 3

4/5 ÷ 3/1 = 4/5 × 1/3 = **4/15**

**Example (Mixed Number Division):** 3 1/2 ÷ 1 1/4

Convert: 7/2 ÷ 5/4 = 7/2 × 4/5 = 28/10 = **14/5 = 2 4/5**

### 3.6 Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---------|------------|
| Adding numerators and denominators directly (e.g., 1/3 + 1/4 = 2/7) | Must find a common denominator first. 1/3 + 1/4 = 4/12 + 3/12 = 7/12. |
| Forgetting to simplify the final answer | Always reduce to lowest terms or convert improper fractions to mixed numbers. |
| Forgetting to flip the second fraction in division | "Keep, Change, Flip" is the rule. Multiply by the reciprocal. |
| Cross-canceling across addition/subtraction | Cross-canceling only works for multiplication. |
| When subtracting mixed numbers, forgetting to borrow when the fraction part is too small | Borrow 1 whole from the whole number part first. |
| Not converting mixed numbers to improper fractions before multiplying/dividing | Mixed numbers must be converted first for multiplication and division. |

### 3.7 Teaching Tips

- Use visual fraction models (fraction bars, circles, number lines) before teaching algorithms.
- For division, explain *why* flipping works: "How many 2/5s fit into 3/4?"
- Teach cross-canceling as a helpful shortcut, not a requirement—students can always simplify at the end.
- Use cooking/baking contexts to make fractions meaningful (e.g., "You need 3/4 cup of sugar but only have a 1/3 cup measure…").
- Practice with fraction equivalency games (matching games, memory cards).

## 4. Decimals

### 4.1 Definitions

- **Decimal:** A number that has a decimal point separating the whole number part from the fractional part.
- **Decimal Place Value:** Each place to the right of the decimal point is a power of 10: tenths (0.1), hundredths (0.01), thousandths (0.001), ten-thousandths (0.0001).
- **Rounding:** Replacing a number with a nearby number that is easier to work with, based on a specific place value.

**Decimal Place Value Chart:**

| Hundreds | Tens | Ones | . | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths |
|----------|------|------|---|--------|------------|-------------|
| 100      | 10   | 1    |   | 0.1    | 0.01       | 0.001       |

### 4.2 Adding and Subtracting Decimals

**Rule:** Line up the decimal points. Add placeholder zeros if needed. Then add or subtract as with whole numbers.

**Example (Addition):** 3.45 + 12.8

```
   3.45
 + 12.80
 -------
  16.25
```

**Example (Subtraction):** 15.7 − 8.346

```
  15.700
-  8.346
--------
   7.354
```

### 4.3 Multiplying Decimals

**Rule:** Multiply as if they are whole numbers, then place the decimal point. The total number of decimal places in the product equals the sum of decimal places in the factors.

**Example (Decimal × Decimal):** 0.3 × 0.47

Multiply as whole numbers: 3 × 47 = 141

Count decimal places: 0.3 has 1 place; 0.47 has 2 places. Total = 3 places.

Move the decimal 3 places from the right: 0.141

**Answer: 0.141**

**Example (Decimal × Whole Number):** 4.56 × 12

```
     4.56
   ×   12
   ------
      912   ← 456 × 2
    456     ← 456 × 10
   ------
    54.72   ← total of 2 decimal places
```

**Example (Decimal × Decimal, larger):** 2.34 × 1.5

234 × 15 = 3,510

Decimal places: 2 + 1 = 3. Place: 3.510 = **3.51**

### 4.4 Dividing Decimals

**Rule:** If the divisor is a whole number, divide as usual and place the decimal point directly above in the quotient. If the divisor is a decimal, move the decimal point to the right to make it a whole number, then move the decimal point in the dividend the same number of places.

**Example (Decimal ÷ Whole Number):** 8.64 ÷ 3

```
       2.88
     -------
  3 / 8.64
      6
      --
      2 6
      2 4
      ---
        2 4
        2 4
        ---
          0
```

**Answer: 2.88**

**Example (Decimal ÷ Decimal):** 3.72 ÷ 0.12

Move the decimal in the divisor two places to the right: 0.12 → 12
Move the decimal in the dividend two places to the right: 3.72 → 372

372 ÷ 12 = **31**

**Example (Whole Number ÷ Decimal):** 5 ÷ 0.25

Move decimal two places: 0.25 → 25; 5 → 500

500 ÷ 25 = **20**

### 4.5 Rounding Decimals

**Rule:** Identify the place value you are rounding to. Look at the digit to the right. If it is 5 or greater, round up. If it is less than 5, keep the digit the same.

**Example:** Round 3.4671 to the nearest hundredth.

Hundredths place = 6. Digit to the right (thousandths) = 7. Since 7 ≥ 5, round up.

**Answer: 3.47**

**Example:** Round 12.093 to the nearest tenth.

Tenths place = 0. Digit to the right = 9. Since 9 ≥ 5, round up.

**Answer: 12.1**

### 4.6 Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---------|------------|
| Not lining up decimal points when adding/subtracting | Always line up the decimal points first, then add placeholder zeros. |
| Misplacing the decimal in multiplication | Count the total decimal places in all factors and apply that to the product. |
| Forgetting to move the decimal in both divisor and dividend | Whatever you do to the divisor, you must also do to the dividend. |
| Dropping trailing zeros incorrectly | 3.50 = 3.5 is fine, but when counting decimal places in multiplication, count from the rightmost digit. |
| Confusing rounding rules | 5 or above rounds up; 4 or below stays the same. |

### 4.7 Teaching Tips

- Use money ($3.45 + $12.80) to make decimal addition/subtraction concrete.
- For decimal multiplication, shade a hundredths grid to show products visually.
- Connect decimal division to fraction division (e.g., 0.5 ÷ 0.2 = 1/2 ÷ 1/5 = 5/2 = 2.5).
- Use a number line marked with tenths and hundredths for rounding practice.

## 5. Number Theory

### 5.1 Definitions

- **Prime Number:** A whole number greater than 1 that has exactly two factors: 1 and itself (e.g., 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, …).
- **Composite Number:** A whole number greater than 1 that has more than two factors (e.g., 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, …).
- **Prime Factorization:** Writing a number as the product of its prime factors.
- **Factor:** A number that divides evenly into another number.
- **Multiple:** A number that is the product of a given number and any whole number.
- **Greatest Common Factor (GCF):** The largest factor that two or more numbers share.
- **Least Common Multiple (LCM):** The smallest multiple that two or more numbers share.
- **PEMDAS:** The order of operations — **P**arentheses, **E**xponents, **M**ultiplication/**D**ivision (left to right), **A**ddition/**S**ubtraction (left to right).

### 5.2 Prime Factorization

**Method: Factor Tree**

Find the prime factorization of 72:

```
       72
      /  \
     2    36
         /  \
        2    18
            /  \
           2     9
               /  \
              3     3
```

**Prime factorization:** 72 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = **2³ × 3²**

**Method: Ladder (Repeated Division)**

```
72 ÷ 2 = 36
36 ÷ 2 = 18
18 ÷ 2 = 9
 9 ÷ 3 = 3
 3 ÷ 3 = 1
```

**Prime factorization:** 2³ × 3²

### 5.3 GCF (Greatest Common Factor)

**Method 1: List All Factors**

GCF of 24 and 36:
- Factors of 24: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24
- Factors of 36: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36
- Common factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12
- **GCF = 12**

**Method 2: Prime Factorization**

GCF of 24 and 36:
- 24 = 2³ × 3
- 36 = 2² × 3²
- GCF = take the lowest power of each common prime: 2² × 3 = **12**

**Application:** Simplifying fractions. Simplify 24/36: divide both by GCF of 12 → 2/3.

### 5.4 LCM (Least Common Multiple)

**Method 1: List Multiples**

LCM of 8 and 12:
- Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, …
- Multiples of 12: 12, 24, 36, 48, …
- **LCM = 24**

**Method 2: Prime Factorization**

LCM of 8 and 12:
- 8 = 2³
- 12 = 2² × 3
- LCM = take the highest power of each prime: 2³ × 3 = **24**

**Application:** Finding common denominators. The LCD of 1/8 and 5/12 is 24.

### 5.5 PEMDAS — Order of Operations

**Rules (in order):**
1. **P**arentheses (or grouping symbols: brackets, braces)
2. **E**xponents
3. **M**ultiplication and **D**ivision (left to right — they have equal priority)
4. **A**ddition and **S**ubtraction (left to right — they have equal priority)

**Example 1:** 4 + 3 × (7 − 2)

Step 1: Parentheses → 7 − 2 = 5
Step 2: 4 + 3 × 5
Step 3: Multiply → 3 × 5 = 15
Step 4: Add → 4 + 15 = **19**

**Example 2:** (6 + 2²) ÷ (3 + 1)

Step 1: Exponents → 2² = 2
Step 2: Parentheses → (6 + 2) = 8 and (3 + 1) = 4
Step 3: Divide → 8 ÷ 4 = **2**

**Example 3:** 10 − 3 × 2 + 8 ÷ 4

Step 1: Multiply → 3 × 2 = 6
Step 2: Divide → 8 ÷ 4 = 2
Step 3: 10 − 6 + 2 (left to right)
Step 4: 10 − 6 = 4; 4 + 2 = **6**

**Example 4:** 5 × (4 + 3)² ÷ 7

Step 1: Parentheses → 4 + 3 = 7
Step 2: Exponents → 7² = 49
Step 3: 5 × 49 ÷ 7 (left to right)
Step 4: 5 × 49 = 245
Step 5: 245 ÷ 7 = **35**

### 5.6 Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---------|------------|
| Thinking 1 is a prime number | 1 is neither prime nor composite. It has exactly one factor. |
| Multiplying before dividing (or vice versa) regardless of left-to-right | M/D and A/S are at the same level. Always go left to right. |
| Forgetting to do operations inside grouping symbols first | Parentheses always come first in PEMDAS. |
| Confusing factors and multiples | Factors divide INTO a number (≤ the number). Multiples come FROM multiplying (≥ the number). |
| Missing prime factors in factorization | Check work: multiply all prime factors back together to verify. |
| Adding before multiplying in expressions like 4 + 3 × 5 | Multiplication comes before addition: 3 × 5 = 15, then 4 + 15 = 19. |

### 5.7 Teaching Tips

- Use "Please Excuse My Dear Aunt Sally" as a PEMDAS mnemonic, but emphasize that M/D and A/S are *equal priority, left to right*.
- Have students create factor trees on poster paper for numbers up to 100.
- Connect GCF to sharing equally (e.g., "What is the largest number of equal groups for 24 and 36 items?") and LCM to repeating events (e.g., "Bus A comes every 8 minutes, Bus B every 12 minutes — when do they arrive together?").
- Play "Prime or Composite?" games with number cards.

## 6. Geometry

### 6.1 Definitions

- **Volume:** The amount of space a three-dimensional figure occupies. Measured in cubic units.
- **Rectangular Prism:** A 3D figure with 6 rectangular faces. Opposite faces are congruent.
- **Cube:** A rectangular prism where all faces are squares (all edges equal).
- **Surface Area:** The total area of all the outside faces (surfaces) of a 3D figure. Measured in square units.
- **Coordinate Plane:** A 2D grid formed by a horizontal x-axis and a vertical y-axis that intersect at the origin (0, 0).
- **Ordered Pair (x, y):** Two numbers that give the location of a point on the coordinate plane. The first number is the x-coordinate (horizontal), the second is the y-coordinate (vertical).
- **Origin:** The point (0, 0) where the x-axis and y-axis meet.
- **Quadrants:** The four sections of the coordinate plane (I, II, III, IV). In Grade 5, focus is mainly on Quadrant I (positive x and y).
- **Polygon:** A closed, flat shape made of straight line segments.
- **Regular Polygon:** A polygon with all sides and all angles equal.
- **Hierarchy of 2D Shapes:** A classification system showing how shapes are related (e.g., a square is a special type of rectangle, which is a special type of parallelogram).

### 6.2 Volume of Rectangular Prisms and Cubes

**Formula:** Volume = length × width × height (V = l × w × h)

Also: Volume = area of base × height (V = B × h)

**Units:** cubic units (cm³, in³, m³, etc.)

**Example 1:** Find the volume of a rectangular prism with l = 5 cm, w = 3 cm, h = 4 cm.

V = 5 × 3 × 4 = **60 cm³**

**Example 2:** A cube has edge length 7 cm. Find its volume.

V = 7 × 7 × 7 = **343 cm³**

**Example 3:** A box has a base of 24 in² and a height of 6 in. Find its volume.

V = 24 × 6 = **144 in³**

**Example 4 (Irregular Prism — Counting Cubes):**

If a figure is built from unit cubes, count the cubes. A figure that is 4 cubes long, 3 cubes wide, and 2 cubes high has volume = 4 × 3 × 2 = **24 cubic units**.

**Finding Missing Dimensions:**

If V = 96 cm³, l = 8 cm, w = 4 cm, find h:
96 = 8 × 4 × h → 96 = 32h → h = 96 ÷ 32 = **3 cm**

### 6.3 Surface Area Basics

**Definition:** The total area of all faces of a 3D figure.

**Rectangular Prism Surface Area:**
SA = 2(lw + lh + wh)

**Example:** Find the surface area of a rectangular prism with l = 4, w = 3, h = 2.

SA = 2(4×3 + 4×2 + 3×2) = 2(12 + 8 + 6) = 2(26) = **52 square units**

**Understanding:** There are three pairs of congruent faces: top/bottom (4×3), front/back (4×2), left/right (3×2).

**Net:** A 2D pattern that can be folded to make a 3D figure. Counting the area of all rectangles in a net gives the surface area.

### 6.4 Coordinate Plane

**Parts of the Coordinate Plane:**
- **x-axis:** Horizontal line (left ↔ right)
- **y-axis:** Vertical line (up ↔ down)
- **Origin:** (0, 0) — where axes cross
- **Quadrant I:** x > 0, y > 0 (upper right)

**Plotting Points:**
To plot (3, 4): Start at the origin → move 3 units right → move 4 units up.

**Reading Coordinates:**
- (2, 5) means x = 2, y = 5
- (0, 7) is on the y-axis
- (6, 0) is on the x-axis

**Example — Graphing Points:**
Plot and connect these points to form a shape:
- A (1, 1), B (5, 1), C (5, 4), D (1, 4)

This forms a rectangle with width = 4 and length = 3.

**Distance on the Coordinate Plane (Simple):**
For horizontal distance: subtract x-coordinates (e.g., from (2,3) to (7,3) = 7 − 2 = 5 units).
For vertical distance: subtract y-coordinates (e.g., from (3,1) to (3,8) = 8 − 1 = 7 units).

### 6.5 Classifying 2D Shapes

**Hierarchy of Quadrilaterals:**
```
Quadrilateral (4 sides)
  ├── Trapezoid (at least 1 pair of parallel sides)
  └── Parallelogram (2 pairs of parallel sides)
        ├── Rectangle (4 right angles)
        │     └── Square (4 equal sides, 4 right angles)
        └── Rhombus (4 equal sides)
              └── Square (4 right angles)
```

**Key Properties:**
- **Triangle:** 3 sides. Types: equilateral (3 equal sides), isosceles (2 equal sides), scalene (no equal sides). Also classified by angles: acute, right, obtuse.
- **Quadrilateral:** 4 sides.
- **Pentagon:** 5 sides.
- **Hexagon:** 6 sides.

**Classifying by Properties:**
- A shape has 2 pairs of parallel sides and 4 right angles → **Rectangle**.
- A shape has 4 equal sides but no right angles → **Rhombus** (but not a square).
- A shape has exactly 1 pair of parallel sides → **Trapezoid**.

### 6.6 Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---------|------------|
| Confusing volume (cm³) with area (cm²) | Volume fills space (cubes); area covers a surface (squares). |
| Forgetting all six faces for surface area | Count each pair of opposite faces. Using the formula 2(lw + lh + wh) ensures you don't miss any. |
| Swapping x and y in ordered pairs | Always remember: (x, y) = (horizontal, vertical). "Over then up." |
| Saying a square is not a rectangle | A square IS a rectangle (4 right angles). A rectangle is NOT necessarily a square. |
| Using the wrong unit for volume | Volume uses cubic units (cm³, in³, m³), not square or linear units. |
| Forgetting to label units in answers | Always include units: cm³, cm², cm, etc. |

### 6.7 Teaching Tips

- Build rectangular prisms from unit cubes (cubes, sugar cubes, or grid paper nets) so students can physically count volume.
- Use floor tiles or a chessboard analogy for the coordinate plane.
- Play "Battleship" on a coordinate grid for ordered pair practice.
- Have students sort shape cards into a hierarchy diagram to see relationships.
- Create nets from graph paper, cut them out, fold them into 3D shapes, and calculate surface area.

- **Customary System:** The measurement system used primarily in the United States (inches, feet, yards, miles; cups, pints, quarts, gallons; ounces, pounds, tons).
- **Metric System:** An international measurement system based on powers of 10 (millimeters, centimeters, meters, kilometers; milliliters, liters; grams, kilograms).

### 7.4 Conversion Worked Examples

**Example 1 (Customary — Small to Big):** Convert 48 inches to feet.

48 ÷ 12 = **4 feet**

**Example 2 (Customary — Big to Small):** Convert 3 gallons to cups.

3 × 16 = **48 cups**

**Example 3 (Customary — Multi-Step):** Convert 5 yards to inches.

5 × 36 = **180 inches** (or 5 × 3 = 15 ft, 15 × 12 = 180 in)

**Example 4 (Metric — Big to Small):** Convert 2.5 meters to centimeters.

2.5 × 100 = **250 centimeters**

**Example 5 (Metric — Small to Big):** Convert 3,450 grams to kilograms.

3,450 ÷ 1,000 = **3.45 kilograms**

**Example 6 (Metric — Multi-Step):** Convert 4.2 km to meters.

4.2 × 1,000 = **4,200 meters**

**Example 7 (Mixed Context):** A recipe calls for 2 quarts of milk. You only have cups. How many cups do you need?

2 quarts × 4 cups/quart = **8 cups**

### 7.5 Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---------|------------|
| Multiplying when you should divide (or vice versa) | Big → Small: multiply. Small → Big: divide. Think: "Are there more small units or more big units?" |
| Confusing capacity units (cups vs. ounces) | 1 cup = 8 fluid ounces. A cup is a container; a fluid ounce is a volume amount. |
| Metric: shifting decimal the wrong direction | km → m: multiply by 1,000 (move decimal right 3). m → km: divide by 1,000 (move decimal left 3). |
| Forgetting intermediate steps in multi-step conversions | Use a "chain" method: 2 gal → 8 qt → 16 pt → 32 c. Or use the direct relationship: 1 gal = 16 c. |
| Mixing up weight and mass terms | Customary uses ounces/pounds/tons (weight). Metric uses grams/kilograms (mass). |

### 7.6 Teaching Tips

- Use the "Gallon Man" visual (gallon body, quart arms/legs, pint hands, cup fingers) for customary capacity.
- For metric, emphasize the power-of-10 connection — use the same skills as multiplying/dividing by powers of 10.
- Do hands-on measurement activities with rulers, measuring cups, and scales.
- Create conversion "stairs" or number lines showing units from small to large.
- Use real-world contexts: converting recipe measurements, distances on a road trip, weight of groceries.

## 8. Data and Probability

### 8.1 Definitions

- **Line Graph:** A graph that uses points connected by lines to show how data changes over time.
- **Mean:** The average; the sum of all values divided by the number of values.
- **Median:** The middle value when data is arranged in order from least to greatest.
- **Mode:** The value that appears most frequently in a data set.
- **Range:** The difference between the greatest and least values in a data set.
- **Probability:** The likelihood that an event will happen, expressed as a fraction between 0 and 1.
- **Outcome:** A possible result of an experiment or event.
- **Sample Space:** The set of all possible outcomes of an event.
- **Simple Event:** An event with a single outcome.
- **Certain Event:** An event that will definitely happen (probability = 1).
- **Impossible Event:** An event that cannot happen (probability = 0).

### 8.2 Line Graphs

**How to Read a Line Graph:**
1. Read the title and labels.
2. Identify what the x-axis and y-axis represent.
3. Read individual data points by tracing to each axis.
4. Look for trends (increasing, decreasing, staying the same).

**How to Create a Line Graph:**
1. Draw and label the axes.
2. Choose an appropriate scale for the y-axis (equal intervals).
3. Plot each data point.
4. Connect the points with straight line segments.
5. Give the graph a title.

**Example:** Monthly rainfall data:
| Month | Rainfall (in) |
|-------|---------------|
| Jan   | 3             |
| Feb   | 2             |
| Mar   | 4             |
| Apr   | 5             |
| May   | 6             |
| Jun   | 4             |

Trends: Rainfall increased from January to May, then decreased in June. The most rainfall was in May (6 inches).

### 8.3 Mean, Median, Mode, and Range

**Example Data Set:** 12, 8, 15, 8, 22, 6, 9

**Step 1 — Arrange in order:** 6, 8, 8, 9, 12, 15, 22

**Mean (Average):**
Sum = 6 + 8 + 8 + 9 + 12 + 15 + 22 = 80
Count = 7
Mean = 80 ÷ 7 ≈ **11.4**

**Median (Middle):**
7 values, so the 4th value is the middle.
Median = **9**

**Mode (Most Frequent):**
8 appears twice; all others appear once.
Mode = **8**

**Range (Difference):**
Greatest − Least = 22 − 6 = **16**

**When there's an even number of values:**
Data: 3, 7, 9, 12
Median = average of the two middle values = (7 + 9) ÷ 2 = **8**

**No mode:** If every value appears once, there is no mode.

**Multiple modes:** If two values tie for most frequent, the data is bimodal (e.g., 3, 3, 5, 7, 7 — modes are 3 and 7).

### 8.4 Probability Basics

**Basic Rule:** Probability = (Number of favorable outcomes) ÷ (Total number of possible outcomes)

Probability is always between 0 and 1 (or 0% and 100%).

| Probability | Likelihood |
|---|---|
| 0 | Impossible |
| Close to 0 | Unlikely |
| 1/2 (0.5) | Equally likely |
| Close to 1 | Likely |
| 1 | Certain |

**Example 1:** A bag contains 3 red marbles, 5 blue marbles, and 2 green marbles. What is the probability of picking a blue marble?

Total marbles = 3 + 5 + 2 = 10
P(blue) = 5/10 = **1/2**

**Example 2:** A standard 6-sided die is rolled. What is the probability of rolling a number greater than 4?

Favorable outcomes: {5, 6} → 2 outcomes
Total outcomes: {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} → 6 outcomes
P(>4) = 2/6 = **1/3**

**Example 3:** A spinner has 4 equal sections: A, B, C, D. What is the probability of NOT landing on A?

P(A) = 1/4
P(not A) = 1 − 1/4 = **3/4**

### 8.5 Sample Space

**Definition:** The complete set of all possible outcomes.

**Example 1:** Tossing a coin.
Sample space = {Heads, Tails}

**Example 2:** Rolling a standard die.
Sample space = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}

**Example 3:** Tossing two coins (organized list or tree diagram).
Possible outcomes:
- HH, HT, TH, TT
Sample space = {HH, HT, TH, TT} — 4 outcomes

**Example 4:** Spinning a 3-section spinner (Red, Blue, Green) and rolling a die.
Sample space: (R,1), (R,2), (R,3), (R,4), (R,5), (R,6), (B,1), … (G,6) → **18 outcomes**

Use **organized lists, tree diagrams, or tables** to systematically list sample spaces.

### 8.6 Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---------|------------|
| Forgetting to arrange data before finding median | Data MUST be in order first. |
| Forgetting to divide by the count when finding mean | Mean = sum ÷ number of values (not just the sum). |
| Confusing mean and median | Mean is the average; median is the middle. They are not the same thing. |
| Counting probability as greater than 1 | Probability is always between 0 and 1. Check: numerator ≤ denominator. |
| Incomplete sample space | Use a systematic method (list, tree diagram, table) to ensure you haven't missed any outcomes. |
| Forgetting that mode can be more than one value | Data can be bimodal or multimodal. |

### 8.7 Teaching Tips

- Use the acronym **MOde** = **MO**st (the one that appears most) to remember mode.
- Have students collect real data (heights, favorite colors, number of pets) and calculate all four measures.
- For probability, use hands-on experiments (spinners, dice, coin flips) before doing calculations.
- For sample space, have students create tree diagrams on large paper.
- Discuss "fairness" in games using probability (is a game fair if P(win) = 1/3 for one player?).

## 9. Ratios

### 9.1 Definitions

- **Ratio:** A comparison of two quantities. Ratios can be written in three forms:
  - Using "to" — 3 to 5
  - Using a colon — 3:5
  - As a fraction — 3/5
- **Equivalent Ratios:** Ratios that represent the same relationship (e.g., 2:3 = 4:6 = 6:9).
- **Ratio Table:** A table that shows pairs of values that are in the same ratio.

### 9.2 Writing Ratios

**Example 1:** A class has 12 boys and 18 girls. Write the ratio of boys to girls.

**12:18** or **12 to 18** or **12/18**

This can be simplified: divide both by 6 → **2:3**

**Example 2:** A fruit bowl has 5 apples, 3 oranges, and 2 bananas.
- Ratio of apples to total fruit: 5:10 = **1:2**
- Ratio of oranges to bananas: **3:2**
- Ratio of bananas to apples: **2:5**

**Order Matters!** "Boys to girls" is different from "girls to boys."

### 9.3 Equivalent Ratios

**Finding equivalent ratios:** Multiply or divide both terms by the same number.

**Example:** Are 4:7 and 20:35 equivalent ratios?

4:7 → multiply both by 5 → 20:35 ✓ **Yes, they are equivalent.**

**Example:** Find three ratios equivalent to 3:4.

Multiply by 2: 6:8
Multiply by 3: 9:12
Multiply by 5: 15:20

### 9.4 Ratio Tables

**Example:** A recipe uses 2 cups of flour for every 3 eggs. Complete the ratio table.

| Flour (cups) | Eggs |
|:---:|:---:|
| 2 | 3 |
| 4 | 6 |
| 6 | 9 |
| 8 | 12 |
| 10 | 15 |

Each pair maintains the ratio 2:3. Multiply both columns by the same factor.

**Example (Missing Value):** If 5 bags cost $15, how much do 8 bags cost?

| Bags | Cost ($) |
|:---:|:---:|
| 5 | 15 |
| 1 | 3 |
| 8 | 24 |

Each bag costs $3 (15 ÷ 5). 8 bags × $3 = **$24**.

### 9.5 Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---------|------------|
| Forgetting that order matters in ratios | "3 to 5" is different from "5 to 3." Always read the problem carefully for the correct order. |
| Mixing up part-to-part and part-to-whole ratios | "Boys to girls" is part-to-part. "Boys to total students" is part-to-whole. These are different ratios. |
| Not simplifying ratios | Always check if the ratio can be simplified (e.g., 12:18 → 2:3). |
| Using different multipliers for the two parts of a ratio | In a ratio table, both values must be multiplied or divided by the same number. |

### 9.6 Teaching Tips

- Use colored objects (tiles, counters, candy) to demonstrate ratios visually.
- Connect ratio tables to multiplication patterns students already know.
- Have students find ratios in everyday life: recipes, sports scores, classroom demographics.
- Use double number lines as a visual tool alongside ratio tables.

## 10. Percentages

### 10.1 Definitions

- **Percent:** A ratio that compares a number to 100. "Per cent" literally means "per hundred."
- **Percent Symbol (%):** Represents "out of 100."
- **Visual Model:** A percent can be shown on a hundredths grid (10 × 10 grid where each square = 1%).

### 10.2 Percent as a Fraction out of 100

**Basic Conversions:**

| Percent | Fraction | Decimal |
|---------|----------|---------|
| 1%      | 1/100    | 0.01    |
| 10%     | 10/100 = 1/10 | 0.10 |
| 25%     | 25/100 = 1/4  | 0.25 |
| 50%     | 50/100 = 1/2  | 0.50 |
| 75%     | 75/100 = 3/4  | 0.75 |
| 100%    | 100/100 = 1   | 1.00  |

**Example:** Write 37% as a fraction.

37% = 37/100 (already in simplest form since 37 is prime and doesn't divide 100).

**Example:** Write 60% as a fraction in simplest form.

60% = 60/100 = 6/10 = **3/5**

**Example:** Write 3/20 as a percent.

3/20 = 15/100 = **15%**

### 10.3 Visual Models

**Hundredths Grid (10 × 10):**
- Shade 35 out of 100 squares → **35%**
- Shade 1 full row (10 squares) → **10%**
- Shade the entire grid → **100%**
- Shade half the grid → **50%**

**Circle Model:**
- A pie chart divided into 100 equal sections
- 25% = 1/4 of the circle shaded

**Fraction Strip / Bar Model:**
- A bar divided into 100 equal parts
- Useful for comparing fractions, decimals, and percents side by side

### 10.4 Finding Percent of a Number

**Method 1: Convert Percent to Fraction**

Find 30% of 80.

30% = 30/100 = 3/10

3/10 × 80 = (3 × 80)/10 = 240/10 = **24**

**Method 2: Convert Percent to Decimal**

Find 25% of 200.

25% = 0.25

0.25 × 200 = **50**

**Method 3: Benchmark Percents (Mental Math)**

| Percent | Shortcut |
|---------|----------|
| 10% of a number | Divide by 10 |
| 20% of a number | Divide by 5 |
| 25% of a number | Divide by 4 |
| 50% of a number | Divide by 2 |
| 100% of a number | The number itself |

**Example:** Find 20% of 45.

20% = 1/5, so 45 ÷ 5 = **9**

**Example:** Find 50% of 360.

50% = 1/2, so 360 ÷ 2 = **180**

**Example:** Find 75% of 80.

75% = 3/4, so (80 ÷ 4) × 3 = 20 × 3 = **60**

### 10.5 Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---------|------------|
| Confusing percent with the number itself (e.g., thinking 25% of 200 is 25) | 25% means 25 out of 100. 25% of 200 = 50, not 25. |
| Forgetting to simplify the fraction form of a percent | 60% = 60/100 = 3/5, not 60/100. |
| Writing 0.5% instead of 50% | 50% = 50/100 = 0.5 as a decimal. 0.5% = 0.5/100 = 0.005. Big difference! |
| Trying to add percents directly without a common context | You can't add "30% of 50" and "20% of 80" as percents of the same whole. They must refer to the same total. |

### 10.6 Teaching Tips

- Start with the 10 × 10 grid — it is the most powerful visual for understanding percent.
- Teach benchmark percents first (10%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100%) before tackling others.
- Use shopping/discount contexts ("This shirt is 30% off — what's the sale price?").
- Connect percents to fractions and decimals explicitly using conversion tables.
- Shade hundredths grids to show equivalence: 3/10 = 30/100 = 30% = 0.30.

## 11. Algebra

### 11.1 Definitions

- **Variable:** A letter or symbol that represents an unknown number (commonly x, y, n).
- **Expression:** A mathematical phrase that contains numbers, variables, and operations but NO equals sign (e.g., 3x + 5).
- **Equation:** A mathematical sentence that shows two expressions are equal, using an equals sign (e.g., 3x + 5 = 20).
- **Numerical Expression:** An expression that contains only numbers and operations (e.g., (4 + 3) × 2).
- **Evaluate:** To find the value of an expression by performing the operations.
- **Input-Output Table (Function Table):** A table that shows the relationship between an input value and an output value based on a rule.
- **Order of Operations:** PEMDAS — the sequence in which operations are performed.

### 11.2 Numerical Expressions with Parentheses

**Example 1:** Write an expression for "add 4 and 7, then multiply by 3."

(4 + 7) × 3 = 11 × 3 = **33**

**Example 2:** Write an expression for "multiply 8 by 5, then subtract 12."

(8 × 5) − 12 = 40 − 12 = **28**

**Example 3:** Write an expression for "the sum of 15 and the product of 4 and 6."

15 + (4 × 6) = 15 + 24 = **39**

**Example 4:** Evaluate (12 − 4) ÷ (2 + 2)

(12 − 4) ÷ (2 + 2) = 8 ÷ 4 = **2**

**Example 5:** Evaluate 3 × (5 + 7) − 10

3 × 12 − 10 = 36 − 10 = **26**

### 11.3 Evaluating Expressions with Variables

**Substitute the given value for the variable, then evaluate using order of operations.**

**Example 1:** Evaluate 4x + 7 when x = 5.

4(5) + 7 = 20 + 7 = **27**

**Example 2:** Evaluate 3(n − 2) + 5 when n = 8.

3(8 − 2) + 5 = 3(6) + 5 = 18 + 5 = **23**

**Example 3:** Evaluate 2a + 3b when a = 4 and b = 5.

2(4) + 3(5) = 8 + 15 = **23**

**Example 4:** Evaluate x² + 3 when x = 6.

6² + 3 = 36 + 3 = **39**

### 11.4 Simple Equations — Solve for x

**Goal:** Get x alone on one side of the equation. Do the same operation to both sides.

**Example 1 (Addition):** x + 7 = 15

Subtract 7 from both sides: x = 15 − 7
**x = 8**

Check: 8 + 7 = 15 ✓

**Example 2 (Subtraction):** x − 9 = 23

Add 9 to both sides: x = 23 + 9
**x = 32**

Check: 32 − 9 = 23 ✓

**Example 3 (Multiplication):** 4x = 36

Divide both sides by 4: x = 36 ÷ 4
**x = 9**

Check: 4 × 9 = 36 ✓

**Example 4 (Division):** x ÷ 5 = 8

Multiply both sides by 5: x = 8 × 5
**x = 40**

Check: 40 ÷ 5 = 8 ✓

**Example 5 (Two-Step):** 2x + 3 = 13

Step 1: Subtract 3 from both sides → 2x = 10
Step 2: Divide both sides by 2 → **x = 5**

Check: 2(5) + 3 = 10 + 3 = 13 ✓

**Example 6 (Two-Step):** 3(x − 4) = 18

Step 1: Divide both sides by 3 → x − 4 = 6
Step 2: Add 4 to both sides → **x = 10**

Check: 3(10 − 4) = 3(6) = 18 ✓

**Inverse Operations Summary:**
| Operation | Inverse Operation |
|-----------|-------------------|
| + x       | − x               |
| − x       | + x               |
| × x       | ÷ x               |
| ÷ x       | × x               |

### 11.5 Input-Output Tables (Function Rules)

**Example 1:** Find the rule and complete the table.

| Input (x) | Output (y) |
|:---------:|:---------:|
| 1         | 5         |
| 2         | 7         |
| 3         | 9         |
| 4         | ?         |
| 10        | ?         |

**Rule:** y = 2x + 3 (each output is 3 more than double the input)

| Input (x) | Output (y) |
|:---------:|:---------:|
| 1         | 5         |
| 2         | 7         |
| 3         | 9         |
| 4         | 11        |
| 10        | 23        |

**Example 2:** Find the rule.

| Input (x) | Output (y) |
|:---------:|:---------:|
| 3         | 12        |
| 5         | 20        |
| 7         | 28        |
| 10        | 40        |

**Rule:** y = 4x (multiply input by 4)

**Example 3:** Find the rule.

| Input (x) | Output (y) |
|:---------:|:---------:|
| 10        | 2         |
| 20        | 4         |
| 30        | 6         |
| 50        | 10        |

**Rule:** y = x ÷ 5 (divide input by 5)

### 11.6 Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---------|------------|
| Not following order of operations when evaluating | Always use PEMDAS. Parentheses first, then exponents, then M/D left to right, then A/S left to right. |
| Not performing the inverse operation on BOTH sides | Whatever you do to one side, you must do to the other. This keeps the equation balanced. |
| Confusing expressions and equations | Expressions have NO equals sign. Equations have an equals sign. |
| Forgetting to check solutions | Always substitute your answer back into the original equation to verify. |
| Incorrectly applying the distributive property | 3(x − 4) = 3x − 12, NOT 3x − 4. Multiply the 3 by BOTH terms inside. |
| Guessing input-output rules without testing | Verify the rule works for ALL given rows before applying it. |

### 11.7 Teaching Tips

- Use a balance scale metaphor: each side of the equation must stay balanced.
- Introduce variables gently with "mystery number" puzzles before formal notation.
- Use input-output machines (a fun "function machine" box concept) for function tables.
- Practice writing expressions from word phrases: "5 more than twice a number" → 2n + 5.
- Have students create their own equations and swap with a partner to solve.

## 12. Word Problems

### 12.1 Definitions

- **Multi-Step Word Problem:** A real-world problem that requires two or more operations to solve.
- **Key Words:** Words in a problem that signal which operation to use.

**Operation Key Words:**
| Operation | Key Words |
|-----------|-----------|
| Addition | sum, total, altogether, in all, combined, plus, increased by |
| Subtraction | difference, left, remaining, fewer, less than, decreased by, how many more |
| Multiplication | product, times, each, every, groups of, doubled, tripled, area |
| Division | quotient, split equally, each, per, shared, average, divided by |

> **Caution:** Key words are helpful hints, not rules. Always read the full problem and think about what makes sense.

### 12.2 Problem-Solving Strategies

1. **Read the entire problem** — don't jump to calculations.
2. **Identify what you know** and **what you need to find**.
3. **Choose the operation(s)** based on the relationship in the problem.
4. **Write an equation or draw a diagram** to represent the situation.
5. **Solve** using the correct math.
6. **Check your answer** — does it make sense in the context? Is it reasonable?
7. **Label your answer** with appropriate units.

### 12.3 Worked Examples

**Example 1 (Multi-Step — Multiplication + Subtraction):**

A school orders 24 boxes of markers. Each box contains 8 markers. After 3 weeks, 42 markers have been used. How many markers are left?

Step 1: Total markers = 24 × 8 = 192
Step 2: Markers left = 192 − 42 = **150 markers**

**Example 2 (Multi-Step — Division + Multiplication):**

A baker made 288 cupcakes. He puts them into boxes of 12. He sells each box for $8. How much money does he make if he sells all the boxes?

Step 1: Number of boxes = 288 ÷ 12 = 24 boxes
Step 2: Total money = 24 × $8 = **$192**

**Example 3 (Fractions — Real World):**

Maria walks 3/5 mile to school and 2/5 mile to the library after school. Then she walks 4/5 mile home. What is the total distance Maria walks?

Step 1: School + library = 3/5 + 2/5 = 5/5 = 1 mile
Step 2: Total = 1 + 4/5 = **1 4/5 miles**

**Example 4 (Decimals — Money):**

Jake has $45.60. He buys a book for $12.75 and a notebook for $3.45. He then splits the remaining money equally between his 2 brothers. How much does each brother get?

Step 1: Total spent = $12.75 + $3.45 = $16.20
Step 2: Remaining = $45.60 − $16.20 = $29.40
Step 3: Each brother = $29.40 ÷ 2 = **$14.70**

**Example 5 (Mixed Operations + Fractions):**

A recipe calls for 3 1/2 cups of flour. You want to make only 1/4 of the recipe. How much flour do you need?

Step 1: 3 1/2 = 7/2
Step 2: 7/2 × 1/4 = 7/8

**Answer: 7/8 cup of flour**

**Example 6 (Percent — Shopping):**

A jacket costs $120. It is on sale for 25% off. What is the sale price?

Step 1: 25% of $120 = 0.25 × 120 = $30 discount
Step 2: Sale price = $120 − $30 = **$90**

**Example 7 (Measurement Conversion):**

A road is 2.5 kilometers long. How many meters long is the road?

2.5 km × 1,000 m/km = **2,500 meters**

**Example 8 (Volume — Real World):**

A fish tank is 60 cm long, 30 cm wide, and 40 cm high. How many liters of water does it hold?
(Note: 1,000 cm³ = 1 liter)

Step 1: V = 60 × 30 × 40 = 72,000 cm³
Step 2: 72,000 ÷ 1,000 = **72 liters**

**Example 9 (Average — Data):**

The temperatures over five days were: 72°F, 68°F, 75°F, 71°F, and 69°F. What was the mean temperature?

Sum = 72 + 68 + 75 + 71 + 69 = 355
Mean = 355 ÷ 5 = **71°F**

**Example 10 (Probability — Real World):**

A bag of marbles has 6 red, 4 blue, and 5 green marbles. What is the probability of randomly picking a red marble? Express as a fraction in simplest form.

Total marbles = 6 + 4 + 5 = 15
P(red) = 6/15 = **2/5**

**Example 11 (Algebra — Real World):**

A plumber charges a $35 house call fee plus $25 per hour. If the total bill is $110, how many hours did the plumber work?

Let h = hours.
35 + 25h = 110
25h = 110 − 35 = 75
h = 75 ÷ 25 = **3 hours**

### 12.4 Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Correction |
|---------|------------|
| Not reading the full problem before starting | Read it twice. Underline the question and circle key information. |
| Choosing the wrong operation based on one keyword | Consider the overall situation. "More" doesn't always mean add (e.g., "How much more?" means subtract). |
| Forgetting to answer the actual question asked | Some problems have hidden final steps. Read the last sentence again. |
| Not checking if the answer is reasonable | If a car's speed comes out to 500 mph in a story problem, something is wrong. |
| Forgetting units or using wrong units | Label every answer. Convert units if needed before calculating. |
| Getting confused by extra information | Identify only the information relevant to solving the problem. |
| Skipping steps in multi-step problems | Write down each step clearly. Partial credit depends on showing work. |

### 12.5 Teaching Tips

- Teach the **CUBES** strategy: **C**ircle key numbers, **U**nderline the question, **B**ox key words, **E**liminate extra info, **S**olve and check.
- Use bar models (Singapore Math) for visual representation of part-whole and comparison problems.
- Have students write their own word problems — this deepens understanding.
- Practice identifying "hidden questions" (intermediate steps) in multi-step problems.
- Use graphic organizers: "What I know / What I need to find / How I'll solve / My answer."
- Encourage estimation before calculating — this builds number sense and catches errors.
