## 1. Place Value

### 1.1 Understanding Ones, Tens, and Hundreds

**Definition:** Place value is the value of a digit based on its position in a number. In our base-10 system, each place is 10 times larger than the place to its right.

**Place Value Chart (3-Digit Numbers):**

```
| Hundreds | Tens | Ones |
|----------|------|------|
|    1     |  3   |  7   |
```

The number 137 means:
- **1 hundred** = 100
- **3 tens** = 30
- **7 ones** = 7
- Total: 100 + 30 + 7 = **137**

**Base-10 Block Representation:**
- **Hundred (flat):** A square made of 10 × 10 unit blocks = 100
- **Ten (rod):** A long block of 10 unit blocks = 10
- **One (unit cube):** A single small cube = 1

**Example: Show 253 with base-10 blocks**
- 2 hundreds flats = 200
- 5 ten rods = 50
- 3 unit cubes = 3
- 200 + 50 + 3 = 253

### 1.2 Expanded Form

**Definition:** Expanded form writes a number as the sum of the value of each digit.

**Examples:**
- 245 = 200 + 40 + 5
- 508 = 500 + 0 + 8 (the zero holds the tens place)
- 630 = 600 + 30 + 0 (the zero holds the ones place)
- 319 = 300 + 10 + 9

**Word Form:**
- 245 = two hundred forty-five
- 508 = five hundred eight
- 630 = six hundred thirty

**Standard, Expanded, and Word Form Comparison:**

| Standard | Expanded | Word |
|----------|----------|------|
| 367 | 300 + 60 + 7 | three hundred sixty-seven |
| 405 | 400 + 0 + 5 | four hundred five |
| 890 | 800 + 90 + 0 | eight hundred ninety |
| 212 | 200 + 10 + 2 | two hundred twelve |

### 1.3 Comparing 3-Digit Numbers

**Steps to Compare:**
1. Start at the hundreds place (leftmost digit)
2. Compare digit by digit from left to right
3. The first digit that differs determines which number is larger

**Examples:**
- Compare 345 and 367:
  - Hundreds: 3 = 3 (same)
  - Tens: 4 < 6 → Therefore **345 < 367**
- Compare 582 and 493:
  - Hundreds: 5 > 4 → Therefore **582 > 493** (don't need to check further)
- Compare 271 and 271:
  - Hundreds: 2 = 2, Tens: 7 = 7, Ones: 1 = 1 → **271 = 271**

**Ordering Numbers (Least to Greatest / Greatest to Least):**
- Order 438, 264, 519, 401:
  - Least to greatest: **264, 401, 438, 519**
  - Greatest to least: **519, 438, 401, 264**

## 2. Addition

### 2.1 Two-Digit Addition with Regrouping

**Regrouping (Carrying):** When the sum of digits in a column is 10 or more, we regroup: write the ones digit and carry the tens digit to the next column.

**Example: 47 + 36**

```
    47
  + 36
  ----
```

**Step-by-step:**
1. Add the ones: 7 + 6 = **13** → Write 3, carry the 1 (ten)
2. Add the tens: 4 + 3 + 1 (carried) = **8**
3. Answer: **83**

```
     ¹
    47
  + 36
  ----
    83
```

**Example with Larger Numbers: 58 + 74**

```
     ¹ ¹
    58
  + 74
  ----
   132
```

1. Ones: 8 + 4 = 12 → Write 2, carry 1
2. Tens: 5 + 7 + 1 = 13 → Write 13
3. Answer: **132**

### 2.2 Adding Three 2-Digit Numbers

**Strategy 1: Add pairs first**
- 24 + 35 + 18
- First: 24 + 35 = 59
- Then: 59 + 18 = 77

**Strategy 2: Add all at once (column addition)**

```
     2¹
    24
    35
  + 18
  ----
    77
```

1. Ones: 4 + 5 + 8 = 17 → Write 7, carry 1
2. Tens: 2 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 7
3. Answer: **77**

**Teaching Tip:** Encourage students to look for "friendly number" pairs (numbers that make 10) in the ones column to simplify mental addition.

### 2.3 Mental Math Strategies

**Breaking Apart (Decomposition):**
- 46 + 23 → (40 + 20) + (6 + 3) → 60 + 9 → **69**

**Making Tens:**
- 28 + 43 → 28 + 2 + 41 → 30 + 41 → **71**
- 39 + 24 → 39 + 1 + 23 → 40 + 23 → **63**

**Compensation (Adjust and Correct):**
- 38 + 27 → Take 2 from 27, give to 38: 40 + 25 = **65**

**Using Doubles:**
- 26 + 24 → Think of 25 + 25 = 50 → **50**

**Adding on a Number Line (Jump Strategy):**

```
47 + 25 = ?

47 ---(+20)--- 67 ---(+5)--- 72
```

Break 25 into 20 + 5. Jump 20 first (to 67), then jump 5 (to 72). Answer: **72**

## 3. Subtraction

### 3.1 Two-Digit Subtraction with Regrouping (Borrowing)

**Regrouping (Borrowing):** When the digit on top is smaller than the digit below, we borrow 1 ten (10 ones) from the next column.

**Example: 52 − 37**

```
    52
  - 37
  ----
```

**Step-by-step:**
1. Ones: 2 − 7 → Can't subtract (2 < 7), so borrow 1 ten from the 5
   - Cross out 5, write 4. Add 10 to the 2 → 12
2. Ones: 12 − 7 = **5**
3. Tens: 4 − 3 = **1**
4. Answer: **15**

```
    ⁴¹²
    52
  - 37
  ----
    15
```

**Example: 81 − 46**

```
    ⁷¹¹
    81
  - 46
  ----
    35
```

1. Ones: 11 − 6 = 5
2. Tens: 7 − 4 = 3
3. Answer: **35**

**Common Mistake:** Forgetting to reduce the top digit in the tens column after borrowing. Always cross out and rewrite to remember.

### 3.2 Subtraction Across Zeros

**Example: 200 − 64**

```
    ¹⁹⁹¹⁰
    200
  - 64
  ----
```

**Step-by-step:**
1. Ones: 0 − 4 → Can't subtract. Borrow from tens, but tens is also 0.
2. Borrow from hundreds: 2 hundreds → 1 hundred, 9 tens, 10 ones
3. Ones: 10 − 4 = **6**
4. Tens: 9 − 6 = **3**
5. Hundreds: 1 − 0 = **1**
6. Answer: **136**

**Example: 305 − 178**

```
    ²⁹¹⁰ ¹⁵
    305
  - 178
  ----
```

1. Ones: 5 − 8 → Borrow from tens (0), then from hundreds
   - Hundreds: 3 → 2. Tens: 0 → 9 (plus the 10 we give to ones). Ones: 5 → 15
   - Wait — let's be careful: borrow from hundreds first.
   - 3 hundreds, 0 tens, 5 ones
   - Borrow 1 hundred = 2 hundreds, 10 tens. Borrow 1 ten from 10 = 9 tens, 15 ones.
2. Ones: 15 − 8 = **7**
3. Tens: 9 − 7 = **2**
4. Hundreds: 2 − 1 = **1**
5. Answer: **127**

**Teaching Tip:** "Zero can't borrow, so the zero next door borrows from next door." Use base-10 blocks to physically show the exchanges.

### 3.3 Checking Subtraction with Addition

**Rule:** Addition undoes subtraction. If A − B = C, then C + B = A.

**Example: Check 83 − 47 = 36**
- Add the difference and the subtrahend: 36 + 47 = 83 ✓
- Since 36 + 47 = 83 (matches the original minuend), the answer is correct!

**Always Check:**
- 52 − 37 = 15 → Check: 15 + 37 = 52 ✓
- 200 − 64 = 136 → Check: 136 + 64 = 200 ✓

**Teaching Tip:** Make checking a habit. "Always add to check!" This also reinforces addition skills.

## 4. Multiplication Introduction

### 4.1 Repeated Addition

**Definition:** Multiplication is a shortcut for repeated addition — adding the same number multiple times.

**Examples:**
- 3 groups of 4: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12, so **3 × 4 = 12**
- 5 groups of 2: 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 10, so **5 × 2 = 10**
- 4 groups of 3: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12, so **4 × 3 = 12**

**Key Vocabulary:**
- **Factors:** The numbers being multiplied (3 and 4 in 3 × 4 = 12)
- **Product:** The answer to multiplication (12 in 3 × 4 = 12)
- **Groups of:** "3 groups of 4" means 3 × 4

### 4.2 Arrays

**Definition:** An array is a rectangular arrangement of objects in rows and columns.

**Example: 3 rows of 4 objects = 3 × 4 = 12**

```
★ ★ ★ ★
★ ★ ★ ★
★ ★ ★ ★
```
3 rows, 4 columns = 12 stars total

**Reading Arrays:**
- Rows × Columns = Total (or Columns × Rows = Total — multiplication is commutative!)
- 4 rows of 5 = 4 × 5 = 20
- "The array has 4 rows with 5 in each row"

**Real-World Arrays:**
- Egg carton: 2 rows × 6 = 12
- Muffin pan: 3 rows × 4 = 12
- Window panes: 2 rows × 3 = 6

### 4.3 Equal Groups

**Drawing Equal Groups:**
- 4 groups of 3: Draw 4 circles with 3 dots in each

```
(• • •)  (• • •)  (• • •)  (• • •)
 group 1   group 2   group 3   group 4
```
4 groups of 3 = 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = **12**

### 4.4 Multiplication Facts: x2, x5, x10

**×2 Facts (Doubling):**

| Problem | Repeated Addition | Product |
|---------|-------------------|---------|
| 2 × 1 | 2 | 2 |
| 2 × 2 | 2 + 2 | 4 |
| 2 × 3 | 2 + 2 + 2 | 6 |
| 2 × 4 | 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 | 8 |

| 2 × 10 | 10 twos | 20 |

**×5 Facts (Skip Counting by 5):**

| Problem | Product | Pattern |

| 5 × 1 | 5 | ends in 5 |
| 5 × 2 | 10 | ends in 0 |
| 5 × 3 | 15 | ends in 5 |

**Pattern:** Products of 5 always end in 5 or 0!

## 5. Division Introduction

### 5.1 Sharing Equally (Fair Sharing)

**Definition:** Division means splitting into equal groups or fair shares.

**Example:**
> 12 cookies shared equally among 3 friends. How many cookies does each friend get?

```
Person 1: 🍪🍪🍪
Person 2: 🍪🍪🍪
Person 3: 🍪🍪🍪
```
12 ÷ 3 = **4 cookies each**

**Sharing Strategy:**
1. Deal out one at a time (one to each person, then repeat)
2. Count how many each person has at the end

### 5.2 Grouping (Measurement Division)

**Example:**
> 12 cookies. Put 3 cookies in each bag. How many bags do you need?

```
Bag 1: 🍪🍪🍪
Bag 2: 🍪🍪🍪
Bag 3: 🍪🍪🍪
Bag 4: 🍪🍪🍪
```
12 ÷ 3 = **4 bags**

### 5.3 Relationship Between Multiplication and Division

**Fact Families for Multiplication and Division:**

| Multiplication Facts | Division Facts |
|---------------------|----------------|
| 3 × 4 = 12 | 12 ÷ 3 = 4 |
| 4 × 3 = 12 | 12 ÷ 4 = 3 |

**Understanding the Relationship:**
- If 3 × 4 = 12, then 12 ÷ 3 = 4 (and 12 ÷ 4 = 3)
- Division is the inverse (opposite) of multiplication

**Examples:**
- 2 × 6 = 12, so 12 ÷ 2 = 6 and 12 ÷ 6 = 2
- 5 × 7 = 35, so 35 ÷ 5 = 7 and 35 ÷ 7 = 5

**Teaching Tip:** When students struggle with division, always ask: "What times what equals that number?" This connects to their multiplication knowledge.

## 6. Fractions Introduction

### 6.1 Equal Parts

**Definition:** A fraction represents equal parts of a whole.

**Key Concepts:**
- The whole must be divided into **equal** (same-size) parts
- If parts are not equal, we cannot describe them with a fraction

**Equal vs. Not Equal:**
```
Equal:            Not Equal:
|------|------|   |----|---------|
  1/2    1/2       this is NOT 1/2
```

### 6.2 Halves, Thirds, and Fourths

**One-Half (½):**
- Divide into 2 equal parts, shade 1 part
- Each part = 1 half (½)

```
┌────┬────┐
│████│    │
└────┴────┘
   ½ shaded
```

**One-Third (⅓):**
- Divide into 3 equal parts, shade 1 part
- Each part = 1 third (⅓)

```
┌────┬────┬────┐
│████│    │    │
└────┴────┴────┘
   ⅓ shaded
```

**One-Fourth or One-Quarter (¼):**
- Divide into 4 equal parts, shade 1 part
- Each part = 1 fourth (¼)

```
┌────┬────┐
│████│    │
├────┼────┤
│    │    │
└────┴────┘
   ¼ shaded
```

### 6.3 Shading Fractions

**Examples:**

| Fraction | Meaning | How to Show |
|----------|---------|-------------|
| 1/2 | 1 out of 2 equal parts | Shade 1 of 2 equal parts |
| 2/3 | 2 out of 3 equal parts | Shade 2 of 3 equal parts |
| 3/4 | 3 out of 4 equal parts | Shade 3 of 4 equal parts |
| 1/6 | 1 out of 6 equal parts | Shade 1 of 6 equal parts |

**Understanding the Notation:**
```
    numerator   (top number) → how many parts are shaded
   ─────────
    denominator (bottom number) → total number of equal parts
```

**Common Mistake:** Confusing numerator and denominator. Teaching tip: "Denominator starts with D like Down — it goes on the bottom."

## 7. Money

### 7.1 Counting Coins and Bills Up to $1

**Coin Values Review:**
- Penny = 1¢
- Nickel = 5¢
- Dime = 10¢
- Quarter = 25¢

**Counting Strategy — Start with the Largest Value:**

**Example 1:** Count these coins: 2 quarters, 1 dime, 3 pennies
- 25¢ + 25¢ = 50¢
- 50¢ + 10¢ = 60¢
- 60¢ + 1¢ + 1¢ + 1¢ = **63¢**

**Example 2:** Count these coins: 4 dimes, 2 nickels, 1 penny
- 10¢ + 10¢ + 10¢ + 10¢ = 40¢
- 40¢ + 5¢ + 5¢ = 50¢
- 50¢ + 1¢ = **51¢**

**Dollar Bills:**
- $1 bill = 100 cents (1 dollar)

**Example 3:** 3 quarters + 2 dimes + 1 nickel = 75¢ + 20¢ + 5¢ = **$1.00**

### 7.2 Making Change

**Definition:** Making change is finding how much money you get back when you pay more than the cost.

**Strategy: Counting Up**
Start at the cost and count up to the amount paid.

**Example:** You buy a pencil for 47¢. You pay with $1.00. How much change?

```
47¢ → 50¢ → $1.00
 +3¢    +50¢
```
- Count from 47 to 50: that's 3 pennies
- Count from 50 to 100: that's 50¢ (half dollar or 2 quarters)
- Total change: 3¢ + 50¢ = **53¢**

**Example 2:** A notebook costs 63¢. You pay with $1.00.
```
63¢ → 65¢ → 70¢ → $1.00
 +2¢   +5¢    +30¢
```
- 63 → 65: 2 pennies
- 65 → 70: 1 nickel (5¢)
- 70 → 100: 1 quarter (30¢)... wait, that's only 30, not right.
- 70 → 100: 3 dimes (30¢)... 2 + 5 + 30 = 37? No.
- Let me recount: 63¢ → $1.00
- 63 → 70: need 7¢
- 70 → 100: need 30¢
- Total change: 7 + 30 = **37¢**

**Teaching Tip:** Use real coins to practice counting up. Students physically hand back coins while counting.

## 8. Time

### 8.1 Telling Time to 5 Minutes

**Clock Basics Review:**
- Short hand = hour, long hand = minute
- Each number on the clock = 5 minutes
- Each tick mark between numbers = 1 minute

**Minute Hand Positions:**

| Minute Hand Points To | Minutes |
|-----------------------|---------|
| 12 | 0 (o'clock) |
| 1 | 5 (five past) |
| 2 | 10 (ten past) |
| 3 | 15 (quarter past) |
| 4 | 20 (twenty past) |
| 5 | 25 (twenty-five past) |
| 6 | 30 (half past) |
| 7 | 35 (twenty-five to) |
| 8 | 40 (twenty to) |
| 9 | 45 (quarter to) |
| 10 | 50 (ten to) |
| 11 | 55 (five to) |

**Examples:**
- Long hand on 3, short hand between 4 and 5 → **4:15** (quarter past four)
- Long hand on 9, short hand between 6 and 7 → **6:45** (quarter to seven)
- Long hand on 6, short hand on 8 → **8:30** (half past eight)

### 8.2 Elapsed Time (Hours)

**Definition:** Elapsed time is the amount of time that passes between a start time and an end time.

**Strategy: Count Forward on a Clock**

**Example 1:** The movie starts at 2:00. It ends at 4:00. How long is the movie?
- From 2:00 to 4:00 = **2 hours**

**Example 2:** School starts at 9:00. Lunch is at 12:00. How long until lunch?
- From 9:00 to 12:00 = **3 hours**

**Example 3:** You arrive at the park at 3:00. You leave at 5:00. How long were you there?
- From 3:00 to 5:00 = **2 hours**

**Teaching Tip:** Use a clock with movable hands. Physically move the hour hand and count the hours.

## 9. Geometry

### 9.1 Polygons

**Definition:** A polygon is a closed 2D shape made of straight line segments (sides).

**Key Rule:** Polygons must be closed (no gaps) and made only of straight lines.

| Polygon | Number of Sides | Number of Vertices | Example |
|---------|-----------------|--------------------|---------|
| Triangle | 3 | 3 | △ |
| Quadrilateral | 4 | 4 | □ |
| Pentagon | 5 | 5 | ⬠ |
| Hexagon | 6 | 6 | ⬡ |
| Heptagon | 7 | 7 | |
| Octagon | 8 | 8 | 🛑 |

**Sides and Vertices Relationship:**
- Every polygon has the SAME number of sides as vertices (corners)
- A triangle has 3 sides and 3 vertices
- A pentagon has 5 sides and 5 vertices

### 9.2 Faces, Edges, and Vertices of 3D Shapes

| Shape | Faces | Edges | Vertices |
|-------|-------|-------|----------|
| Cube | 6 (all squares) | 12 | 8 |
| Rectangular Prism | 6 (rectangles) | 12 | 8 |
| Triangular Prism | 5 (2 triangles, 3 rectangles) | 9 | 6 |
| Cylinder | 2 flat + 1 curved | 2 | 0 |
| Cone | 1 flat + 1 curved | 1 | 1 |
| Sphere | 0 flat + 1 curved | 0 | 0 |

### 9.3 Symmetry

**Definition:** A shape has symmetry if you can fold it in half and both sides match perfectly. The fold line is called the **line of symmetry**.

**Examples of Lines of Symmetry:**

| Shape | Lines of Symmetry | Drawing |
|-------|--------------------|---------|
| Square | 4 | vertical, horizontal, 2 diagonals |
| Rectangle | 2 | vertical, horizontal |
| Equilateral Triangle | 3 | from each vertex to midpoint of opposite side |
| Circle | Infinite | any diameter |
| Regular Hexagon | 6 | through opposite vertices and midpoints |

**Teaching Tip:** Fold paper shapes to physically test for symmetry. Use mirrors on the fold line to check if the reflection matches.

## 10. Measurement

### 10.1 Using Standard Units

**Customary Units (US):**

| Unit | Tool | Best For |
|------|------|----------|
| Inches (in) | Ruler | Small objects (pencil, eraser) |
| Feet (ft) | Yardstick/tape measure | Medium objects (desk, door) |
| Yards (yd) | Yardstick | Larger spaces (room, field) |

**Metric Units:**

| Unit | Tool | Best For |
|------|------|----------|
| Centimeters (cm) | Ruler | Small objects |
| Meters (m) | Meter stick/tape measure | Larger objects and distances |

**Measuring Steps:**
1. Line up the zero mark of the ruler with one end of the object
2. Read the number at the other end
3. Include the unit in your answer

### 10.2 Comparing Lengths

**Examples:**
- A pencil is 6 inches long. A crayon is 4 inches long. The pencil is **2 inches longer**.
- A book is 30 cm wide. Another book is 25 cm wide. The first book is **5 cm wider**.

**Comparing in Different Units:**
- 12 inches = 1 foot
- 3 feet = 1 yard
- 100 centimeters = 1 meter

**Example:** A table is 4 feet long. A desk is 3 feet long.
- How much longer is the table? 4 − 3 = **1 foot longer**

## 11. Data

### 11.1 Creating Bar Graphs

**Steps to Create a Bar Graph:**
1. Choose a title
2. Draw horizontal and vertical axes
3. Label the categories
4. Choose a scale (how much each mark represents)
5. Draw bars to the correct height
6. Include a key if needed

**Example: Favorite Pets Survey**

```
Favorite Pets

Dog     |████████████| 12
Cat     |█████████| 9
Fish    |██████| 6
Bird    |████| 4
Hamster |███| 3
        +------------+
        0 3 6 9 12 15
```

**Reading the Graph:**
- Most popular pet: Dog (12 votes)
- Least popular: Hamster (3 votes)
- Total pets: 12 + 9 + 6 + 4 + 3 = **34**

### 11.2 Pictographs

**When creating pictographs:**
- Each symbol = a set amount (key must be stated)
- Use partial symbols for amounts that don't divide evenly (Grade 2: use whole symbols only)

**Example:**

| Day | Books Read |
|-----|-----------|
| Monday | 📚📚📚 |
| Tuesday | 📚📚 |
| Wednesday | 📚📚📚📚 |
| Thursday | 📚📚📚 |
| Friday | 📚📚📚📚📚 |

**Key: 📚 = 2 books**

**Questions:**
- How many books read on Wednesday? 4 × 2 = **8 books**
- Which day had the most books? **Friday** (5 × 2 = 10 books)

### 11.3 Line Plots

**Definition:** A line plot shows data on a number line with X marks above each value.

**Example: Lengths of crayons (in inches)**

```
Lengths of Crayons

3 | X
4 | X X X X
5 | X X X X X
6 | X X
7 | X
```

**Reading a Line Plot:**
- Most common length: 5 inches (5 X's)
- How many crayons measured? 1 + 4 + 5 + 2 + 1 = **13 crayons**
- How many crayons are longer than 4 inches? 5 + 2 + 1 = **8 crayons**

## 12. Word Problems

### 12.1 One-Step Problems

**Addition:**
> Maria has 24 stickers. Her brother gives her 15 more. How many stickers does Maria have now?
>
> **24 + 15 = 39 stickers**

**Subtraction:**
> There were 56 students in the cafeteria. 23 went outside. How many are still in the cafeteria?
>
> **56 − 23 = 33 students**

**Multiplication:**
> There are 4 boxes. Each box has 6 crayons. How many crayons in all?
>
> **4 × 6 = 24 crayons**

**Division:**
> 18 marbles are shared equally among 3 friends. How many marbles does each friend get?
>
> **18 ÷ 3 = 6 marbles each**

### 12.2 Two-Step Problems

**Example 1 (Add then Subtract):**
> A store had 45 toy cars. They received 28 more. Then they sold 15. How many toy cars are left?
>
> Step 1: 45 + 28 = 73 (total after delivery)
> Step 2: 73 − 15 = 58
> **Answer: 58 toy cars left**

**Example 2 (Multiply then Add):**
> There are 3 bags with 5 apples in each bag. Someone adds 4 more apples. How many apples in total?
>
> Step 1: 3 × 5 = 15 (apples in bags)
> Step 2: 15 + 4 = 19
> **Answer: 19 apples**

**Example 3 (Divide then Multiply):**
> 24 cookies are arranged in rows of 4. How many rows? If each row costs $2, how much for all rows?
>
> Step 1: 24 ÷ 4 = 6 rows
> Step 2: 6 × $2 = $12
> **Answer: $12**

**Teaching Tip:** Have students identify the two separate questions (or operations) within a two-step problem. Use highlighters to mark the first and second parts.
