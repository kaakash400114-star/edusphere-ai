## 1. Integers

### 1.1 Recap and Number Line

**Integers:** Z = {…, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, …}

On the number line, negative integers are to the left of zero and positive integers are to the right.

### 1.2 Properties of Integers

**1. Closure Property:**

| Operation | Closed? | Example |
|-----------|---------|---------|
| Addition | Yes ✅ | (−3) + 7 = 4 ∈ Z |
| Subtraction | Yes ✅ | 5 − (−3) = 8 ∈ Z |
| Multiplication | Yes ✅ | (−4) × 3 = −12 ∈ Z |
| Division | **No** ❌ | (−4) ÷ 3 = −4/3 ∉ Z |

**2. Commutativity:**

| Operation | Commutative? | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Addition | Yes ✅ | (−3) + 5 = 5 + (−3) = 2 |
| Subtraction | No ❌ | 5 − (−3) ≠ (−3) − 5 |
| Multiplication | Yes ✅ | (−3) × 5 = 5 × (−3) = −15 |
| Division | No ❌ | 6 ÷ (−2) ≠ (−2) ÷ 6 |

**3. Associativity:**

| Operation | Associative? | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Addition | Yes ✅ | [(−3) + 5] + (−2) = (−3) + [5 + (−2)] = 0 |
| Subtraction | No ❌ | |
| Multiplication | Yes ✅ | [(−2) × 3] × (−4) = (−2) × [3 × (−4)] = 24 |
| Division | No ❌ | |

**4. Distributive Property of Multiplication over Addition:**
- a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c
- Example: (−3) × [5 + (−2)] = (−3) × 5 + (−3) × (−2) = −15 + 6 = −9
- Also: (−3) × 3 = −9 ✅

**5. Additive Identity:** a + 0 = a (0 is the additive identity)
**6. Multiplicative Identity:** a × 1 = a (1 is the multiplicative identity)
**7. Additive Inverse:** a + (−a) = 0 (for every integer a, −a is its additive inverse)
**8. Multiplicative Inverse (Reciprocal):** a × (1/a) = 1 (Note: 1/a is NOT an integer for most a, so this applies in Q, not Z)

### 1.3 Multiplication of Integers — Extended

**Multiplication of more than two integers:**
- Count the number of negative signs.
- **Even number of negatives → Result is positive**
- **Odd number of negatives → Result is negative**

**Worked Example 1:** (−2) × (−3) × (−4) × (−5)
- 4 negative signs (even) → Positive
- = 2 × 3 × 4 × 5 = **120**

**Worked Example 2:** (−1) × (−1) × (−1) × (−1) × (−1)
- 5 negative signs (odd) → Negative
- = **−1**

### 1.4 Division of Integers — Extended

**Rules (same sign pattern as multiplication):**

| Operation | Result |
|-----------|--------|
| (+) ÷ (+) | (+) |
| (+) ÷ (−) | (−) |
| (−) ÷ (+) | (−) |
| (−) ÷ (−) | (+) |

**Worked Example 3:** (−48) ÷ 6 = **−8**
**Worked Example 4:** (−45) ÷ (−9) = **5**

### 1.5 Properties of Division

- Division by zero is **undefined**.
- Zero divided by any non-zero integer = **0**.
- a ÷ 0 is NOT defined; 0 ÷ a = 0 (a ≠ 0).
- Division is NOT commutative, associative, or distributive.

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ Forgetting that integers are closed under subtraction (unlike whole numbers)
- ❌ Counting negative signs incorrectly in multi-factor products
- ❌ Attempting to divide by zero

### Teaching Tips
- Use bank account balance (deposits/withdrawals) for real-world context
- Play "sign game" — students predict sign of product/quotient before calculating
- Have students verify properties with both positive and negative examples

## 2. Fractions and Decimals

### 2.1 Operations with Fractions

**Addition and Subtraction:**
- Convert to like fractions (common denominator = LCM of denominators).
- Add/subtract numerators, keep common denominator.
- Simplify the result.

**Worked Example 1:** 3/8 + 5/6 − 1/4
- LCM(8, 6, 4) = 24
- 3/8 = 9/24; 5/6 = 20/24; 1/4 = 6/24
- 9/24 + 20/24 − 6/24 = 23/24

**Multiplication:** Multiply numerators and denominators separately; simplify before or after.
- a/b × c/d = (a × c)/(b × d)

**Worked Example 2:** 3/7 × 14/15
- = (3 × 14)/(7 × 15) = 42/105
- Simplify: HCF(42, 105) = 21; 42 ÷ 21 = 2, 105 ÷ 21 = 5
- **= 2/5**
- (Shortcut: Cancel 3 with 15 → 1/5; cancel 7 with 14 → 2/1; result: 2/5)

**Division:** Multiply by the reciprocal (flip the second fraction).
- a/b ÷ c/d = a/b × d/c

**Worked Example 3:** 4/9 ÷ 2/3
- = 4/9 × 3/2 = 12/18 = **2/3**

**Mixed Number Operations:**
- Convert mixed numbers to improper fractions first.
- Then apply the appropriate operation.

**Worked Example 4:** 2⅓ × 1½
- = 7/3 × 3/2 = 21/6 = **7/2 = 3½**

### 2.2 Decimal Numbers — Recap

**Place Values:**

| Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones | . | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths |
|-----------|-----------|------|------|---|---------|-----------|-------------|
| | | | 3 | . | 7 | 5 | 2 |

- 3.752 = 3 ones + 7 tenths + 5 hundredths + 2 thousandths

**Writing Fractions as Decimals:**
- Fractions with denominator 10, 100, 1000… → Write directly
- Other fractions → Perform long division

**Worked Example 5:** Convert 3/8 to decimal
- 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375

**Terminating vs Non-terminating Decimals:**
- Terminating: 1/4 = 0.25 (division ends)
- Non-terminating repeating: 1/3 = 0.333… = 0.3̄

### 2.3 Operations with Decimals

**Addition/Subtraction:** Line up decimal points; add/subtract as usual.

**Worked Example 6:** 12.45 + 7.382
```
  12.450
+  7.382
--------
  19.832
```

**Multiplication:**
- Multiply as whole numbers.
- Count total decimal places in both factors.
- Place decimal point in the product accordingly.

**Worked Example 7:** 2.5 × 1.4
- 25 × 14 = 350
- Total decimal places: 1 + 1 = 2
- **Answer: 3.50 = 3.5**

**Division of Decimals:**
- a) Dividing a decimal by a whole number: Divide normally; place decimal in quotient.
  - 15.6 ÷ 3 = **5.2**

- b) Dividing a decimal by a decimal: Multiply both numbers by a power of 10 to make divisor a whole number.
  - 3.72 ÷ 0.6
  - Multiply both by 10: 37.2 ÷ 6 = **6.2**

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ Not converting mixed numbers before multiplication/division
- ❌ Misplacing the decimal point after multiplication
- ❌ Forgetting to align decimal points in addition/subtraction

### Teaching Tips
- Use money (₹, $) for decimal operations context
- Grid shading for visualizing decimal multiplication
- Connect fractions and decimals with conversion drills

## 3. Rational Numbers

### 3.1 Definition

**Rational Number:** A number that can be expressed in the form **p/q** where p and q are integers and q ≠ 0.

- Examples: 1/2, −3/7, 5 (= 5/1), 0 (= 0/1), −2 (= −2/1), 0.5 (= 1/2), 0.75 (= 3/4)
- **All integers are rational numbers** (since any integer n = n/1).
- **All fractions are rational numbers** (by definition).
- Terminating and repeating decimals are rational.

**Not Rational (Irrational):** Non-terminating, non-repeating decimals like √2 = 1.41421356…, π = 3.14159…

**Important:** Q = {p/q : p, q ∈ Z, q ≠ 0}

### 3.2 Rational Numbers on the Number Line

To represent a rational number on the number line:

**Worked Example 1:** Represent 3/5 on the number line.
1. Draw a number line from 0 to 1.
2. Divide the segment [0, 1] into 5 equal parts.
3. Mark the 3rd division point → **3/5**

**Worked Example 2:** Represent −2/3 on the number line.
1. Note it lies between −1 and 0 (negative side).
2. Divide the segment [−1, 0] into 3 equal parts.
3. Mark the 2nd division point from −1 → **−2/3**

### 3.3 Positive and Negative Rational Numbers

- **Positive:** Both numerator and denominator have the same sign. Examples: 3/5, −3/−5
- **Negative:** Numerator and denominator have different signs. Examples: −3/5, 3/−5

### 3.4 Comparison of Rational Numbers

**Step 1:** Make denominators equal (find LCM) if they are different.
**Step 2:** Compare numerators.

**Worked Example 3:** Which is greater: −2/5 or −3/7?
- LCM(5, 7) = 35
- −2/5 = −14/35; −3/7 = −15/35
- −14/35 > −15/35 (less negative = greater)
- **−2/5 > −3/7**

### 3.5 Operations with Rational Numbers

**Addition:**
- Same sign: Add absolute values, keep the sign.
- Different signs: Subtract smaller absolute value from larger, take sign of larger.

**Worked Example 4:** (−3)/4 + 5/6
- LCM(4, 6) = 12
- (−3)/4 = −9/12; 5/6 = 10/12
- −9/12 + 10/12 = 1/12

**Subtraction:** a/b − c/d = a/b + (−c/d)

**Multiplication:** (a/b) × (c/d) = (a × c)/(b × d)
- Apply integer sign rules.

**Worked Example 5:** (−2/3) × (4/5) = −8/15

**Division:** (a/b) ÷ (c/d) = (a/b) × (d/c)
- Multiply by the reciprocal.

**Worked Example 6:** (−3/4) ÷ (9/8) = (−3/4) × (8/9) = −24/36 = **−2/3**

### 3.6 Properties of Rational Numbers

| Property | Addition | Multiplication |
|----------|----------|---------------|
| Closure | Yes ✅ | Yes ✅ |
| Commutative | Yes ✅ | Yes ✅ |
| Associative | Yes ✅ | Yes ✅ |
| Distributive | a × (b + c) = ab + ac ✅ | |
| Additive Identity | 0 | |
| Multiplicative Identity | | 1 |
| Additive Inverse | −(p/q) exists | |
| Multiplicative Inverse | | q/p exists (p ≠ 0) |

**Note:** Rational numbers are NOT closed under division (division by zero is undefined).

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ Thinking all decimals are rational (only terminating and repeating ones are)
- ❌ Forgetting to take reciprocal when dividing
- ❌ Mishandling signs in multiplication/division

### Teaching Tips
- Show that Q is a superset of Z and includes all fractions
- Use fraction strips and number lines for comparison
- Explore patterns: 1/9 = 0.1̄, 2/9 = 0.2̄, 3/9 = 0.3̄, etc.

## 4. Exponents and Powers

### 4.1 Introduction

**Exponential Notation:** aⁿ means "a multiplied by itself n times"
- aⁿ = a × a × a × … × a (n times)
- **Base:** a (the number being multiplied)
- **Exponent (Power):** n (how many times)

**Examples:**
- 2⁵ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32
- 10³ = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000
- 3⁴ = 81

### 4.2 Laws of Exponents

For any non-zero integers a and b, and whole numbers m and n:

**Law 1: Multiplying Same Bases**
- aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ
- Example: 3² × 3⁴ = 3²⁺⁴ = 3⁶ = 729

**Law 2: Dividing Same Bases**
- aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ (for m > n)
- Example: 5⁶ ÷ 5² = 5⁶⁻² = 5⁴ = 625

**Law 3: Power of a Power**
- (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐˣⁿ
- Example: (2³)⁴ = 2³ˣ⁴ = 2¹²

**Law 4: Multiplying Same Powers**
- aᵐ × bᵐ = (a × b)ᵐ
- Example: 2³ × 5³ = (2 × 5)³ = 10³ = 1000

**Law 5: Dividing Same Powers**
- aᵐ ÷ bᵐ = (a ÷ b)ᵐ
- Example: 8³ ÷ 2³ = (8 ÷ 2)³ = 4³ = 64

**Law 6: Zero Exponent Rule**
- a⁰ = 1 (for any non-zero a)
- Example: 7⁰ = 1; 100⁰ = 1; (−5)⁰ = 1

**Law 7: Negative Exponent Rule (introductory)**
- a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ
- Example: 2⁻³ = 1/2³ = 1/8

### 4.3 Expressing Large Numbers in Standard Form (Exponential Form)

**Standard Form:** A number written as a × 10ⁿ where 1 ≤ a < 10 and n is a positive integer.

**Steps:**
1. Move the decimal point so only one non-zero digit is to its left.
2. Count the number of places moved.
3. If moved to the left, n is positive; if moved right, n is negative.

**Worked Example 1:** Write 345,000,000 in standard form.
- Move decimal: 3.45 (moved 8 places to the left)
- **Answer: 3.45 × 10⁸**

**Worked Example 2:** Write 0.0000056 in standard form.
- Move decimal: 5.6 (moved 6 places to the right)
- **Answer: 5.6 × 10⁻⁶**

### 4.4 Using Exponents with Large Numbers

| Number | Exponential Form |
|--------|-----------------|
| 10 | 10¹ |
| 100 | 10² |
| 1,000 | 10³ |
| 10,000 | 10⁴ |
| 1,00,000 (1 lakh) | 10⁵ |
| 10,00,000 (10 lakh / 1 million) | 10⁶ |
| 1,00,00,000 (1 crore) | 10⁷ |

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ Confusing a⁰ with 0 (a⁰ = 1, not 0)
- ❌ Adding exponents when bases are different: 2³ × 3³ ≠ 6³⁺³
- ❌ Forgetting the zero exponent rule applies only to non-zero bases

### Teaching Tips
- Use folding paper activity: fold in half repeatedly → 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 = 2¹, 2², 2³, 2⁴, 2⁵
- Compare writing out large numbers vs exponential form for efficiency
- Create a "laws of exponents" reference card for students

## 5. Algebraic Expressions

### 5.1 Terms, Coefficients, and Variables

**Variable:** A symbol that can take different values (e.g., x, y, z, a, b).

**Constant:** A fixed value (e.g., 5, −3, ½).

**Term:** A constant, a variable, or a product of constants and variables.
- In 5x² + 3x − 7: the terms are **5x²**, **3x**, and **−7**.

**Coefficient:** The numerical factor of a term (including its sign).
- In 5x²: coefficient of x² is **5**
- In 3x: coefficient of x is **3**
- In −7: it is a constant term (coefficient concept applies to variables)

### 5.2 Like and Unlike Terms

**Like Terms:** Terms that have the **same variable(s) raised to the same power(s)**.
- 3x and 5x are like terms
- 2x² and −7x² are like terms
- 4xy and 9xy are like terms

**Unlike Terms:** Terms that have different variables or the same variable raised to different powers.
- 3x and 3y are unlike terms
- 5x² and 5x are unlike terms (different powers of x)
- 2xy and 2x are unlike terms

### 5.3 Addition and Subtraction of Algebraic Expressions

**Rule:** Only like terms can be added or subtracted (combine coefficients; keep the variable part unchanged).

**Adding Like Terms:**
- 3x + 5x = (3 + 5)x = 8x
- −2a + 7a = (−2 + 7)a = 5a

**Subtracting Like Terms:**
- 8y − 3y = (8 − 3)y = 5y
- 4m − 9m = (4 − 9)m = −5m

**Adding/Subtracting Expressions:**

**Worked Example 1:** Add 3x² + 5x − 2 and x² − 3x + 7
- Group like terms:
  - x² terms: 3x² + x² = 4x²
  - x terms: 5x + (−3x) = 2x
  - Constants: (−2) + 7 = 5
- **Answer: 4x² + 2x + 5**

**Worked Example 2:** Subtract (2a − 3b + 4) from (5a + 7b − 8)
- = 5a + 7b − 8 − 2a + 3b − 4
- Group: (5a − 2a) + (7b + 3b) + (−8 − 4)
- **= 3a + 10b − 12**

**Worked Example 3:** Find the value of 3x + 2y when x = 2 and y = −1
- = 3(2) + 2(−1) = 6 − 2 = **4**

### 5.4 Finding the Value of an Expression

Substitute given values and evaluate.

**Worked Example 4:** If x = 3, find x² − 2x + 1
- = 3² − 2(3) + 1 = 9 − 6 + 1 = **4**

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ Adding/subtracting unlike terms: 3x + 3y ≠ 6xy
- ❌ Forgetting to distribute the negative sign when subtracting an expression
- ❌ Confusing 2x with x²

### Teaching Tips
- Color-code like terms for visual grouping
- Use "expression machines" — input values, output results
- Practice with column addition of expressions (aligning like terms vertically)

## 6. Linear Equations in One Variable

### 6.1 Definition

**Equation:** A mathematical statement with an equals sign (=) asserting that two expressions are equal.

**Linear Equation in One Variable:** An equation of the form ax + b = 0, where a ≠ 0.
- The highest power of the variable is 1.
- It has exactly **one solution**.

**Examples:**
- 2x + 3 = 11 (solution: x = 4)
- 5y − 7 = 18 (solution: y = 5)
- 3(x − 2) = 9 (solution: x = 5)

### 6.2 Solving Linear Equations — Step-by-Step

**Goal:** Isolate the variable on one side of the equation.

**Rules:**
1. Add the same quantity to both sides.
2. Subtract the same quantity from both sides.
3. Multiply both sides by the same non-zero quantity.
4. Divide both sides by the same non-zero quantity.

**Worked Example 1:** Solve 3x + 7 = 22
- Step 1: Subtract 7 from both sides
  - 3x = 22 − 7 = 15
- Step 2: Divide both sides by 3
  - x = 15 ÷ 3 = **5**

**Worked Example 2:** Solve 5(x − 2) = 25
- Step 1: Expand: 5x − 10 = 25
- Step 2: Add 10 to both sides
  - 5x = 35
- Step 3: Divide by 5
  - x = **7**

**Worked Example 3:** Solve (2x + 3)/5 = 3
- Step 1: Multiply both sides by 5
  - 2x + 3 = 15
- Step 2: Subtract 3
  - 2x = 12
- Step 3: Divide by 2
  - x = **6**

**Worked Example 4:** Solve 4(x + 1) − 3(x − 2) = 14
- Step 1: Expand both sides
  - 4x + 4 − 3x + 6 = 14
- Step 2: Combine like terms
  - x + 10 = 14
- Step 3: Subtract 10
  - x = **4**

### 6.3 Transposition Method

Moving a term from one side to the other by changing its sign.

- If ax + b = c, then ax = c − b (moved b to the right, changed its sign)
- If x/a = b, then x = a × b (moved a to the right, changed ÷ to ×)

**Worked Example 5:** Solve 7x − 5 = 2x + 10
- Move 2x to LHS: 7x − 2x − 5 = 10
- Move −5 to RHS: 7x − 2x = 10 + 5
- 5x = 15
- x = **3**

### 6.4 Applications (Word Problems)

**Steps:**
1. Read the problem carefully.
2. Identify the unknown and assign a variable.
3. Form the equation.
4. Solve the equation.
5. Verify the solution.
6. State the answer clearly.

**Worked Example 6:** The sum of three consecutive numbers is 66. Find the numbers.
- Let the numbers be x, x + 1, x + 2
- x + (x + 1) + (x + 2) = 66
- 3x + 3 = 66
- 3x = 63
- x = 21
- **Numbers: 21, 22, 23**
- Check: 21 + 22 + 23 = 66 ✅

**Worked Example 7:** A number is 3 more than another number. Their sum is 27. Find both numbers.
- Let the numbers be x and x + 3
- x + (x + 3) = 27
- 2x = 24
- x = 12
- **Numbers: 12 and 15**

**Worked Example 8:** Arjun is twice as old as his sister. The sum of their ages is 36 years. Find their ages.
- Let sister's age = x, Arjun's age = 2x
- x + 2x = 36
- 3x = 36
- x = 12
- **Sister = 12 years, Arjun = 24 years**

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ Forgetting to change the sign when transposing
- ❌ Not distributing correctly: 3(x + 2) ≠ 3x + 2
- ❌ Dividing by the variable's coefficient incorrectly

### Teaching Tips
- Use a balance scale (physical or virtual) to demonstrate equation solving
- Start with simple equations and gradually increase complexity
- Have students write their own word problems and exchange with classmates

## 7. Lines and Angles

### 7.1 Basic Terms

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Point** | A dot that determines a location (no dimension) |
| **Line** | Extends infinitely in both directions |
| **Line Segment** | A portion of a line with two endpoints |
| **Ray** | Extends infinitely in one direction from an endpoint |
| **Angle** | Formed by two rays with a common endpoint (vertex) |

**Complementary Angles:** Two angles whose sum is **90°**.
- If ∠A + ∠B = 90°, then ∠A and ∠B are complementary.
- Each angle is the **complement** of the other.
- Example: 35° and 55° are complementary.

**Supplementary Angles:** Two angles whose sum is **180°**.
- If ∠A + ∠B = 180°, then ∠A and ∠B are supplementary.
- Each angle is the **supplement** of the other.
- Example: 120° and 60° are supplementary.

**Adjacent Angles:** Two angles that:
- Have a common vertex
- Have a common arm
- Non-common arms are on opposite sides of the common arm

**Linear Pair:** Two adjacent angles whose non-common arms form a straight line (sum = 180°).

### 7.2 Pairs of Angles Formed by a Transversal

**Transversal:** A line that intersects two or more lines at different points.

When a transversal cuts two lines, 8 angles are formed.

**If the two lines are parallel:**

| Pair | Definition | Property |
|------|-----------|----------|
| **Corresponding Angles** | Same relative position at each intersection | Equal (congruent) |
| **Alternate Interior Angles** | Inside the two lines, on opposite sides of transversal | Equal (congruent) |
| **Alternate Exterior Angles** | Outside the two lines, on opposite sides of transversal | Equal (congruent) |
| **Consecutive Interior Angles** (Same-side Interior) | Inside the two lines, on the same side of transversal | Supplementary (sum = 180°) |

**Visual Guide:**
```
Line 1:       ∠1  /  ∠2
           --------/--------
                  / transversal
           --------\--------
Line 2:       ∠3  \  ∠4
```
- ∠1 and ∠3 are corresponding angles (same position)
- ∠2 and ∠4 are corresponding angles
- Interior angles on same side: supplementary

**Worked Example 1:** Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. One angle measures 65°. Find all other angles.
- Corresponding angle = 65°
- Alternate interior angle = 65°
- Angle on a straight line with 65° = 180° − 65° = 115°
- All other angles: **65° and 115° (four of each)**

### 7.3 Angle Sum Property at a Point

**The sum of angles around a point is 360°.**

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ Confusing corresponding angles with alternate interior angles
- ❌ Assuming the angle relationships apply when lines are NOT parallel
- ❌ Forgetting that linear pairs are always supplementary

### Teaching Tips
- Use coloured paper strips and pins to create angle models
- Draw parallel lines with transversals and color-code angle pairs
- Search for parallel lines and transversals in the real world (railroad tracks, window grills)

## 8. Triangles

### 8.1 Properties of Triangles

**Angle Sum Property:** The sum of the three interior angles of any triangle = **180°**.

**Worked Example 1:** In triangle ABC, if ∠A = 50° and ∠B = 70°, find ∠C.
- ∠C = 180° − (50° + 70°) = 180° − 120° = **60°**

**Exterior Angle Property:** An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of its two opposite interior angles.

```
         C
        /\
       /  \
    E/_____\D
     \     /
      \   /
       \ /
        A--------B
```
- ∠ACD (exterior at C) = ∠A + ∠B

**Worked Example 2:** In triangle PQR, an exterior angle at R measures 120°. If ∠P = 45°, find ∠Q.
- Exterior angle = ∠P + ∠Q
- 120° = 45° + ∠Q
- ∠Q = 120° − 45° = **75°**

### 8.2 Types of Triangles (Grade 7 Extension)

**Based on Sides:**
- Equilateral: All sides equal (all angles = 60°)
- Isosceles: Two sides equal (angles opposite equal sides are equal)
- Scalene: All sides different

**Based on Angles:**
- Acute: All angles < 90°
- Right: One angle = 90°
- Obtuse: One angle > 90°

**Pythagorean Theorem (Introduction):**

In a **right-angled triangle**, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.

**Formula:** a² + b² = c² (where c is the hypotenuse — the side opposite the right angle)

**Worked Example 3:** In a right triangle, the two shorter sides are 3 cm and 4 cm. Find the hypotenuse.
- c² = 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25
- c = √25 = **5 cm**

**Common Pythagorean Triples (memorize!):**
- (3, 4, 5) → 3² + 4² = 5²
- (5, 12, 13) → 5² + 12² = 13²
- (6, 8, 10) → 6² + 8² = 10²
- (8, 15, 17) → 8² + 15² = 17²

### 8.3 Congruence of Triangles

**Congruent Triangles:** Two triangles are congruent if they have the **same shape and same size**. All corresponding sides and angles are equal.

**Notation:** ΔABC ≅ ΔPQR means:
- AB = PQ, BC = QR, AC = PR
- ∠A = ∠P, ∠B = ∠Q, ∠C = ∠R

**Conditions for Congruence:**

| Criterion | Full Name | Requirement |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| **SSS** | Side-Side-Side | All three sides of one triangle equal to corresponding three sides of the other |
| **SAS** | Side-Angle-Side | Two sides and the **included angle** of one equal to those of the other |
| **ASA** | Angle-Side-Angle | Two angles and the **included side** of one equal to those of the other |
| **RHS** | Right angle-Hypotenuse-Side | Right angle, hypotenuse, and one side of one right triangle equal to those of the other |

**Note:** ASS (or SSA) and AAA are NOT valid congruence criteria. AAA gives similar triangles, not necessarily congruent.

**Worked Example 4:** In ΔABC and ΔDEF:
- AB = DE = 5 cm
- BC = EF = 7 cm
- AC = DF = 6 cm
- **By SSS criterion, ΔABC ≅ ΔDEF**

**Worked Example 5:** In ΔPQR and ΔSTU (both right-angled):
- ∠P = ∠S = 90°
- PQ = ST = 13 cm (hypotenuse)
- PR = SU = 5 cm
- **By RHS criterion, ΔPQR ≅ ΔSTU**

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ Using AAA as a congruence criterion (it gives similarity, not congruence)
- ❌ Misidentifying the hypotenuse (it's always opposite the right angle)
- ❌ Confusing SAS with SSA (the angle must be between the two sides)

### Teaching Tips
- Use straws of fixed lengths to construct triangles and test congruence conditions
- Paper cutting and superimposing to verify congruence
- "Pythagorean theorem proof by squares" — draw squares on each side of a right triangle

## 9. Comparing Quantities

### 9.1 Ratios

**Ratio:** Comparison of two quantities by division (same units).

**Equivalent Ratios:** Multiply or divide both terms by the same number.

**Worked Example 1:** Divide ₹600 in the ratio 2 : 3.
- Total parts = 2 + 3 = 5
- One part = 600 ÷ 5 = 120
- First share = 2 × 120 = ₹240
- Second share = 3 × 120 = ₹360

### 9.2 Percentages

**Percentage:** A fraction with denominator 100. The symbol "%" means "per hundred."

**Conversion Formulas:**

| From | To | Method |
|------|----|--------|
| Fraction → Percentage | Multiply by 100 and add % sign | 3/4 × 100 = 75% |
| Percentage → Fraction | Remove %, divide by 100 | 45% = 45/100 = 9/20 |
| Decimal → Percentage | Multiply by 100 | 0.35 × 100 = 35% |
| Percentage → Decimal | Divide by 100 | 72% = 0.72 |

**Worked Example 2:** Convert 7/8 to a percentage.
- 7/8 × 100 = 700/8 = **87.5%**

### 9.3 Profit, Loss, and Discount

**Key Terms:**

| Term | Definition | Formula |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Cost Price (CP)** | Price at which an item is bought | |
| **Selling Price (SP)** | Price at which an item is sold | |
| **Profit** | SP > CP | Profit = SP − CP |
| **Loss** | SP < CP | Loss = CP − SP |
| **Profit %** | Profit as percentage of CP | (Profit/CP) × 100 |
| **Loss %** | Loss as percentage of CP | (Loss/CP) × 100 |
| **Discount** | Reduction on marked price | Discount = Marked Price − SP |
| **Discount %** | Discount as percentage of MP | (Discount/MP) × 100 |

**Worked Example 3:** A shopkeeper buys a shirt for ₹400 and sells it for ₹500. Find profit and profit %.
- Profit = 500 − 400 = ₹100
- Profit % = (100/400) × 100 = **25%**

**Worked Example 4:** A laptop marked at ₹50,000 is sold at a 10% discount. Find the selling price.
- Discount = 10% of 50,000 = 5,000
- SP = 50,000 − 5,000 = **₹45,000**

### 9.4 Simple Interest

**Simple Interest (SI):** Interest calculated only on the principal amount.

**Formula:**
- **SI = (P × R × T) / 100**
- **Amount (A) = P + SI**

Where:
- P = Principal (the original amount borrowed/invested)
- R = Rate of interest per annum (%)
- T = Time in years

**Worked Example 5:** Find the simple interest on ₹5,000 at 8% per annum for 3 years.
- SI = (5000 × 8 × 3) / 100 = 1,20,000 / 100 = **₹1,200**
- Amount = 5000 + 1200 = **₹6,200**

**Worked Example 6:**₹12,000 is invested at 5% per annum. In how many years will it earn ₹1,800 as simple interest?
- SI = (P × R × T) / 100
- 1800 = (12000 × 5 × T) / 100
- 1800 = 600T
- T = 1800/600 = **3 years**

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ Calculating profit % on SP instead of CP (always use CP!)
- ❌ Forgetting to convert time to years (if given in months, divide by 12)
- ❌ Confusing discount % with discount amount

### Teaching Tips
- Use real newspaper ads/marketing flyers for discount problems
- Simulate a "classroom shop" with buying and selling activities
- Create a bank deposit scenario for simple interest

## 10. Data Handling

### 10.1 Measures of Central Tendency

**Mean (Arithmetic Average):**
- Mean = (Sum of all observations) / (Total number of observations)
- Mean = (x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ) / n

**Median:** The middle value when data is arranged in ascending/descending order.
- If n is odd: Median = (n+1)/2-th observation
- If n is even: Median = average of (n/2)-th and (n/2 + 1)-th observations

**Mode:** The value that occurs most frequently in the data.
- A dataset can have one mode, more than one mode (bimodal), or no mode.

**Worked Example 1:** Find the mean, median, and mode of: 12, 15, 18, 12, 14, 15, 12, 16
- **Mean** = (12 + 15 + 18 + 12 + 14 + 15 + 12 + 16) / 8 = 114 / 8 = **14.25**
- **Sorted:** 12, 12, 12, 14, 15, 15, 16, 18 (n = 8, even)
  - Median = average of 4th and 5th values = (14 + 15) / 2 = **14.5**
- **Mode** = **12** (occurs 3 times, more than any other value)

**Worked Example 2:** Find the median of: 7, 3, 11, 5, 9
- Sorted: 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 (n = 5, odd)
- Median = (5+1)/2 = 3rd value = **7**

**When to Use Which:**
- **Mean:** Best for data without extreme outliers
- **Median:** Best when data has extreme values (e.g., income data)
- **Mode:** Best for categorical/frequency data (e.g., most common shoe size)

### 10.2 Probability — Introduction

**Probability:** A measure of the likelihood of an event happening. It is always between 0 and 1.

**Formula:**
- **P(Event) = Number of favorable outcomes / Total number of possible outcomes**

**Key Terms:**
- **Experiment:** An action with uncertain outcomes (e.g., tossing a coin, rolling a die)
- **Outcome:** A possible result of an experiment
- **Event:** A set of outcomes of interest
- **Equally likely outcomes:** Outcomes that have the same chance of occurring

**Probability Scale:**
- **0** — Impossible event (will never happen)
- **1** — Certain event (will always happen)
- **0.5** — 50-50 chance

**Worked Example 3:** What is the probability of getting a head when a fair coin is tossed?
- Favorable outcomes: 1 (Head)
- Total outcomes: 2 (Head, Tail)
- P(Head) = 1/2 = **0.5 (50%)**

**Worked Example 4:** A bag contains 3 red balls, 5 blue balls, and 2 green balls. A ball is drawn at random. Find the probability of getting:
- (a) A red ball: P(Red) = 3/(3+5+2) = 3/10 = **0.3**
- (b) A blue ball: P(Blue) = 5/10 = **0.5**
- (c) A green ball: P(Green) = 2/10 = **0.2**

**Worked Example 5:** A die is rolled once. Find the probability of getting:
- (a) An even number: Favorable = {2, 4, 6} = 3 outcomes; Total = 6
  - P(Even) = 3/6 = **1/2**
- (b) A number greater than 4: Favorable = {5, 6} = 2 outcomes
  - P(>4) = 2/6 = **1/3**

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ Not arranging data before finding the median
- ❌ Using the wrong formula for even vs odd number of observations
- ❌ Not counting total outcomes correctly in probability

### Teaching Tips
- Use classroom data (heights, marks, etc.) to practice mean/median/mode
- Conduct actual coin toss and die roll experiments (collect real data!)
- Use spinner games for probability exploration

## 11. Perimeter and Area

### 11.1 Recap — Rectangle and Square

| Shape | Perimeter | Area |
|-------|-----------|------|
| Rectangle | 2(l + b) | l × b |
| Square | 4s | s² |

### 11.2 Area of a Parallelogram

**Parallelogram:** A quadrilateral with opposite sides parallel and equal.

**Area = base × height (altitude)**

**Note:** The height must be perpendicular to the base. It is NOT the slant side.

**Worked Example 1:** A parallelogram has base 12 cm and height 5 cm. Find its area.
- Area = 12 × 5 = **60 cm²**

### 11.3 Area of a Triangle

**Area = ½ × base × height**

**Worked Example 2:** Find the area of a triangle with base 10 cm and height 6 cm.
- Area = ½ × 10 × 6 = **30 cm²**

**Relation to Parallelogram:** A triangle is half of a parallelogram with the same base and height. That's why the formula has ½.

**Worked Example 3:** The area of a triangle is 48 cm² and its base is 12 cm. Find the height.
- 48 = ½ × 12 × h
- 48 = 6h
- h = **8 cm**

### 11.4 Circumference and Area of a Circle

**Circumference (Perimeter of a circle):**
- C = 2πr = πd (where r = radius, d = diameter, π ≈ 22/7 or 3.14)

**Area of a circle:**
- A = πr²

**Worked Example 4:** Find the circumference of a circle with radius 7 cm. (Take π = 22/7)
- C = 2 × (22/7) × 7 = 2 × 22 = **44 cm**

**Worked Example 5:** Find the area of a circle with radius 14 cm. (Take π = 22/7)
- A = (22/7) × 14 × 14 = 22 × 2 × 14 = **616 cm²**

### 11.5 Conversion of Units

| Unit | Conversion |
|------|-----------|
| 1 m² | 10,000 cm² |
| 1 km² | 1,000,000 m² (10⁶ m²) |
| 1 hectare | 10,000 m² |
| 1 acre | 4,047 m² (approximately) |

**Worked Example 6:** A rectangular field measures 500 m by 300 m. Find its area in hectares.
- Area = 500 × 300 = 150,000 m²
- In hectares: 150,000 ÷ 10,000 = **15 hectares**

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ Using the slant side instead of perpendicular height for parallelogram/triangle
- ❌ Forgetting the ½ in the triangle area formula
- ❌ Not squaring the radius in the circle area formula
- ❌ Mixing up 2πr (circumference) with πr² (area)

### Teaching Tips
- Cut a parallelogram and rearrange it into a rectangle to derive the area formula
- Fold a parallelogram to show it contains two triangles
- Measure circular objects (plates, lids) to find circumference and compare with πd

## 12. Visualising Solid Shapes

### 12.1 Introduction to 3D Shapes (Solids)

**Plane Figures (2D):** Shapes with length and breadth only (circle, triangle, rectangle, etc.)

**Solid Figures (3D):** Shapes with length, breadth, and height/depth.

| Solid | Faces | Edges | Vertices |
|-------|-------|-------|----------|
| **Cube** | 6 (all squares) | 12 | 8 |
| **Cuboid** | 6 (rectangles, may include 2 squares) | 12 | 8 |
| **Cylinder** | 3 (2 circular, 1 curved) | 2 | 0 |
| **Cone** | 2 (1 circular, 1 curved) | 1 | 1 |
| **Sphere** | 1 (curved surface) | 0 | 0 |
| **Triangular Prism** | 5 (2 triangles, 3 rectangles) | 9 | 6 |

**Euler's Formula:** For any convex polyhedron:
- **F + V − E = 2**
- Where F = Faces, V = Vertices, E = Edges

**Verify for a cube:** 6 + 8 − 12 = 2 ✅
**Verify for a triangular prism:** 5 + 6 − 9 = 2 ✅

### 12.2 Nets of 3D Shapes

**Net:** A 2D shape that can be folded to form a 3D solid.

**Examples:**
- **Cube net:** 6 squares arranged in a cross or T pattern (11 distinct nets exist)
- **Cuboid net:** 6 rectangles arranged appropriately
- **Cylinder net:** A rectangle with two circles on opposite sides
- **Cone net:** A sector of a circle + a circle

**Worked Example 1:** Which of these is a valid net for a cube?
- Four squares in a row with one square above the second and one below the third → **Yes, this folds into a cube**
- Three squares in a row → **No, only 3 faces — needs 6**

### 12.3 Views of 3D Shapes

**Front View:** What you see when looking from the front.
**Side View:** What you see when looking from the side.
**Top View:** What you see when looking from above.

**Worked Example 2:** A cuboid has dimensions 3 cm × 4 cm × 5 cm.
- Front view (3 × 4 face): Rectangle 3 cm × 4 cm
- Side view (4 × 5 face): Rectangle 4 cm × 5 cm
- Top view (3 × 5 face): Rectangle 3 cm × 5 cm

### 12.4 Drawing 3D Shapes on Paper (Oblique Sketches and Isometric Sketches)

**Oblique Sketch:** A 3D-looking drawing where one face is drawn as its actual shape and depth is shown at an angle.

**Isometric Sketch:** Uses a special grid (isometric dot paper) where all three dimensions are shown with equal foreshortening.

### 12.5 Cross-Sections

**Cross-Section:** The shape obtained when a solid is cut by a plane.

**Examples:**
- Cross-section of a cylinder (cut horizontally): **Circle**
- Cross-section of a cylinder (cut vertically): **Rectangle**
- Cross-section of a cone (cut horizontally): **Circle**
- Cross-section of a cone (cut vertically through the vertex): **Triangle**
- Cross-section of a cube (cut diagonally through a face): **Rectangle or Triangle (depending on the cut)**

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ Confusing edges with faces
- ❌ Miscounting F, V, or E for solids (especially cylinder and cone)
- ❌ Not being able to visualize which net folds into which solid

### Teaching Tips
- Build 3D shapes from clay, play-dough, or straws/joints
- Cut out nets from paper and fold them to make solids
- Shine a torch on objects to observe shadows (which relate to views)
- Use everyday objects (dice = cube, can = cylinder, ice cream cone = cone)
