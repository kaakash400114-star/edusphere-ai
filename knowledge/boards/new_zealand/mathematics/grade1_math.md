# New Zealand Curriculum (NZC) Grade 1 Mathematics


## Number and Algebra: Counting and Ordering
Students count forwards to at least 100 and backwards from 20. They read, write, and order numbers to 50. Students group objects in twos, fives, and tens to support counting. They find the number that is one before or after a given number. Students solve simple addition and subtraction problems using materials like pebbles or kūmara (sweet potatoes). For example, finding how many piupiu (traditional flax skirts) are left after two are given away from a group of five.

## Number and Algebra: Patterns and Relationships
Students recognise, create, and continue spatial, object, and sound patterns involving at least two elements, such as 'circle, square, circle, square' or a rhythmic clap-snap sequence. They identify and describe the core repeating unit and predict subsequent elements using positional language like 'next', 'after', and 'before'. Sorting and classifying activities involve grouping objects based on one or two attributes, for instance, arranging kōura from smallest to largest or sorting leaves by colour and shape. Students articulate the rule governing a pattern using comparative terms like 'bigger', 'smaller', 'lighter', or 'darker' to describe relationships between elements. This builds foundational understanding for identifying number properties and relationships.

## Geometry and Measurement: Length and Volume
Students use direct comparison to determine the length of objects by aligning them from a baseline point and identifying which is longer, shorter, or taller. To measure volume and capacity, they fill one container and pour its contents into another to see which holds more or less. Lengths are then ordered, such as from shortest to longest, by physically arranging a set of objects. Similarly, containers can be ordered by volume after comparative filling activities, for instance, comparing the capacity of different-sized pōhā (traditional bags). These practical comparisons are made using everyday items like raukura (feathered ornaments) or kūmara to solidify understanding of measurement vocabulary.

## Geometry and Measurement: Time and Shapes
Students name and describe common 2D shapes like circles, triangles, and squares. They identify 3D objects like cubes and cylinders. Students understand the concept of time, using language like 'morning', 'afternoon', and 'yesterday'. They sequence events in their day, such as eating breakfast before going to kura (school). For example, naming shapes they see on a tukutuku (wall panel) or talking about the All Blacks game being in the evening.

## Statistics: Sorting and Graphing
In statistics, students learn to collect data about familiar topics such as favourite colours, pets, or school lunches, and then sort this information into clear categories. They represent the data using pictographs, where each picture symbol stands for one response, or tally marks, which are grouped in fives for efficient counting. Students construct these graphs by arranging the symbols or marks to visually display the number of items in each category. The key concept of interpretation is developed by analysing the graphs to identify and compare quantities, answering questions like "Which category has the most?" or "How many more are in one group than another?". For example, after sorting toy dinosaurs into types, children create a pictograph to determine which dinosaur is the most popular in the class.

## Problem Solving: Using Mathematics
Students apply counting and basic addition and subtraction to solve real-world problems, following the principle of making sense of a situation before acting. A key method is to represent problems using physical materials, drawings, or acting out scenarios to model the mathematical thinking required. They learn to communicate their chosen strategies and solutions, exploring multiple approaches such as grouping or partitioning to reach an answer. For example, students might calculate the total number of cupcakes by counting in twos, determining how many can be shared evenly among three friends, or finding how many more are needed for a class of five children. These experiences build foundational understanding of operations as practical tools for finding solutions.
