# New Zealand Curriculum (NZC) Level 6 Grade 11 Mathematics  

## Algebraic Expressions and Equations  
Students manipulate and solve complex algebraic expressions. They expand and factorise quadratic expressions, including the difference of squares. Methods for solving quadratic equations involve factorisation, completing the square, and using the quadratic formula ($x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$). Equations may include rational expressions, which require finding a common denominator. For example, solving $\frac{2}{x} + \frac{3}{x-1} = 5$ involves multiplying through by $x(x-1)$ to clear fractions. Students also solve linear inequalities and represent solutions on number lines.  

## Functions and Graphs  
Students analyse and graph linear, quadratic, and cubic functions. They determine key features such as domain, range, intercepts, and turning points using both algebraic and graphical methods. For quadratic functions in vertex form ($y = a(x-h)^2 + k$), students identify the vertex and axis of symmetry. They also solve simultaneous equations graphically and algebraically. Real-world contexts, like calculating the height of a piupiu fabric hanging under gravity, are used to model quadratic relationships. Transformations of functions, including reflections and translations, are explored using function notation.  

## Trigonometry  
Students extend their understanding of trigonometric ratios to solve problems involving triangles and real-world applications. They use the sine rule ($\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}$) and cosine rule ($c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C$) to find unknown sides and angles in non-right-angled triangles. Trigonometric identities, such as $\sin^2 \theta + \cos^2 \theta = 1$, are used to simplify expressions. Students apply these concepts to NZ contexts, like determining the angle of elevation when viewing a taniwha from a waka. The unit circle and radian measure are introduced for advanced problem-solving.  

## Calculus: Differentiation  
Students are introduced to calculus by learning differentiation to find the gradient of curves and rates of change. They differentiate polynomial functions using the power rule ($\frac{d}{dx}(x^n) = nx^{n-1}$) and apply it to position, velocity, and acceleration problems. The derivative of exponential functions, $\frac{d}{dx}(e^{kx}) = ke^{kx}$, is also covered. Students find the equation of a tangent line to a curve at a given point, which is useful in modelling scenarios like the speed of an All Blacks player during a haka. Applications to optimisation problems, such as maximising the area of a marae garden, are practised.  

## Calculus: Integration  
Students learn integration as the reverse of differentiation, used to find areas under curves and solve accumulation problems. They integrate polynomial functions using the reverse power rule ($\int x^n dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$) and definite integrals to calculate areas between curves and the x-axis. The integral of exponential functions, $\int e^{kx} dx = \frac{1}{k}e^{kx} + C$, is explored. Real-world examples include calculating the distance travelled by a kiwi bird given its velocity function. Students also apply integration to solve differential equations involving rates of change in biological or physical contexts.  

## Probability and Statistics  
Students analyse multi-stage events and calculate probabilities using tree diagrams and Venn diagrams. They calculate conditional probabilities and apply the law of total probability. In statistics, they compare box plots and calculate measures of spread, such as standard deviation. Students use bivariate data to analyse relationships using correlation coefficients and linear regression. For example, they might investigate the correlation between rainfall (mm) and kumara yield in Te Puke. NZ contexts are prioritised, like analysing the performance of the Silver Ferns in netball leagues using statistical software.
