# New Zealand Curriculum (NZC) Grade 10 Mathematics  

## Number and Algebra
Students extend their skills in manipulating algebraic expressions and solving equations. They learn to expand and factorise quadratic expressions, such as (x + 3)(x - 4) = x² - x - 12. Solving linear and quadratic equations involves methods like factorisation, completing the square, and using the quadratic formula. Financial mathematics includes calculating compound interest for savings, like an All Blacks jersey investment growing at 5% annually. They also work with proportional reasoning, scaling recipes for a hāngi or calculating costs for piupiu materials.

## Geometry and Measurement
Students explore the properties of 2D and 3D shapes, including calculating volumes and surface areas of prisms and cylinders. For example, finding the volume of a cylindrical water tank for a marae involves V = πr²h. They apply the Pythagorean theorem and trigonometric ratios (sine, cosine, tangent) to solve problems, like determining the height of a pou when given the angle of elevation. Scale drawings are used for map reading, such as planning a walkway to a kiwi sanctuary, while conversion between metric units ensures accuracy in building projects.

## Statistics
Students learn to collect, analyse, and interpret bivariate data. They construct scatter plots and use trend lines to identify relationships, such as correlating rainfall with crop yields in a local farm. Measures of central tendency (mean, median, mode) and spread (range, interquartile range) are applied to datasets, like analysing scores from a regional kapa haka competition. They evaluate statistical reports critically, considering sampling methods and bias, to make informed decisions, like assessing the popularity of a new All Blacks jersey design.

## Probability
Students calculate probabilities using fractions, decimals, and percentages for simple and compound events. They explore independent and dependent events, such as drawing multiple kōura (crayfish) from a pond without replacement. Tree diagrams and two-way tables are used to represent and solve probability problems, like the chances of winning a raffle for concert tickets. Simulations, like modelling the likelihood of rain during a sports day, help them understand real-world uncertainty and make predictions.

## Patterns and Relationships
Students recognise and describe linear, quadratic, and exponential patterns. They generate sequences and find rules, such as the number of seats in increasing rows at a stadium. Graphing linear and quadratic equations helps analyse relationships, like the trajectory of a ball during a game of ki o rahi. Functions are explored through real-world contexts, such as calculating the cost of hiring a bus for a school trip using a linear function.

## Mathematical Reasoning
Students apply deductive reasoning to establish geometric proofs, such as demonstrating the sum of angles in a triangle, and use inductive reasoning to formulate conjectures from number patterns, like identifying arithmetic sequences. They learn to construct logical arguments to verify mathematical statements, employing methods such as direct proof and counterexamples. For instance, they prove whether a given budget for a community event is feasible by calculating total costs and comparing them to available funds. Estimation and reasonableness checking are critical, where students assess if the perimeter of a wharenui design aligns with material constraints. Finally, they communicate their reasoning precisely, articulating the steps taken to solve problems involving angle measurements in cultural formations.
