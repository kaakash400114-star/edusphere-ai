# New Zealand Curriculum (NZC) Grade 7 English

## Language Features
Students explore figurative language, including similes, metaphors, and personification. They learn to identify how writers use these features to create imagery and evoke emotion. For example, a simile like "as brave as a All Blacks forward" or a metaphor comparing a steep mountain path to a "pūrerehua" (spinning top) helps to create vivid pictures in the reader's mind. Students analyse texts to uncover these layers of meaning, appreciating how such language enhances storytelling and descriptive power in both written and oral contexts.

## Structure and Organisation
Students learn to structure texts for a range of purposes. They focus on writing well-formed paragraphs that begin with a topic sentence, followed by supporting sentences and a concluding sentence. For instance, when writing a report on the kiwi, they will organise their work into paragraphs describing its habitat, appearance, and conservation status. They also understand how to sequence ideas logically to build a coherent argument, ensuring their writing is clear, purposeful, and easy for an audience to follow.

## Purpose and Audience
Students analyse how different text types are crafted for specific audiences and purposes. They learn that an email to a friend uses informal language and a personal tone, while a formal letter to a community marae requires respectful and structured language. For example, a persuasive piece to protect a local ngahere (forest) will use facts and emotive language to appeal to the community's sense of kaitiakitanga (guardianship), ensuring the communication is effective for its intended listeners or readers.

## Vocabulary and Grammar
Students expand their vocabulary, focusing on precise and descriptive language. They learn to use domain-specific terms, such as 'kaitiakitanga' when discussing environmental issues or 'waiata' when analysing songs. Grammatically, they consolidate their understanding of complex sentences, including those with subordinate clauses, to express more detailed ideas. They also explore how verb tenses can be used to shift perspectives in narratives, such as switching from past to present to create a sense of immediacy during a haka performance.

## Close Reading
Students develop skills in close reading to analyse how language features contribute to the meaning of texts. They examine specific words, phrases, and sentences in detail, explaining their effect. For example, while reading a story about a Māori legend, they might discuss the use of onomatopoeia like 'whir-whir' to describe the sound of a piupiu (traditional flax skirt) and how this contributes to the story's atmosphere. This critical analysis helps them understand the craft of writing and its impact on the reader.

## Creating Texts
Students create a variety of texts for different purposes. They might write a poem that uses imagery from the natural environment, draft a persuasive speech on a local issue, or compose a feature article about the All Blacks for a school newsletter. They apply their knowledge of structure, language features, and audience to craft engaging and effective pieces. They also learn to provide constructive feedback to peers, focusing on strengths and areas for improvement to enhance their writing.
