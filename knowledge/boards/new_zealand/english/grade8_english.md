# New Zealand Curriculum (NZC) Grade 8 English  
## Understanding Character Development in Narrative  
Students analyse how authors reveal character traits through dialogue, actions, and motivations. They learn to identify explicit (e.g., "She slammed the door") and implicit (e.g., "He avoided eye contact") evidence. For example, in a story about a young rugby player, their dedication might be shown by pre-dawn training sessions. Students compare and contrast characters, exploring how traits like resilience (e.g., recovering from an injury to play in the final) drive the plot. They use text-based evidence to support their interpretations, linking character choices to broader themes like identity or belonging.  

## Writing Persuasive Texts for Authentic Audiences  
Students craft persuasive arguments using techniques such as rhetorical questions, emotive language, and statistics. They structure texts with a clear introduction stating their position, body paragraphs with evidence and explanations, and a compelling conclusion. For instance, a student might write a letter to the school board advocating for a Māori culture week, using examples like the haka to build cultural pride. They learn to anticipate counterarguments and address them respectfully, ensuring their writing is both convincing and culturally aware, tailored to influence a specific audience.  

## Analysing Unfamiliar Texts  
Students learn strategies to decode and analyse unseen texts, including advertisements, news articles, and speeches. They identify the writer’s purpose (to inform, persuade, or entertain) and target audience. Students examine techniques like alliteration, metaphors, and repetition, such as the rhythmic language of a whakataukī (proverb). They make inferences about the writer’s bias and evaluate how cultural context influences meaning. For example, an advertisement featuring a piupiu might connect the product to Māori heritage and authenticity.  

## Researching and Presenting Information  
Students conduct short research projects on New Zealand-related topics, such as the conservation of kiwi or the history of the All Blacks. They gather information from reliable sources, distinguishing between facts and opinions, and cite them correctly. Students organise findings into structured reports or presentations, using visuals like graphs or maps to support their points. They practise summarising key ideas and presenting their work clearly to peers, acknowledging sources to maintain academic integrity and respect for intellectual property.  

## Understanding Structural and Language Features of Poems  
Students explore how poets use structure (e.g., stanzas, line breaks) and language (e.g., imagery, rhyme) to create meaning and evoke emotion. They analyse poems about New Zealand themes, such as the natural environment (e.g., "the roar of the Rangitīkei") or cultural identity (e.g., references to whānau). Students identify poetic devices like personification (e.g., "the wind whispers secrets") and discuss how they contribute to the tone. They compare different poetic forms, such as haiku and narrative poetry, to appreciate their unique effects.  

## Participating in Collaborative Discussions  
Students develop oral communication skills through formal and informal discussions on texts and issues. They learn to listen actively, build on others’ ideas, and articulate their own viewpoints respectfully. Topics might include the significance of the haka in New Zealand culture or the importance of environmental conservation. Students practise using appropriate language for different contexts, such as debating a motion about school uniforms or sharing personal reflections after a shared reading. They are encouraged to use te reo Māori where appropriate to enrich discussions.
