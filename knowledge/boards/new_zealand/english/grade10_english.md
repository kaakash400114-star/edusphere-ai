# New Zealand Curriculum (NZC) Grade 10 English


## Visual and Oral Language
Students will deconstruct the semantic and persuasive functions of visual and oral language by applying analytical frameworks. In visual texts, they identify intentional devices such as symbolism, colour palettes, and camera angles, for instance, how a tourism ad uses wide-angle shots to convey the vastness of a landscape. For oral language, they will evaluate rhetorical strategies, including tone, diction, and the use of kōrero pōrī to establish authority. Students assess how texts reflect New Zealand contexts by examining the use of te reo Māori in public discourse and how these cultural elements influence audience perception. This analysis requires the development of skills to identify, analyse, and evaluate the deliberate construction of meaning across diverse text forms.

## Written Language: Reading and Viewing
Students critically engage with a range of written texts, including novels, short stories, and media articles, focusing on authorial intent and thematic development. They identify and analyse how authors use structure, symbolism, and language to explore ideas. For example, students might analyse how a story about a young kiwi navigating challenges in a native forest uses the setting to reflect themes of identity and belonging, linking to concepts like whanaungatanga (relationships).

## Written Language: Writing
Students learn to construct formal texts by applying the PEEEL paragraph structure: Point, Evidence, Explanation, Example, Link, such as in persuasive essays arguing for conservation efforts. They also master creative techniques including symbolism, like using the pohutukawa tree to represent resilience, and effective dialogue to reveal character, as in narratives about a family preparing for a pōwhiri. Across all writing, students adhere to the principles of coherence, ensuring logical flow between ideas, and purpose, tailoring their register to suit specific audiences. Key concepts include understanding audience appeal and purpose to guide tone and style. They apply grammatical rules for sentence structure and punctuation to ensure clarity and correctness in both argumentative and creative pieces.

## Language Features and Conventions
Students analyse and apply a range of grammatical structures, including subordinate clauses and parallelism, to build complex sentences that enhance clarity and stylistic effect. They master the conventions of complex punctuation, such as using semicolons to link closely related independent clauses and em-dashes to set off parenthetical information. Vocabulary development focuses on precise word choice and the strategic integration of te reo Māori terms to add cultural depth, for example, using 'kaitiakitanga' in a discussion on environmental responsibility. Students learn to deconstruct how specific grammatical choices create meaning and effect in texts, applying these principles to their own writing. This formal knowledge is used to ensure their communication is accurate, clear, and culturally resonant.

## Understanding Connections
Students apply comparative analysis to identify interconnections between texts, audiences, and contexts, evaluating how form and style influence meaning. They examine how specific literary devices, such as symbolism in a Māori creation story and metaphor in a contemporary poem about urban life, construct thematic explorations of identity and belonging. Students analyse how texts are interpreted through cultural perspectives, assessing how different viewpoints, including those of tangata whenua, inform a reading of a narrative on historical events. By synthesising evidence from multiple sources, such as a news article on a social issue and a speech by a community leader, students articulate the complex relationship between texts and the world.

## Critical Thinking in Literature
Students learn to apply critical thinking by analysing literary texts through specific lenses, including the formalist approach which focuses on how elements like symbolism, irony, and narrative structure create meaning. They will evaluate character motivation and thematic depth by examining how an author’s choices influence reader interpretation and impact. Students will also identify and question underlying societal values by comparing and contrasting a text’s message against contemporary norms. For instance, in studying a dystopian novel like *The Giver*, students will analyse the societal trade-offs of individual freedom versus collective order by examining the author’s use of character development and plot progression to present the central conflict.
