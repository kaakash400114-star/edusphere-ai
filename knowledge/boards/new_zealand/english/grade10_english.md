# New Zealand Curriculum (NZC) Grade 10 English

## Visual and Oral Language
Students analyse how visual and oral texts, including advertisements and speeches, construct meaning. They examine the use of symbolism, colour, and camera angles in visuals, and rhetoric, tone, and kōrero pōrī (proverbs) in spoken language. Students evaluate how texts reflect New Zealand contexts, such as a tourism ad promoting the Rotorua geothermal parks or a haka performed by the All Blacks, and how they influence audiences.

## Written Language: Reading and Viewing
Students critically engage with a range of written texts, including novels, short stories, and media articles, focusing on authorial intent and thematic development. They identify and analyse how authors use structure, symbolism, and language to explore ideas. For example, students might analyse how a story about a young kiwi navigating challenges in a native forest uses the setting to reflect themes of identity and belonging, linking to concepts like whanaungatanga (relationships).

## Written Language: Writing
Students develop skills in crafting formal and creative written texts. In formal writing, they construct structured arguments, such as a persuasive essay on the importance of protecting the endangered kākāpō. In creative writing, they produce narratives using descriptive language, symbolism, and dialogue, perhaps crafting a story about a family preparing for a pōwhiri (welcome ceremony), ensuring their writing is coherent, purposeful, and audience-appropriate.

## Language Features and Conventions
Students deepen their understanding of grammar, punctuation, and vocabulary for effective communication. They learn to use a range of sentence structures for stylistic effect and correctly apply complex punctuation, such as semicolons and dashes. Vocabulary development includes integrating te reo Māori terms naturally, like using 'kaitiakitanga' (guardianship) in an essay on environmental conservation, ensuring their language is precise and culturally resonant.

## Understanding Connections
Students analyse the interconnections between texts, audiences, and contexts. They compare how different texts, such as a traditional Māori legend and a modern New Zealand novel, explore similar themes like courage or resistance. Students also consider how cultural perspectives, including those of tangata whenua (Māori people), shape the interpretation of texts, examining how a poem about the Treaty of Waitangi reflects different viewpoints on its significance.

## Critical Thinking in Literature
Students critically evaluate literary texts to identify underlying messages and values. They analyse character motivation, plot development, and thematic depth, questioning how texts reflect or challenge societal norms. For example, students might debate whether a story about a young rugby player aspiring to join the All Blacks promotes or subverts traditional ideals of masculinity, using evidence from the text to support their analysis.
