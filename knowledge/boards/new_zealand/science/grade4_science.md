# New Zealand Curriculum (NZC) Grade 4 Science

## Physical World: Properties of Materials
Materials have properties like strength, flexibility, and transparency that make them useful for different jobs. In Aotearoa, we use strong wood like kauri for buildings and flexible harakeke for weaving. Scientists test these properties by pulling, bending, or shining light on materials. For example, piupiu are made from flax and are flexible yet strong. The properties of materials determine if they are good for making a rugby ball or a pōhā (traditional food container). Understanding these properties helps us choose the right material for the job.

## Living World: Animal Adaptations
Animals have special features, called adaptations, that help them survive in their environment. The kiwi is a perfect example; its long beak helps it find worms in the soil, and its strong legs help it dig. The All Blacks use teamwork, which is a behavioural adaptation for winning rugby games. Other animals have adaptations like a kakapo’s wings for camouflage or a penguin’s feathers for swimming in cold water. These features are passed down from parents to help the next generation survive in Aotearoa’s unique habitats.

## Earth and Space: Weather Patterns
Weather is the condition of the air outside, like temperature, wind, and rain. In Aotearoa, the weather can change quickly because of the surrounding moana (ocean). We measure temperature with a thermometer in Celsius. A haka is often performed on a cool day, but people can still get warm from the energy of the performance. Wind can be gentle or strong, like the westerlies that bring rain to the West Coast. Tracking these patterns over days helps us understand the climate of our local area.

## Planet Earth: Rocks and Soils
Rocks and soils are made from different materials and can be found all over Aotearoa. Igneous rocks, like basalt, are formed from cooled lava. Pounamu is a special type of hard igneous rock used for tools. Soil is made from broken-down rock and rotting plants. It has different layers, and the topsoil is where plants grow their roots. Healthy soil is full of nutrients, just like the soil in a garden that helps kūmara grow. We can use magnifying glasses to see the tiny bits of rock and living things in soil.

## The Nature of Science: Investigating Like a Scientist
Scientists ask questions about the world and find answers through investigations. To investigate like a scientist, we make a guess (a hypothesis), then test it with an experiment. For example, we might ask, "Which material makes the best umbrella?" We would test piupiu, plastic, and paper in the rain. We record our findings by counting how much water gets through each material. We share our ideas, just like the All Blacks share strategies to win a game. This helps everyone learn.

## Science in Society: Technology from Nature
Humans use technology to solve problems, and many ideas come from nature. The design of the waka ama (outrigger canoe) was inspired by how fish use their fins to stay steady. Māori weaving, or raranga, uses flax (harakeke) to create baskets and clothing. These are examples of biomimicry, where we copy nature’s smart designs. Modern technology, like a rugby ball with a good grip, also follows this idea. By looking at the natural world, we can create clever solutions for everyday life in Aotearoa.
