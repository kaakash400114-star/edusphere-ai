# New Zealand Curriculum (NZC) Grade 9 Science  

## Matter and Its Properties
Students explore the classification of matter into elements, compounds, and mixtures. They learn about atoms and molecules using models, and distinguish between physical and chemical changes. For example, rusting of iron is a chemical change, while freezing water is a physical one. Practical work involves testing conductivity and solubility, using everyday materials like salt and copper wire. Concepts are linked to Māori perspectives, such as the understanding of *atua* (deities) associated with natural elements like Tāne (forest) and Tangaroa (sea).

## Energy Transformations
Energy is studied through kinetic and potential forms, with equations like KE = ½mv². Students investigate energy transfers in systems, such as a pendulum swinging or a car braking. Examples include calculating the speed of a rugby ball or the gravitational potential energy of a All Blacks player jumping for a lineout. The principle of conservation of energy is emphasised, alongside real-world applications like hydroelectric dams (e.g., Manapouri) and renewable energy sources. Te reo terms like *kaha* (strength) are used to describe energy levels in traditional contexts.

## Forces and Motion
Newton’s laws of motion are foundational, with students analysing forces acting on objects. They calculate acceleration (a = F/m) and explore friction, gravity, and air resistance. Examples include a piupiu skirt flapping in the wind or the trajectory of a rugby ball during a Haka performance. Experiments might involve measuring the force needed to push a trolley or terminal velocity of a falling object. Connections are made to Māori navigation (*wayfinding*) and the understanding of wind (*hau*) as a force influencing voyages.

## Ecosystems and Interactions
Students will analyse biotic factors like pūriri trees and kākāpō alongside abiotic factors such as soil pH and rainfall in specific New Zealand ecosystems. They will construct and interpret food webs, identifying producers, primary, secondary, and tertiary consumers, and decomposers. Key ecological concepts are modelled through limiting factors and carrying capacity. Human impacts like deforestation in the Waitakere Ranges are examined for their effects on biodiversity, alongside conservation initiatives such as predator-free island projects. The principle of *kaitiakitanga* underpins all discussions of environmental management and species protection.

## Earth’s Processes
Geological features are examined through plate tectonics, focusing on the Pacific and Australian plates. Students learn about earthquakes, volcanoes (e.g., Ruapehu), and erosion. Rock classification (igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic) and the rock cycle are taught, with examples of geothermal activity in Rotorua. Māori perspectives include the legend of Māui fishing up the North Island and the role of *Tāwhirimātea* (god of winds) in shaping the land. Practical tasks include identifying rock samples and interpreting geological maps.

## Chemical Reactions and Acids/Bases
Students explore reaction types (synthesis, decomposition) and factors affecting rates, like temperature and catalysts. Acid-base chemistry is studied using pH indicators, with examples of volcanic lakes (e.g., Lake Taupō) or lemon juice. Safety protocols are emphasised when handling substances like sodium hydroxide. Real-world applications include neutralisation in soil treatment and the role of acids in digestion. Te reo terms like *whakaatu* (representation) are used to describe chemical models, linking to Māori symbolic representations in nature.
