# New Zealand Curriculum (NZC) Grade 3 Science

## Living World: Ngā Tamariki o Te Taiao
Students explore the characteristics of living things, focusing on native species like the kiwi and tuatara. They learn that living things need air (hirua), water (wai), food (kai), and a safe place (kaitiakitanga). Through observation, students group plants and animals based on shared features, such as having fur, feathers, or leaves. They discuss how Māori knowledge (mātauranga Māori) views humans as part of the natural world, responsible for caring for the environment (kaitiakitanga). This builds an understanding of life cycles and the interdependence of all living things in their habitat.

## Planet Earth and Beyond: Te Whenua me Rangi
Students investigate daily and seasonal weather patterns in Aotearoa. They learn to describe the weather using te reo Māori terms like *ahua* (weather), *uhi* (rain), and *ra* (sun). Using simple thermometers, they measure and record temperature changes, observing how the Maramataka (Māori lunar calendar) guides planting and harvesting. They explore the water cycle, focusing on how rain falls in the Waitākere Ranges and flows into rivers like the Waikato, connecting to the concept of *kaitiakitanga* (guardianship) of freshwater resources.

## Material World: Ngā Hua
Students identify and classify common materials (metals, plastics, wood, glass) based on their properties like hardness and flexibility. They conduct experiments to see which materials float or sink, linking to NZC Level 2. Using natural materials like harakeke (flax) from a local *pā harakeke* (flax garden), they learn how Māori traditionally use plants for weaving (*raranga*) and medicine (*rongoā*). Students observe that some materials, like plastics, can last for a long time in *whenua* (land), raising questions about sustainable choices.

## Physical World: Te Ao Hurihuri
Students explore movement and forces through simple investigations. They push, pull, and roll objects to understand that forces cause changes in motion. They observe how a rugby ball’s shape affects its distance during a kick, linking to the All Blacks’ training. Using ramps and toy cars, they test how steepness affects speed. They learn about gravity (*tangaroa*) as the force pulling things down, connecting to falling leaves from a *pōhutukawa* tree, a symbol of summer in New Zealand.

## Science Skills: Ngā Pūkenga
Students develop practical skills like observing, measuring, and recording data. They use simple tools like magnifying glasses to examine insects in a school garden, noting features like wings or antennae. They draw detailed pictures (*whakaaturanga*) of their findings and share them with peers through oral presentations (*kōrero*). They learn to follow instructions (*whakarāpopotanga*) for simple experiments, like making a volcano erupt using baking soda and vinegar, emphasizing safety (*āwhina*) in the classroom.

## Context: Science in Our Community
Students explore how science is used in their local community. They might visit a marae (*whare whakairo*) to learn about traditional Māori engineering in carving (*whakairo*) or architecture (*whare tūpuna*). They investigate how farmers use science to care for *kune* (geese) or grow crops like kūmara. By interviewing a local *tohunga* (expert) or visiting a museum (*whare taonga*), students see how science and culture (*tikanga*) are intertwined, fostering a sense of pride in New Zealand’s unique innovations and traditions.
