# International Baccalaureate (IB) Grade 12 Mathematics


## Further Calculus and Differential Equations
Students master advanced integration techniques such as integration by parts for products of functions, trigonometric substitution for integrals involving √(a²-x²), and partial fractions for rational functions. These methods are then applied to calculate precise areas and volumes of revolution and to determine the centre of mass of a lamina. The study of differential equations deepens to include analytical solutions for first-order linear and homogeneous second-order linear differential equations. Students utilize these solution methods to model real-world phenomena, such as Newton's Law of Cooling and the motion of a damped harmonic oscillator, with a focus on global applications in epidemiological models for disease spread.

## Discrete Mathematics
Students learn to apply graph algorithms, such as Dijkstra's for shortest paths and Kruskal's for minimum spanning trees, to model real-world scenarios like optimizing global supply chain logistics. They investigate fundamental properties of graphs, including Eulerian and Hamiltonian paths, and theorems for identifying planar graphs. In combinatorics, students master the principles of permutations and combinations, using the Binomial Theorem to expand expressions and calculate probabilities. These concepts are applied to data coding theory, such as using permutations to create encryption keys for secure international communications and analysing network reliability. Formal proofs are employed to verify key theorems and understand the logical underpinnings of discrete structures.

## Mathematical Induction and Proof
Mathematical induction is a formal proof technique for establishing the validity of statements indexed by positive integers. It follows a two-step process: first, verifying the base case, typically P(1), and second, proving that if the statement holds for an arbitrary integer k, then it also holds for k + 1, the inductive step. This method is used to prove summation formulas, such as the sum of the first n positive integers, and divisibility rules, for instance, demonstrating that 3^n - 1 is divisible by 2 for all n ≥ 1. The curriculum also explores proof by contradiction, which involves assuming the negation of a statement to derive a logical inconsistency, and the strategic use of counterexamples to disprove conjectures, reinforcing rigorous, logical reasoning.

## Vectors and Matrices in Three Dimensions
In three-dimensional space, students perform vector operations such as the scalar product to find the angle between two vectors using **a · b = |a||b|cos θ** and the vector product to calculate the area of a parallelogram formed by two vectors with **|a × b|**. For linear systems, they analyse matrices by solving for eigenvalues, λ, from the characteristic equation **det(A - λI) = 0**, and compute corresponding eigenvectors, which define the principal axes of transformations. These concepts are applied to solve systems of linear differential equations and to project three-dimensional objects onto a two-dimensional plane for rendering in computer graphics, demonstrating their fundamental role in both theoretical and applied mathematics.

## Probability and Distributions
Students master conditional probability using the formula P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B), applying it to scenarios like disease testing. They learn Bayes' Theorem to update probabilities with new evidence, such as revising fraud detection odds after a transaction. The course covers discrete distributions like the Binomial, for modeling coin flips with P(X=k) = (n choose k) p^k (1-p)^(n-k), and the Poisson for rare events. For continuous data, they use the Normal distribution with its probability density function and z-scores, applying it to model standardized test scores or sensor error margins. These methods provide a robust analytical framework for quantifying uncertainty in scientific, financial, and industrial contexts.

## Mathematical Applications in a Global Context
Students will apply mathematical modeling to complex global challenges by constructing and analyzing functions to describe phenomena like compound interest in international finance or population growth rates. They will use sequences and series, including arithmetic and geometric progressions, to project carbon emission trends and depletion rates of natural resources. Learners will solve differential equations to model rates of change, such as the spread of infectious diseases, and employ statistical methods to analyze economic data across different nations. The development of sustainable development goals is examined through the optimization of resource allocation using linear programming. This involves interpreting the results of models, discussing their limitations, and making informed recommendations on global issues like climate change and equitable economic policy.
