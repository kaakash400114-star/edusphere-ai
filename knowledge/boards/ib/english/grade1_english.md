# International Baccalaureate (IB) Grade 1 English


## Narrative Structures
Learners investigate the basic components of a story. They learn that stories have a character, a setting, and a sequence of events. Students identify the beginning (what happens first), the middle (what happens next), and the end (what happens last). They can sequence picture cards to tell a simple story, such as a frog jumping from lily pad to lily pad in a pond. Learners use this structure to create and orally recount their own short narratives.

## Phonemic Awareness
Learners develop phonemic awareness by segmenting and blending individual sounds, or phonemes, within spoken words. They learn to identify the initial, medial, and final sounds in simple words, distinguishing between the /c/, /a/, and /t/ in 'cat'. Students practice orally blending phonemes to form whole words, connecting the sounds /p/, /i/, and /n/ to read 'pin'. Conversely, they segment words into their constituent phonemes, such as breaking 'sun' into /s/ /u/ /n/. This foundational skill is strengthened through explicit instruction and playful activities like identifying initial sounds in 'I Spy'.

## High-Frequency Sight Words
Grade 1 English Language Arts introduces high-frequency sight words as essential tools for fluent reading. These common words, such as 'the', 'is', 'was', 'to', and 'and', appear frequently in texts and often defy standard phonetic rules, requiring instant recognition. Students master these words through direct instruction and daily practice, developing a mental bank of sight vocabulary. They are taught to identify these words in isolation and then apply that knowledge within simple, decodable sentences. For instance, after learning 'the', 'cat', and 'is', a student can read "The cat is big." This systematic approach builds foundational reading fluency and allows learners to focus on decoding less common words.

## Listening and Responding
Active listening is a core skill taught through specific methods that require students to maintain eye contact with the speaker, minimize distractions, and internalize the information presented. They practice these skills by listening to a variety of texts, including read-aloud stories like "The Gruffalo," poems such as "Jabberwocky," and traditional songs from various cultures. Following the listening activity, students are guided to formulate responses using concrete examples from the text to answer direct, comprehension-based questions. This structured approach ensures they can identify key narrative elements, such as a main character or a significant event. The consistent application of these rules and methods is designed to build foundational abilities for understanding instructions, acquiring information, and demonstrating respect.

## Language Conventions: Punctuation
Learners understand that punctuation signals the structure and meaning of sentences. They learn to recognize and apply the period (.) to mark the end of a declarative statement, as in "The cat is napping." Similarly, they use a question mark (?) to signal a question, such as "Is the cat napping?" Students practice reading and writing simple sentences to differentiate between statements and questions, observing how each punctuation mark clarifies the speaker's intent. Instruction emphasizes that both signals are placed at the conclusion of a complete thought.

## Non-Fiction: Identifying Facts
Learners develop foundational comprehension skills by distinguishing factual information from fictional narrative. Students learn to identify key facts within simple non-fiction texts, such as identifying the main point in "A giraffe has a very long neck." They practice locating specific details, such as an animal's size, habitat, or diet, and verifying these facts against images or real-world observations. The methodology involves guided reading of captions and simple sentences to extract explicit information. For example, after reading "Penguins live in Antarctica," learners can point to the penguin on the page and state its habitat as a learned fact. This process reinforces the connection between textual evidence and real-world knowledge.
