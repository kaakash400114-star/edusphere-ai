# International Baccalaureate (IB) Grade 4 English
## Narrative Structure and Story Elements
Learners investigate the components of a narrative. They identify and analyse the main elements: plot (beginning, middle, and end), characters, setting, and conflict. Students learn that the plot is the sequence of events, the characters are the people or beings in the story, and the setting is the time and place. For example, in the story of 'The Three Little Pigs', the plot involves building houses and a wolf, the characters are the three pigs and the wolf, and the setting is a countryside with different building materials.

## Understanding Character Motivation
Learners explore the concept of character motivation, which is the reason why a character acts in a certain way. They use evidence from the text to support their inferences. For instance, in the story of 'Cinderella', learners can infer that Cinderella's motivation for attending the ball is to find happiness and escape her difficult life with her stepsisters. Students learn to ask 'Why did the character do that?' to uncover the driving forces behind actions, which is essential for comprehension.

## Identifying the Main Idea and Supporting Details
Learners investigate how to identify the main idea, which is the central message or key point of a text. They learn that the main idea is often stated in a topic sentence, but sometimes must be inferred. Supporting details are the specific examples or facts that explain and back up the main idea. For example, in a paragraph about the water cycle, the main idea is 'the continuous movement of water on Earth,' and supporting details include evaporation, condensation, and precipitation.

## Figurative Language and Imagery
Learners explore figurative language, such as similes, metaphors, and personification, to understand how authors create vivid imagery. A simile compares two things using 'like' or 'as' (e.g., 'Her smile was as bright as the sun'). A metaphor is a direct comparison (e.g., 'He is a shining star'). Personification gives human qualities to non-human things (e.g., 'The wind whispered through the trees'). Students analyse how these devices make writing more engaging and descriptive.

## Informational Text Features
Learners investigate the features of informational texts, such as headings, subheadings, captions, glossaries, and indexes. They understand that these features help to navigate and find information efficiently. For example, in a non-fiction book about animals, a heading like 'Predators of the Savannah' signals the topic of the section, while a caption under a picture of a lion explains its behaviour. Students learn to use these tools to locate specific facts and support their research.

## Structuring Explanatory Writing
Learners learn to write clear and structured explanatory texts. They follow a logical order by using an introduction to state the topic, body paragraphs with facts and details to explain the topic, and a concluding summary to bring the information together. For example, when writing an explanation of the life cycle of a butterfly, students would begin by introducing the stages, then detail each stage (egg, caterpillar, chrysalis, butterfly), and finish by summarizing the process.
