# International Baccalaureate (IB) Grade 10 Science  
## Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table  
Students learn that atoms consist of protons, neutrons, and electrons, with the atomic number defining the element. Electron configuration across periods and groups explains chemical reactivity. For example, sodium (Na, 11 protons) has an electron configuration of 2.8.1, losing one electron to form Na⁺. Trends include increasing atomic radius down a group and decreasing across a period due to effective nuclear charge. Halogens like chlorine (Cl) are highly reactive due to their seven valence electrons, seeking one to complete their shell. The periodic table organizes elements by these properties, enabling predictions of behaviour.  

## Chemical Bonding and Reactions  
Students investigate ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding. Ionic bonds form via electron transfer, as in sodium chloride (Na⁺Cl⁻), while covalent bonds involve sharing, like in methane (CH₄). Metal bonds have delocalized electrons, explaining conductivity. They balance equations, e.g., 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, ensuring mass conservation. Reaction types include synthesis (2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO), decomposition (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂), and single-replacement (Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂). Catalysts like manganese dioxide speed up reactions without being consumed.  

## Energy Transformations and Thermodynamics  
Students explore energy conversion, e.g., chemical to thermal in combustion (C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O). The law of conservation of energy states energy cannot be created or destroyed. They calculate efficiency (useful output/total input × 100%). Specific heat capacity (Q = mcΔT) determines energy transfer; water’s high value (4.18 J/g°C) stabilizes temperatures. Global examples include geothermal plants using Earth’s heat and solar panels converting sunlight to electricity. Entropy measures disorder, increasing in spontaneous processes like ice melting.  

## Electricity and Magnetism  
Students analyse circuits using Ohm’s Law (V = IR). For example, a 12V battery with a 6Ω resistor yields a 2A current (I = V/R). Series circuits share current, parallel share voltage. Magnetic fields arise from moving charges; electromagnets use coiled wire and a core (e.g., iron). Electromagnetic induction, discovered by Faraday, generates current by changing magnetic fields, applied in generators. Lenz’s law states induced current opposes the change. Global examples include wind turbines converting kinetic energy to electricity and MRI machines using strong magnetic fields.  

## Forces and Motion  
Students apply Newton’s laws: an object accelerates (F = ma) when a net force acts on it. For instance, a 5N force on a 2kg mass gives 2.5 m/s² acceleration. Projectile motion follows parabolic paths due to constant horizontal velocity and vertical acceleration (g = 9.8 m/s²). Momentum (p = mv) is conserved in collisions; a 1000kg car at 10 m/s has 10,000 kg·m/s momentum. Friction opposes motion, calculated as F = μN. Global examples include roller coaster design (potential to kinetic energy) and automotive airbags reducing impact force.  

## Environmental Science and Sustainability  
Students investigate human impacts: carbon dioxide from fossil fuels causes climate change, measured in parts per million (e.g., 420 ppm in 2023). Biodiversity loss stems from habitat destruction; coral reefs, covering 0.1% of the ocean, support 25% of marine species. Sustainable practices include renewable energy (solar, wind) reducing carbon footprints. The circular economy minimizes waste by reusing materials, like recycling aluminium cans. Students evaluate solutions, such as reforestation projects in the Amazon sequestering carbon, to address global challenges like pollution and resource depletion.
