# South Korean (NCCE) Grade 11 Science
## Quantum Mechanics and Atomic Structure
Students study the Bohr model and quantum numbers to describe electron configurations. The principle quantum number (n), azimuthal quantum number (l), magnetic quantum number (mₗ), and spin quantum number (mₛ) define an electron's state. They learn the Pauli Exclusion Principle and Hund's Rule. For instance, iron (Fe, atomic number 26) has an electron configuration of [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶, with five unpaired d-electrons. This foundational knowledge is crucial for understanding chemical bonding and the properties of elements, which are tested extensively in the CSAT.

## Chemical Bonding and Molecular Geometry
This section covers ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding using valence bond theory and hybridization. Students learn to predict molecular geometry using VSEPR theory. For example, the methane molecule (CH₄) undergoes sp³ hybridization, resulting in a tetrahedral shape with a bond angle of 109.5°. In contrast, carbon dioxide (CO₂) is linear due to sp hybridization. Understanding these principles explains the physical and chemical properties of substances, such as the hardness of diamonds (giant covalent structure) versus the low melting point of iodine (simple molecular structure).

## Thermodynamics and Chemical Equilibrium
Students learn the first and second laws of thermodynamics, focusing on enthalpy (ΔH), entropy (ΔS), and Gibbs free energy (ΔG). The equation ΔG = ΔH - TΔS determines reaction spontaneity. A reaction with a negative ΔG is spontaneous, like the fermentation of cabbage into kimchi, which releases heat (exothermic, ΔH < 0). They also study Le Chatelier's Principle, explaining how increasing pressure affects the equilibrium in the Haber process (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃) to produce ammonia for fertilizers, vital for South Korea's agricultural sector.

## Organic Chemistry: Hydrocarbons and Functional Groups
Students identify and name alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, and aromatic compounds. They learn the structure of benzene (C₆H₆) and its stability due to resonance. Functional groups like alcohols (-OH), carboxylic acids (-COOH), and amines (-NH₂) determine a molecule's reactivity. For example, ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) is the active component in soju, a traditional Korean alcoholic beverage. Nomenclature follows IUPAC rules, such as naming CH₃COOH as ethanoic acid, a key component in vinegar used in Korean cuisine.

## Electromagnetism and Its Applications
This module covers electric fields, magnetic fields, and electromagnetic induction. Students apply Faraday's Law (ε = -NΔΦ/Δt) to understand how generators produce alternating current (AC). In Seoul, the electric grid relies on this principle to power homes and the high-speed KTX trains. They also study the Lorentz force (F = q(v × B)), explaining the deflection of electrons in a cathode ray tube, which was foundational for older television displays. Applications include the design of efficient electric motors used in industry.

## Modern Physics: Nuclear Physics and Energy
Students examine nuclear reactions, including fission and fusion. They learn about mass defect and binding energy, calculated using Einstein's equation E=mc². Fission powers nuclear reactors, providing a significant portion of South Korea's electricity, reducing reliance on imported fossil fuels. Radioactive decay follows first-order kinetics, with a half-life (t₁/₂) defined as t₁/₂ = ln(2)/λ. For example, Carbon-14 dating is used to determine the age of ancient artifacts like hanbok textiles found in historical sites.
