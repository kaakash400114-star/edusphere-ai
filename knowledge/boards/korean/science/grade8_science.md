# South Korean (NCCE) Grade 8 Science
## The Nature of Chemical Bonds
In Grade 8, students learn that chemical bonds form through the transfer or sharing of electrons to achieve stable electron configurations. Ionic bonds result from the complete transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal, creating charged ions held by electrostatic forces, like sodium chloride (table salt). Covalent bonds involve the sharing of electron pairs between non-metal atoms, such as the molecules of water (H₂O) or carbon dioxide (CO₂). The type of bond determines a substance's properties, influencing its state of matter at room temperature, solubility in water, and electrical conductivity.

## Chemical Equations and Stoichiometry
Students understand that chemical equations represent chemical reactions, showing reactants on the left and products on the right, separated by an arrow. They must be balanced to obey the law of conservation of mass, meaning the number of atoms of each element is equal on both sides. For example, the combustion of methane in Seoul's heating systems is represented as CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Stoichiometry uses these balanced equations to calculate the quantities of reactants needed and products formed, which is crucial for industrial processes like producing kimchi in bulk.

## The Composition and Classification of Matter
Grade 8 science classifies matter into pure substances and mixtures. Pure substances can be elements (like gold or oxygen) or compounds (like water or carbon dioxide). Mixtures are combinations of two or more substances that are not chemically bonded and can be separated by physical means, such as filtering sand from water in the Han River. Homogeneous mixtures have a uniform composition, like a cup of coffee, while heterogeneous mixtures have a non-uniform composition, like a bowl of bibimbap.

## Mechanics and Forces
Students learn that force is a push or a pull that can change the motion of an object, measured in Newtons (N). According to Newton's first law, an object at rest stays at rest and an object in motion stays in motion at a constant velocity unless acted upon by an unbalanced force. A common example is the force needed to push a shopping cart in a Seoul market. Forces can be contact forces, like friction, or non-contact forces, like gravity, which pulls objects towards the centre of the Earth.

## Electricity and Magnetism
This topic covers the fundamentals of electric circuits and magnetism. Students learn that electric current is the flow of electric charge, measured in Amperes (A), and requires a complete path, a source of voltage (like a battery), and a load. Ohm's Law (V = IR) describes the relationship between voltage (V), current (I), and resistance (R). For example, a 5 V battery with a 10 Ω resistor produces a current of 0.5 A. Magnets have two poles, north and south, and like poles repel while unlike poles attract, a principle used in the design of high-speed trains like the KTX.

## Heredity and Variation
Students study the principles of heredity, learning that traits are passed from parents to offspring via genes on chromosomes. DNA, the molecule that carries genetic information, is found in the nucleus of cells. In humans, there are 23 pairs of chromosomes. Gregor Mendel's laws of inheritance explain how traits are expressed through dominant and recessive alleles. For example, a child inherits one gene for eye colour from each parent, determining whether their eyes are dark, like a traditional hanbok, or light.
