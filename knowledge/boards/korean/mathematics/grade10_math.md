# South Korean (NCCE) Grade 10 Mathematics

## Understanding Functions
Students analyze various types of functions, focusing on quadratic, rational, and irrational functions. They master methods for determining the domain, range, and asymptotes. Key topics include finding the maximum and minimum values of quadratic functions using the vertex formula, $f(x) = a(x-h)^2 + k$, and understanding the properties of composite functions. Practical applications involve calculating profit models for businesses in Seoul or analyzing population growth trends within South Korea's provinces.

## Equations and Inequalities
Students master solving quadratic equations using the quadratic formula, completing the square, and factoring, while extending this to solve inequalities by considering the sign changes of the roots. For fractional equations, they must identify and exclude extraneous solutions that result in division by zero. Students learn to solve systems of two linear equations using substitution and elimination methods, and systems of three equations using systematic elimination. The absolute value function is explored through equations and inequalities of the form |ax + b| = c or |ax + b| < c, requiring the application of defined rules for splitting the expression. These skills are applied to model real-world phenomena like calculating the optimal production levels for a local craft business to maximize profit or analyzing the range of possible investment yields.

## Sequences and Series
In Grade 10 Mathematics, students master the explicit and recursive formulas for arithmetic and geometric sequences to determine the nth term. They learn to calculate the sum of the first n terms using specific formulas, such as the formula for the sum of a finite geometric series. The curriculum introduces mathematical induction as a formal proof technique to verify general statements for all positive integers. Students apply these concepts to model real-world scenarios, like calculating the accumulated number of visitors to the Namsan Seoul Tower over multiple years using arithmetic series, or determining the total cost of a national pension plan with a fixed annual contribution, reflecting practical financial applications in South Korea.

## Plane and Solid Geometry
This topic explores the properties of two- and three-dimensional figures, including circles, polygons, prisms, and pyramids. Students learn to calculate the area of composite shapes and the volume of complex solids. Key theorems, such as the Pythagorean theorem and the properties of similar triangles, are applied. A practical example involves calculating the surface area of a traditional kim fermentation jar or determining the optimal dimensions for a new building in Gangnam.

## Probability and Statistics
Students master probability rules for single events, applying the addition rule for mutually exclusive events and the multiplication rule for independent events, while also calculating conditional probability. In statistics, they compute key measures of central tendency, such as mean and median, along with dispersion metrics including variance and standard deviation, to analyse data sets effectively. Students represent these distributions visually through histograms and box plots, gaining skills in data interpretation. An applied example involves using a binomial distribution model to predict consumer preference success for a new fusion product by surveying a sample of the target market. This integrated approach connects theoretical probability with statistical analysis for practical decision-making.

## Trigonometric Functions
Students define trigonometric functions using the unit circle, where the sine and cosine of an angle correspond to the y- and x-coordinates of a point on the circle, respectively. They solve for unknown sides and angles in non-right-angled triangles using the Law of Sines (a/sinA = b/sinB) and the Law of Cosines (c² = a² + b² - 2ab cosC), essential for applications in surveying and navigation. Students analyze the properties of sine, cosine, and tangent functions, including their periods, amplitudes, and asymptotes, to graph their transformations and solve equations such as sin(x) = cos(x). For example, they might use the Law of Cosines to calculate the length of a support cable for a suspension bridge in Busan or apply trigonometric graphs to model the periodic motion of a traditional Korean fan.
