# South Korean (NCCE) Grade 12 Mathematics
## Vectors and Operations in 3D Space
Students master the manipulation of vectors in three-dimensional Cartesian coordinates. They learn to represent vectors using component form, i, j, k notation, and magnitude-direction representation. Key operations include vector addition, subtraction, and scalar multiplication, which follow the parallelogram law. The dot product is defined as the sum of the products of corresponding components and is used to find the angle between two vectors and the projection of one vector onto another. For example, the dot product of vector A = (3, -2, 1) and vector B = (4, 1, -2) is calculated as (3)(4) + (-2)(1) + (1)(-2) = 12 - 2 - 2 = 8.
## The Cross Product and its Geometric Applications
This section introduces the cross product of two vectors in three-dimensional space, defined only for non-parallel vectors. Students learn to compute the cross product using a determinant method involving the unit vectors i, j, and k. The geometric interpretation is crucial: the resulting vector is orthogonal to the plane formed by the original vectors, with a magnitude equal to the area of the parallelogram they span. For instance, the cross product of vectors representing the width and length of a rectangular plot of land in Seoul would yield a vector perpendicular to the plot, with a magnitude equal to the plot's area, useful for calculating construction materials.
## Equations of Lines and Planes in 3D Space
Students learn to derive and use vector equations, parametric equations, and symmetric (Cartesian) equations for lines in three-dimensional space. For planes, the standard form using the normal vector and a point is covered. A key skill is finding the equation of a plane given three non-collinear points or a line and a point not on the line. An example is determining the equation of a flat roof (a plane) for a modern Seoul building, where the coordinates of its corners are used to find the plane's equation for architectural planning and material estimation.
## Matrices, Determinants, and their Properties
This module focuses on matrix operations, including addition, scalar multiplication, and matrix multiplication. Students explore the properties of the identity and zero matrices. The calculation of determinants for 2x2, 3x3, and larger matrices is taught, using methods like cofactor expansion. A critical concept is that a matrix is invertible if and only if its determinant is non-zero. For example, calculating the determinant of a matrix representing the price fluctuations of kimchi ingredients over three months can help determine if the price system is stable or susceptible to singular, unpredictable shifts.
## Introduction to Linear Transformations
Students study how matrices can represent linear transformations, such as rotation, reflection, scaling, and shearing in the plane and in space. They learn to find the matrix corresponding to a given transformation and to apply a transformation matrix to a vector or geometric shape. Concepts like the kernel and image of a transformation are introduced. An application is designing a computer graphics program to scale and rotate a digital image of a hanbok, where transformation matrices are used to map the original pixels to new positions to create the desired visual effect.
## Introduction to Probability and Statistics
This section covers advanced probability, focusing on conditional probability, independent events, and Bayes' Theorem. In statistics, students learn about discrete and continuous probability distributions, particularly the binomial and normal distributions. They practice calculating probabilities and expected values. For instance, a company in South Korea might use the binomial distribution to model the probability of a certain number of defective units in a batch of products, while the normal distribution could model the distribution of citizens' heights across Seoul for ergonomic design of public seating.
