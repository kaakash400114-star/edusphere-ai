# Tamil Nadu State Board Class 5 English

## Abstract and material nouns

Abstract nouns are words that name ideas, feelings, or qualities that cannot be touched or seen, like happiness, courage, and freedom. Material nouns are words that name things we can touch, feel, or see, such as water, sand, and gold. We learn to tell the difference between them by asking if we can hold the thing; if yes, it is a material noun. For example, “bravery” is abstract because we cannot see it, but “basketball” is material because we can play with it. This helps us understand and use words correctly when we speak and write.

## Relative pronouns

We learn about relative pronouns like who, which, and that which join sentences together to give more details. A relative pronoun replaces a noun and connects it to the main part of the sentence, like saying "The girl who is wearing a red dress is my friend." We use 'who' for people, such as "The boy who runs fast won the race." We use 'which' for animals and things, like "The dog which barks loudly is my neighbour's pet." We use 'that' for people, animals, or things, for example, "This is the book that I wanted to read." These words help us make our sentences longer and more interesting.

## Perfect tenses (has/have done)

Perfect tenses tell us about actions that are finished but still important today. We use “has” for one person or thing, like “He has finished his homework.” We use “have” for two or more, like “They have played in the park.” To make a sentence, we say “has/have” + the past form of the verb. For example, “She has drawn a picture” means the drawing is done and we see it now. This helps us talk about things that happened before and matter now.

## Modals: can-may-must

Children learn to use the modals ‘can’, ‘may’, and ‘must’ to express different ideas about ability, permission, and necessity. ‘Can’ is used to show what someone is able to do, for example, "I can ride a bicycle." ‘May’ is used to ask for or give permission, like "May I go to the playground?" ‘Must’ is used to show something that is necessary or a rule, such as "You must wear a helmet while cycling." We use these special words before the main verb to share what is possible, allowed, or required. Remembering how to use ‘can’, ‘may’, and ‘must’ helps us speak and write clearly.

## Active-passive intro

In Class 5, students learn to change sentences from active voice to passive voice. The passive voice focuses on the action being done, not who does it. For example, in “The dog chased the cat,” the subject “dog” does the action. In the passive form, “The cat was chased by the dog,” the cat becomes the subject. To make passive sentences, we use forms of the verb “be” (like is, was, are) and the past participle of the main verb, like “chased.” We also use the word “by” to show who did the action, if needed. This helps children understand how sentences can be written in different ways.

## Reported statements

In Class 5, we learn to change statements from direct speech into reported speech. When reporting what someone says, we move the verb back and remove quotation marks. For example, "I am happy" becomes "He said that he was happy". We also change the pronouns to match the speaker, like changing "I" to "he" or "she". The tense of the verb usually moves one step back, so present tense becomes past tense. This helps us share what others have said in a clear way.

## Punctuation and paragraphs

In Class 5, we learn to use punctuation marks like full stops, question marks, and commas correctly to make our writing clear. We also learn to write paragraphs by grouping related sentences together, starting with a main idea. For example, we put a full stop at the end of a thought like "The sun is bright." and start a new sentence for a new idea like "I like to play outside." We use a comma to separate words in a list, such as "I bought apples, bananas, and oranges." These rules help us write neatly and make others understand our writing easily.

## Fact vs opinion reading

In this topic, children learn to distinguish between facts and opinions by identifying statements that can be proven true and those that show feelings. A fact is something true for everyone, like "The sun rises in the east," while an opinion is what someone believes, such as "Chocolate is the best flavour." They practise by reading sentences and sorting them into facts and opinions using clues like numbers and dates for facts, and words like "best" or "beautiful" for opinions. Through simple stories and examples, children understand that facts are always true, but opinions can be different for each person.

## Formal letter

In Class 5, we learn to write formal letters to important people like the Headmaster or a government officer. A formal letter must have a clear structure: the sender’s address on top left, the date below, then the receiver’s address on top right. We start by writing a proper subject line like “Request for New Library Books,” followed by a respectful greeting like “Respected Sir/Madam.” The letter must be polite, use full sentences, and end with “Yours faithfully” or “Yours sincerely,” followed by our name and signature. For example, we can write a letter to the Headmaster asking for more playtime in the school playground.

## Story completion

In Class 5, students learn to complete stories by writing logical and interesting endings. They use story clues like characters and settings to guess what happens next. For example, if a story ends with a lost puppy, students write how the puppy finds its way home. They also learn to use simple sentences and correct grammar to finish the story. This helps children think creatively and follow a story's structure. By practising, they become better at writing and understanding how stories work.
