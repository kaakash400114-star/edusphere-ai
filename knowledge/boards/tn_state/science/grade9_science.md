# Tamil Nadu State Board Class 9 Science

## Matter in our surroundings

In this topic, we learn that matter is anything that has mass and takes up space, like water in a glass or the air we breathe. We discover that matter can exist in three main forms: solid (with a fixed shape, like a stone), liquid (which flows and takes the shape of its container, like milk), and gas (which has no fixed shape or volume, like the steam from a kettle). We also understand that matter can change from one form to another through processes like melting (ice to water) and freezing (water to ice), but the total amount of matter stays the same.

## Is matter around us pure

Matter around us can be pure or impure. Pure substances are made of only one type of particle, like salt or sugar, and cannot be broken down into other substances by physical methods. A mixture contains two or more pure substances that are mixed together but not chemically combined, such as salt dissolved in water or air, which is a mixture of gases. We can separate the components of a mixture using physical methods like filtration, to separate sand from water, or evaporation, to get salt back from salt water. A solution is a special kind of mixture where one substance dissolves completely in another, like lemonade made by mixing lemon juice in water.

## Atoms and molecules

In class 9, you learn that everything is made of tiny particles called atoms and molecules. Atoms are the smallest unit of an element, like a single gold atom, while molecules are formed when two or more atoms join together, like a water molecule (H₂O) with two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. You will study the law of conservation of mass, which says that matter cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. Chemical formulas, like NaCl for sodium chloride (common salt), are used to write substances in short form using symbols. You also learn about the structure of atoms, including their tiny parts called protons, neutrons, and electrons.

## Structure of the atom

Atoms are the smallest particles of an element that take part in chemical reactions. Every atom has a central part called the nucleus, which contains protons (positively charged) and neutrons (no charge). Electrons, which are negatively charged, move in orbits or shells around the nucleus. The first shell can hold up to 2 electrons, the second up to 8, and the third up to 8 as well. For example, the oxygen atom has 8 protons, 8 neutrons, and 8 electrons, with 2 electrons in the first shell and 6 in the second shell.

## The fundamental unit of life

Living things are made of very tiny building blocks called cells. Every plant and animal is built from one or more of these basic units. The cell has three main parts: the outer wall called the cell membrane, a jelly-like substance inside called the cytoplasm, and a small part called the nucleus that acts like the control room. For example, a leaf of a plant is made of many plant cells, and a small insect is made of many animal cells. Different cells have different jobs, like muscle cells helping us move and skin cells protecting our body.

## Tissues

In class 9, we learn that tissues are groups of similar cells working together to perform a specific function in plants and animals. In plants, simple tissues like parenchyma help in storage, while complex tissues such as xylem and phloem transport water and food. In animals, epithelial tissues cover body surfaces, and muscle tissues help in movement. We also study connective tissues like blood, which carries oxygen and nutrients throughout the body. Understanding tissues helps us see how different parts of living things work together to stay healthy and alive.

## Diversity in living organisms

In Class 9 Science, students learn about the amazing diversity of living organisms around them. They study the five-kingdom classification system, which groups living things into Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia based on their characteristics. For example, they learn that bacteria belong to the Monera kingdom, while animals with backbones are placed in the Animalia kingdom. The topic also covers how plants are further divided into groups like Thallophyta, Bryophyta, and Pteridophyta, and how animals are separated into invertebrates and vertebrates. This helps students understand how scientists organise the vast variety of life on Earth.

## Motion

Students learn about motion, which is the change in an object's position with time. They understand that objects can move in a straight line or in a circular path, and they learn to identify fast and slow motion. The basic formula for speed is speed equals distance divided by time, and they use it to calculate how fast something is moving, like a car covering 60 km in 1 hour has a speed of 60 km/h. They also learn about uniform motion, where an object covers equal distances in equal time intervals, and non-uniform motion, where the speed changes. Examples include a train moving steadily on tracks and a ball rolling down a hill, speeding up as it goes.

## Force and laws of motion

In this topic, students learn about force and how it makes objects move, stop, or change direction. They study Newton’s three laws of motion, which explain that a moving object stays in motion unless a force stops it, more force is needed to move heavier objects, and every action has an equal and opposite reaction. We use examples like a cricket ball being hit, a car braking suddenly, and rockets launching into space to understand these rules. The formula for force is force equals mass times acceleration (F = m × a). Students also learn about different types of forces like friction, gravity, and muscular force.

## Gravitation

Gravitation is the force of attraction between two objects. On Earth, this force pulls everything towards the ground, making things fall. Sir Isaac Newton discovered that the same force that pulls an apple down also keeps the Moon moving around the Earth. The weight of an object is the measure of the gravitational force acting on it. We can calculate this weight using the formula: Weight = Mass × Acceleration due to gravity. For example, a book has more mass and therefore more weight than a feather because of this strong pull from our planet.

## Work and energy

Work is done when a force makes an object move, and we measure it in joules. The formula for work is Work = Force × Distance, like pushing a book across a table. Energy is the ability to do work, and it comes in different forms like light, heat, and sound. When we lift a ball, we give it potential energy, and when it falls, that changes to kinetic energy. Energy can never be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another, like a battery powering a torch.

## Sound

Sound is a form of energy produced by vibrations that travel through a medium like air, water, or solids. We hear sounds when these vibrations reach our ears and make the eardrum vibrate. Sound travels as waves, and it needs a medium to move; it cannot travel in a vacuum like outer space. The loudness of a sound is measured in decibels (dB), and its pitch depends on how fast the object vibrates—faster vibrations create higher-pitched sounds. For example, a drum has a low pitch because it vibrates slowly, while a whistle has a high pitch because it vibrates quickly.

## Why do we fall ill

When we fall ill, our body is invaded by tiny germs called microorganisms, like bacteria or viruses, which can make us sick. Our body has a natural defense system called immunity that fights these germs and protects us from getting diseases. Sometimes, when we eat unhealthy food, do not get enough rest, or live in dirty surroundings, our immunity becomes weak, and germs can easily attack us. Some common diseases caused by germs are colds, fever, and stomach pain, which can be prevented by washing our hands, eating nutritious food, and keeping our surroundings clean. Maintaining good health habits helps our body stay strong and fight off illnesses effectively.

## Natural resources

Natural resources are things we get from nature, like air, water, soil, and minerals. They are divided into two types: renewable resources, such as sunlight and trees which can be replaced, and non-renewable resources, like coal and petroleum which get used up forever. We must protect our natural resources because they are very important for all living things to survive. For example, we use clean water to drink and plants to make food. If we waste resources, they will finish, and we will not have them for our future.

## Improvement in food resources

Students learn about improving food resources by using better seeds, manure, and fertilisers to grow more food. They study the importance of crops like rice, wheat, and pulses, and how irrigation helps in growing them well. Children also learn about protecting crops from insects and diseases using natural methods or pesticides. They understand that animals need good feed, shelter, and care to give us milk, eggs, and meat. Finally, students explore how we can grow more food by using modern farming methods without harming our environment.
