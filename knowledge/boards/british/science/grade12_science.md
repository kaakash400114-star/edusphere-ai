# British Year 12 Science - National Curriculum/GCSE

## A-Level Biology: biological molecules-cells

In A-Level Biology, you will learn about the four main biological molecules: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. You will explore their specific chemical structures, such as the glucose ring in carbohydrates or the amino acid chain in proteins, and understand their functions, like how proteins act as enzymes. For instance, you will discover that starch is a polysaccharide made of many glucose units used for energy storage in plants. You will also examine the structure of cells, identifying organelles like the nucleus, which controls the cell and contains DNA, and the mitochondria, which produce energy through respiration.

## A-Level Chemistry: physical-inorganic-organic foundations

In this module, you will learn how to write the names and formulas for different types of chemical compounds, including simple ionic salts like sodium chloride (table salt) and covalent molecules such as water. You will explore the giant structures, like ionic lattices and metallic bonding, and find out how to draw dot-and-cross diagrams to show how electrons are shared or transferred between atoms. The course also covers basic organic chemistry, where you will study the structures and reactions of key compounds like alkanes and alcohols, including methane and ethanol, which you might find in natural gas and alcoholic drinks. Understanding these ideas helps explain why substances have the properties they do, from why metals conduct electricity to why plastics are strong.

## A-Level Physics: particles-radiation-waves-mechanics

In this topic, you learn about the tiny particles that make up everything, like atoms and quarks, and how they can give off energy in the form of radiation. You explore how waves, such as light and sound, travel through different materials and can be reflected or bent. The rules of motion and forces are key, with Newton’s laws explaining why objects move and how to calculate their speed using formulas like speed = distance ÷ time. You also see how forces, like gravity and friction, affect things on Earth and in space. For example, understanding waves helps explain how radios work, and studying forces shows why a car needs brakes to stop safely.

## Practical-skills: required-techniques

In Year 12, you will learn essential laboratory techniques to handle chemicals and equipment safely, following strict rules like wearing goggles and tying back long hair. You will practise measuring substances accurately using pipettes, burettes, and top-pan balances, ensuring readings are precise for reliable results. You will also master methods like titration to find the concentration of an acid or alkali, and chromatography to separate and identify mixtures, such as inks or food colourings. Recording data systematically and drawing valid conclusions from your observations is a key part of developing your practical skills for GCSE Science.

## Exchange-transport-genetic-information

In this topic, you learn how substances like glucose and oxygen move in and out of cells through diffusion and osmosis, which are passive processes. You also explore how substances are actively transported using energy against a concentration gradient, for example, when root cells take in mineral ions from the soil. You study how genetic information is stored in DNA as a double helix structure and copied during cell division through mitosis. Finally, you discover how DNA codes for proteins by carrying instructions for amino acids, which are linked in specific sequences by ribosomes in the cytoplasm.

## Energetics-kinetics-equilibria

In this topic, you learn how energy changes in chemical reactions using enthalpy, with exothermic reactions releasing heat and endothermic reactions absorbing it, like a hand-warmer getting hot. You also discover reaction rates, which speed up with higher temperatures and greater surface area, such as powdered magnesium reacting faster with acid than a solid lump. Finally, you explore reversible reactions and equilibrium, where the forward and backward reactions occur at the same rate, as seen in the Haber process for making ammonia, which reaches a balance point.

## Periodicity-group-2-7

In GCSE Chemistry, you explore how elements in Groups 2 and 7 show periodic trends, meaning their properties change as you go down the group. Group 2 metals, like magnesium and calcium, become more reactive as you descend the group and form compounds with oxygen and chlorine, such as magnesium oxide (MgO). Group 7 non-metals, like fluorine and chlorine, become less reactive down the group and gain one electron to form negative ions, like chloride (Cl⁻). You also learn about the displacement reactions, where a more reactive element can push a less reactive one out of its compounds. For example, chlorine displaces bromine from potassium bromide solution.

## Currents-fields

In the Currents-Fields topic, you learn about how electric currents create magnetic fields and how magnets can induce electricity. You discover that a current flowing through a wire produces a circular magnetic field around it, which you can find using the right-hand grip rule. For example, this is used in electromagnets to lift heavy scrap metal. Faraday’s Law shows that moving a magnet in a coil of wire generates an electric current, which is the basis of generators. Fleming’s left-hand rule helps you predict the direction of the force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field, essential for understanding how electric motors work.

## Nuclei-thermal

In the Nuclei-thermal topic, you learn how the nucleus of an atom holds most of its mass and all of its positive charge. You discover that radioactive atoms are unstable and release radiation to become more stable, with different types like alpha, beta, and gamma. The half-life of a radioactive substance is the time it takes for half of its atoms to decay, which is a key concept used in things like carbon dating. You also explore how energy is released from nuclear reactions, such as in the Sun, where hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium and release massive amounts of heat and light. Finally, you compare the energy released in nuclear reactions to chemical reactions, seeing that nuclear changes release far more energy per atom.

## Data-analysis-experimental-design

In GCSE Science, you will learn how to design fair experiments by identifying control variables and repeating trials to ensure reliable data. You will practise calculating the mean of multiple measurements to minimise random errors and understand the difference between accuracy and precision. Graph skills include plotting data points correctly, drawing line of best fit graphs, and identifying correlations to make predictions. For example, you might test how temperature affects enzyme activity, changing only one factor while keeping others constant to see its impact.
