# British Year 8 Science - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Cells-tissues: digestion-respiration

In Year 8, you will learn that all living things are made of tiny building blocks called cells, which group together to form tissues like skin or muscle. You will find out about your digestive system, a long tube that starts with your mouth and helps break down food into tiny particles your body can use. In your respiration topic, you will see that your cells need oxygen to release energy from food, and you will explore how your lungs and bloodstream work to deliver that oxygen all around your body.

## Atoms-elements-compounds

Atoms are tiny building blocks that make up everything around you, like your chair or even the air you breathe. An element is a substance made of only one type of atom, such as gold, oxygen, or carbon, which can be found on the periodic table. When different atoms join together in a special way, they form a new substance called a compound, like water, which is made of hydrogen and oxygen atoms stuck together. Compounds have different properties to the individual elements they are made from, for example, salt (sodium chloride) is a white powder even though sodium is a metal and chlorine is a gas. You can show what these compounds are made of using simple formulas, such as H₂O for water, which tells you it has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom.

## Energy: calculated transfers

In this topic, you will learn how to calculate energy transfers using the formula energy transferred = power × time. You will explore how different devices, like light bulbs and kettles, transfer electrical energy into other forms, such as heat and light. By measuring power in watts and time in seconds, you can work out the energy transferred in joules. For example, a 100-watt bulb left on for 10 seconds transfers 1000 joules of energy. You will also understand that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only moved from one place to another.

## Forces-motion: speed-graphs

In Year 8 Science, you will learn to use distance-time graphs to show how things move. A straight line means the object is moving at a steady speed, and a flat line shows it has stopped. You will find out how to calculate speed using the formula: speed = distance ÷ time. For example, if a car travels 100 metres in 20 seconds, its speed is 5 metres per second. Steeper lines on a graph mean a faster speed, while gentle lines show a slower speed.

## Light-sound: wave models

Light and sound both travel as waves, which are like moving patterns of energy that can be reflected, absorbed or pass through materials. When light hits a shiny surface, it bounces back in a predictable way, which is why we see our reflection in a mirror. Sound needs a material like air or water to travel through, and it moves as vibrations that get smaller the further they go from the source, making distant sounds quieter. Waves have important parts like their height, which tells us how loud a sound is or how bright a light is, and their length, which affects the colour of light or the pitch of a sound.

## Reproduction-genetics intro

In this topic, you will learn that babies look like their parents because they inherit characteristics, or traits, from them. These traits are passed down through tiny things called genes, which are found inside cells. Genes act like a set of instructions, deciding things like eye colour or whether you can roll your tongue. Some characteristics are controlled by a single gene, for example, having a straight hairline or a widow’s peak. You will also learn that each parent contributes one gene for each trait, which is why you might inherit features from both of them.

## Chemical reactions: metals-acids

In science, you learn that some metals react with acids to make a new substance called a salt and bubbles of gas. The gas produced is always hydrogen, and you can test for it with a lit splint which makes a popping sound. For example, when magnesium reacts with hydrochloric acid, it makes magnesium chloride salt and hydrogen gas. The general rule is that a metal plus an acid always produces a salt and hydrogen. Different metals react at different speeds, with very reactive metals like magnesium fizzing quickly and less reactive metals like copper not reacting at all.

## Electricity-magnetism deeper

You will learn how electricity and magnetism are closely linked, like two best friends who always work together. We explore how a simple magnet can make electricity move in a wire, which is how generators make power for our homes. You will discover the rule for making an electromagnet: wrap wire around a metal nail and connect it to a battery to create a temporary magnet that can pick up paperclips. We also learn about the direction electricity flows in a circuit, from the battery’s positive side to its negative side, just like water flows from a hill down to a river. Finally, you will find out how switches can start or stop the flow of electricity in a circuit, controlling whether a light bulb lights up or stays dark.

## Periodic table-chemical patterns

In science, you will learn how the periodic table is special because it groups elements by what their atoms are like. Elements in the same group have the same number of electrons in their outer shell, which makes them behave in similar ways. For example, all group one elements, like lithium and sodium, are very reactive metals that have one electron on the outside. You will also look at how elements are arranged by their atomic number and what patterns you see, like how metals are on the left and non-metals are on the right. By studying these patterns, you can start to understand how different elements might join together to make new substances.

## Ecosystems-material cycles

In ecosystems, materials like carbon, water, and nitrogen move in cycles between living things and their surroundings. Plants take in carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil to make food during photosynthesis, releasing oxygen. Animals eat plants or other animals, taking in these materials, and then release them back through respiration or waste when they die, decomposers like fungi and bacteria break down dead plants and animals, returning nutrients to the soil for new plants to use. These cycles ensure materials are never lost, just reused again and again in nature.
