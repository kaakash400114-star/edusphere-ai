# British Year 9 Science - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Biology: cells-transport-organisation

In Year 9 biology, you will learn that all living things are made of cells, which are tiny building blocks like bricks for a wall. You will find out how these cells get the food and oxygen they need through a process called diffusion, which is like sugar dissolving in water to spread out evenly. You will also explore how different cells work together to form tissues, like muscle tissue in your arm, which then build organs such as your heart. Finally, you will see how organs, like the stomach and lungs, work together in organ systems to keep your body alive and working properly.

## Chemistry: atomic structure-bonding-quantities

In Year 9 Science, you learn that all solids, liquids, and gases are made of tiny particles called atoms. Atoms join together using chemical bonds, like ionic bonds between metals and non-metals to form salts (such as sodium chloride, or table salt) or covalent bonds between non-metals to make water (H₂O). You will also discover how to count these particles using the mole in chemistry, which helps you measure the amount of a substance in a reaction, like figuring out how much oxygen is needed to burn a candle. Finally, you explore how different arrangements of atoms create unique materials, from hard diamond to soft graphite.

## Physics: energy-forces-waves in depth

In Year 9 physics, you learn that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only moved or changed, like turning electrical energy into light and heat in a lamp. You explore forces, which are pushes or pulls, and find out that they can make objects speed up, slow down, or change direction, such as friction slowing down a toy car. Waves transfer energy without moving matter, like light waves letting us see and sound waves letting us hear, and you discover how they can reflect off mirrors or bend when passing through water.

## Genetics-evolution

In genetics, you learn how living things inherit features from their parents, like eye colour or height, through tiny parts called genes. You discover that different versions of genes, called alleles, decide if you have brown eyes or blue eyes. Evolution happens when living things change over many years to survive better, like giraffes growing longer necks to reach higher leaves. These changes happen because nature selects the best features for a particular environment, helping animals and plants adapt and stay alive.

## Chemical changes-electrolysis

In Year 9 Science, you will learn that electrolysis is the process of using electricity to split substances called ionic compounds into simpler parts, like when passing an electric current through molten sodium chloride. You will discover that ionic compounds must be molten or dissolved in water to conduct electricity, as their ions can move freely; for example, copper chloride solution breaks down into copper metal at the negative electrode and chlorine gas at the positive electrode. The rules of electrolysis explain that positive ions move to the negative electrode (cathode) to gain electrons, while negative ions move to the positive electrode (anode) to lose electrons. This helps explain how metals are extracted from ores and how metals are plated onto other surfaces.

## Forces-motion equations

In Year 9, you learn how to use forces and motion equations to describe how things move. You discover the formula speed = distance ÷ time, which helps you calculate how fast an object is travelling, like finding the speed of a car that goes 60 miles in 2 hours. You also learn about the relationship between force, mass and acceleration with the formula force = mass × acceleration, which explains why pushing a heavy shopping trolley takes more effort than pushing an empty one. These equations let you describe and predict the movement of objects in different situations.

## Electricity-circuits

In this topic, you learn how electricity moves in circuits and the rules that make it work safely. You find out that a circuit needs a power source, like a battery, and conductors, such as wires, to let the current flow. Ohm’s Law teaches you how voltage, current, and resistance are linked with the formula V = I × R. For example, if a bulb has a high resistance, it lets less current through. You also discover that components must be connected in series or parallel, and parallel circuits let electricity take different paths, so if one bulb breaks, the others still light up.

## Organic chemistry intro

In organic chemistry, you learn about carbon compounds, which are the building blocks of living things like plants and animals. A key rule is that carbon atoms share electrons with other atoms, forming covalent bonds to create different molecules. You will see how carbon atoms can link together in long chains or rings, like the straight chain in propane (C₃H₈) or the ring shape in cyclohexane. Simple molecules, such as methane (CH₄) and ethanol (C₂H₅OH), are studied to understand their structures and how they are made. You also explore functional groups, which are special groups of atoms that determine a molecule’s behaviour, like the -OH group in alcohols.

## Space physics

In this topic, you will learn about Earth, the Sun and other planets in our solar system, and how they move in space. You will understand why we have day and night because the Earth spins on its axis, and the reason for seasons is because the Earth tilts as it orbits the Sun. You will also find out about gravity, a force that pulls objects towards each other, which keeps planets like Earth in orbit around the Sun and makes apples fall from trees. By looking at the Moon, you will see how it orbits the Earth and its changing shape creates the different moon phases we see, like a full moon or a crescent.

## Required practical skills

In Year 9 Science, you will learn how to carry out practical work safely by always wearing goggles and following instructions carefully. You will practise using measuring tools like rulers, thermometers, and stopwatches to get accurate results for your experiments. It is important to know how to plan a fair test by only changing one thing and keeping everything else the same. You will also learn to record your observations in tables and draw simple line graphs to show what you found. For example, you might measure how the temperature of water changes over time when it cools down.
