# British Year 10 Science - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Biology: cell biology-organisation-infection-response

In Year 10, you learn that all living things are made of tiny building blocks called cells, which group together to form tissues like muscle or skin. These tissues then work together as organs, such as the heart or stomach, to perform specific jobs within an organ system like the circulatory system. You also discover how bacteria and viruses can make you ill by invading your body and multiplying, but your immune system fights back by producing white blood cells that destroy these germs to keep you healthy. Understanding how cells are organised helps you see how your body stays protected from infections every day.

## Chemistry: atomic structure-bonding-quantitative chemistry

In GCSE Science, you will learn that everything is made of tiny particles called atoms, which have a small central nucleus packed with protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons. You will explore how atoms bond together by sharing or transferring electrons, forming structures like simple molecules (such as water, H₂O) or giant lattices like salt. You will also practice balancing chemical equations to show how atoms are never lost or created in reactions, and use the periodic table to calculate the amounts of substances in grams and moles using formulas like moles = mass ÷ relative formula mass.

## Physics: energy-electricity-particle model

In Year 10 Physics, you learn how energy moves through electrical circuits and how it is measured in joules. You discover that electricity flows when a complete circuit is made, including a power source like a battery and components such as bulbs or motors. The relationship between voltage, current, and resistance is explained by Ohm’s Law, which helps us calculate how much electricity flows. You also explore the particle model to explain solids, liquids, and gases, and how energy changes when materials melt or freeze, like ice turning into water.

## Required practicals: microscopy-osmosis-circuits

In microscopy, you learn to prepare slides safely using a microscope to see tiny cells, like onion skin or cheek cells, and must focus carefully using the coarse and fine focus knobs. For osmosis, you investigate how water moves through a partially permeable membrane by placing potato chips in different sugar solutions and measuring their mass change to see which one gains or loses water. In circuits, you build series and parallel circuits to test how current flows and identify that series circuits have one loop while parallel circuits have more than one, using ammeters to measure the current in different parts.

## Homeostasis-response intro

Homeostasis is how our bodies keep conditions steady, like maintaining a constant temperature. We learn about key examples, such as keeping blood sugar levels at about 4-5 mmol/L after eating sweets. Other examples include regulating water balance through the kidneys and controlling body temperature through sweating or shivering. Our bodies use sensors, like thermoreceptors in the skin, to detect changes and send messages to the brain or effectors, like muscles, to respond. This response counteracts the change to maintain a stable internal environment for cells to work properly.

## Chemical changes-rates

In science, you learn about how fast chemical reactions happen, which we call the rate of reaction. You will find out that things like temperature, concentration, and surface area can speed up or slow down a reaction. For example, powdered sugar reacts much faster than a sugar cube because it has a larger surface area. You also learn how to measure the rate of a reaction by seeing how quickly a product, like gas, is made or how a reactant disappears. Changing conditions gives us control over reactions in everyday life, from baking a cake to stopping food from going off.

## Forces-motion-equations

In Year 10 Science, you learn how to describe how things move using special maths rules. You discover how to calculate an object’s speed by dividing the distance it travels by the time it takes, like working out how fast a car goes on a motorway. You also find out how to figure out an object’s final speed when it speeds up or slows down, using its starting speed, how much it changes, and the time taken. A key idea is that forces like pushes and pulls cause movement, and if they are balanced, an object will either stay still or move at a steady speed without getting faster or slower.

## Waves-light-electromagnetic

In Year 10, you learn about how light and other waves travel, and how we see colours. Light travels in straight lines but can be reflected, like in a mirror, or refracted, bending when it passes through water. You will explore the electromagnetic spectrum, which includes visible light, radio waves for TVs, and X-rays that doctors use to see inside your body. A key idea is that all these waves transfer energy and can travel through space, unlike sound which needs a material to move through. Finally, you will discover how our eyes work by detecting light and sending signals to our brain, allowing us to see the world around us.

## Organic chemistry-polymer intro

Polymers are very large molecules made by joining many smaller units called monomers together, like links in a long chain. They can be natural, such as proteins in your body or cellulose in plant cell walls, or synthetic, like plastics from factories. Addition polymers form when unsaturated monomers, such as ethene, join without losing any atoms, creating chains like polyethene used for plastic bags. Condensation polymers form when monomers join and release a small molecule, like water, for example, making nylon or polyester fabrics. These different types of polymers have unique properties that make them useful for many everyday objects, from clothing to packaging.

## Space physics (single award)

In space physics, you learn about the Solar System, the Earth’s movement, and gravity’s role. You discover how the Earth orbits the Sun once a year, and the Moon orbits the Earth roughly once a month. Gravity pulls objects together, like how it keeps planets in orbit and makes things fall to the ground. You also explore the phases of the Moon, which happen because of how the Sun lights it up as it moves around the Earth. Space physics helps us understand the bigger world beyond our planet.
