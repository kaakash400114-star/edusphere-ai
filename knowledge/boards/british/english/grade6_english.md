# British Year 6 English - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Comprehension: compare texts

In Year 6, you learn how to compare different texts to find things that are the same and things that are different. You might look at two stories and see if both characters face a similar problem, or compare a poem and a book about animals to find different words used to describe them. You will look closely at the language authors use, like describing a forest with ‘giant, leafy trees’ in one book and ‘tall, dark trees’ in another. This helps you understand why an author chooses certain words and how they make the text feel different or the same.

## Grammar: subjunctive-active-passive

We learn about the subjunctive mood, which is used to talk about wishes or things that are not real. For example, "I wish I were a bird." The active voice is when the subject does the action, like "The dog chased the ball." The passive voice is when the subject receives the action, and we use 'was' or 'were', like "The ball was chased by the dog." You can change an active sentence to a passive one by moving the object to the beginning and using 'was' or 'were'. We practice spotting these different ways to write sentences.

## Punctuation: semicolons-dashes-colons

In Year 6, you learn how to use a semicolon to link two closely related sentences that could stand alone, like "The sun was shining; the birds were singing." You also learn to use a dash to add extra information or show a sudden break in thought, for example: "We were late for school—because the bus broke down!" Finally, you will use a colon to introduce a list or an explanation, such as "I packed three things: my book, my snack, and my water bottle." These marks help your writing clearer and more interesting.

## Vocabulary: figurative-connotation

In Year 6, you will learn that words can have special meanings beyond their ordinary definitions. You will explore figurative language, such as similes, which compare two things using 'like' or 'as' (e.g., "as brave as a lion"). You will also discover connotation, which is the feeling or idea a word suggests, even if its basic meaning is the same (e.g., "slim" is positive, but "thin" can be neutral or negative). By identifying these features, you can better understand how writers create pictures in your mind and share feelings. You will practise finding these words in stories and poems to see how they make the text more exciting.

## Formal writing-layout conventions

In Year 6, you learn how to set out formal writing like letters and reports correctly. You must place your address at the top right, then the date, and the recipient’s address on the left. The formal greeting ‘Dear [Name],’ is followed by a line space before your main message, and it ends with ‘Yours sincerely,’ or ‘Yours faithfully,’. Finally, you write your name at the end. Proper layout helps the reader understand who the message is for and makes it look neat and respectful.

## Poetry: performance-analysis

In Year 6, you will learn how to say a poem out loud with clear voices and actions to make the words exciting. You will also explore the structure of a poem, counting the lines in each verse and spotting rhyming words at the end of lines. For example, in "The Cat and the Moon" by W.B. Yeats, you might notice how the sound of words helps to make the poem feel mysterious. You will find out how poets choose special words to create pictures in your head, like describing the sun as "golden". Finally, you will share your ideas about a poem with your class, explaining what you think it is about.

## Narrative: characterisation

In this topic, you learn how writers create characters by describing what they look like, how they feel, and what they say or do. For example, a character might be ‘kind’ because they always ‘share their toys’ or ‘help others’ when they are ‘sad’. You also learn to spot clues about a character’s personality from their actions, like if someone ‘runs fast’ they might be ‘energetic’. Finally, you practice writing your own characters by giving them clear details so that others can imagine them clearly too.

## Non-fiction: reports-arguments

In Year 6, you learn how to write a report that shares facts and your own opinion, which is an argument. You start by picking a topic, like why playtime is important or why we should recycle. Your report needs a clear beginning to introduce your topic, a middle with reasons and examples, and an end that repeats your main idea. For example, you could write about why we should have longer playtimes, giving reasons like "it helps our brains work better" and "we feel happier". You use facts to back up your ideas and make your argument strong so people will agree with you.

## Spelling: statutory lists

In Year 6, you will learn to spell words from the official statutory word list for your age. These are important words that you need to know how to spell correctly in your writing. The list includes words with specific spelling patterns, such as ‘necessary’ with two 'c's and one 's', and ‘embarrass’ with double 'r' and 's'. You will also learn words that sound the same but have different meanings, like 'stationery' for writing materials and 'stationary' for not moving. Practising these words will help you become a confident speller.

## Speaking: presentations-debate

In Year 6, you learn to give clear presentations by speaking confidently and planning what you want to say first, like telling your class about a favourite book or sharing a science project. You also get to join in simple debates, where you listen to others and share your own thoughts on topics such as "Should we have more playtime?" or "Are computers better than books?". The rules are to be polite, take turns, and give reasons for your ideas so everyone can understand. These activities help you get better at explaining things and listening carefully to what your friends say.
