# British Year 12 English - National Curriculum/GCSE

## A-Level English: literary-criticism-approaches

In this module, you will learn to analyse texts using different critical approaches, such as feminist, Marxist, and postcolonist criticism. For example, when reading a novel, you might explore how a feminist critic would look at the roles of male and female characters, or how a Marxist critic would examine social class and power struggles in the story. You will also learn to understand and apply key concepts like the author’s intended meaning versus a reader’s personal interpretation. By using these tools, you will be able to develop more insightful and varied interpretations of literature for your exams.

##  set-texts: close-analysis

In close analysis of GCSE set texts, you learn to examine how writers use language, structure and form to create meaning. You explore specific techniques like metaphor, symbolism and characterisation, explaining their effects on the reader. For example, you might analyse how the rhythm in a poem creates tension, or how a character’s dialogue reveals their personality. You also develop skills to link quotations to the writer’s intentions and the text’s broader themes. This involves carefully selecting evidence to support your interpretations and showing a clear understanding of the context.

## Poetry-post-1900-anthology

In this poetry module, pupils explore a diverse post-1900 anthology, analysing how poets use form, structure, and language to shape meaning. You will learn to compare two poems by examining devices like enjambment, metaphor, and the use of a persona, considering how these elements create effects on the reader. For example, you might look at Carol Ann Duffy’s use of dramatic monologue in *Havisham* or Simon Armitage’s conversational tone in *The Manhunt*. The key is to develop personal, evidenced responses, linking specific quotes to the poet’s intentions and the poem’s broader themes. This builds skills in interpretation and critical evaluation, essential for GCSE success.

## Prose-set-study

In prose-set-study, you learn to explore a whole novel in detail, thinking about how the writer uses language and structure to tell their story. You will analyse key characters, such as how they speak and act, to understand their motivations and development within the plot. The course also teaches you to identify important themes, like friendship or conflict, and to explain how the writer uses techniques such as setting or symbolism to build meaning. You will use specific examples from the text to support your answers, showing you can compare different parts of the story.

## Drama-set-study

In drama, you will study how to analyse live and recorded plays by identifying key dramatic techniques, like staging, lighting, and sound, and exploring how these create meaning for the audience. You will learn to structure your analysis by focusing on the playwright’s intentions, the use of dramatic devices, and how these shape the audience’s experience. For example, you might examine how tension is built through a character’s dialogue or how symbolism in the set design reinforces the play’s themes. You will also develop the skill of writing structured evaluations, using specific examples from the text to support your points. Ultimately, you will gain a deeper understanding of how drama communicates powerful ideas and emotions.

## Comparative-essays

In Year 12, you will learn how to write comparative essays to analyse the connections and differences between two texts, such as a novel and a poem. Your writing should have a clear structure with a focused introduction that presents your argument, paragraphs that compare specific aspects like themes or language, and a conclusion that summarises your findings. For example, you might explore how both ‘Romeo and Juliet’ and a modern love story show the idea of fate. It is essential to use precise evidence from both texts to support every point you make, ensuring your analysis is balanced and thoughtful.

## Unseen-extracts

In Year 12, you will learn how to analyse unseen extracts by closely reading the language and structure of a text. You will identify and evaluate the writer's use of techniques like imagery, tone, and sentence structure to create meaning and effect. For example, you might explain how a short sentence builds tension or how descriptive language creates a vivid setting. You will also need to make connections between the writer's choices and the themes or ideas being explored in the text. Finally, you will develop your skills by comparing two different extracts and explaining their similarities and differences.

## Context-critics-interpretations

In this topic, you learn how to explore texts by examining their context, the opinions of critics, and different interpretations. Context involves understanding the historical, social, and cultural background that shaped the writing, such as how a novel reflects the attitudes of its time. Critics’ interpretations are the analyses and viewpoints of experts, which you can compare and challenge to deepen your own reading. By evaluating these elements, you form well-supported explanations of a text’s meaning and significance. For example, you might explore how a play’s themes are influenced by the era it was written in and how critics have debated its messages.

## Essay-craft: argument-evidence

In Year 12, you will learn to build a strong argument by clearly stating your opinion on a text or topic in a focused thesis statement. You will then practice selecting and analysing the most compelling evidence, such as key quotations or specific examples, to directly support your points and convince your reader. Finally, you will develop the skill of explaining exactly how this evidence proves your idea, ensuring every paragraph links back to your main argument. For instance, you might argue a character is brave and use a scene where they face danger as your main proof.

## Independent-reading-research

In Year 12, you will learn to choose a book to read independently and then carry out a detailed investigation into a specific theme, character, or issue within it. This means you will select a suitable text, identify a focus for your research, and then gather evidence from the book to support your ideas. For example, you might explore how a character changes throughout the novel by collecting quotes and analysing their actions. Your research will help you to build a deeper understanding of the text and develop your skills in critical thinking and analysis. You will then present your findings in a structured report or essay, showing how your research supports your interpretation.
