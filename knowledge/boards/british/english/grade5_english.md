# British Year 5 English - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Prefixes-suffixes: Latin-Greek

In Year 5, you will learn how prefixes and suffixes are like word puzzles that change the meaning of a root word. A prefix is a little letter group added to the start of a word to change its meaning, such as 'un-', 'dis-', or 're-', which turn 'happy' into 'unhappy'. A suffix is added to the end of a word, like '-ful', '-less', or '-ly', which turn 'help' into 'helpful' or 'helpless'. By understanding these parts, you can work out the meaning of new, longer words and become a better reader and writer.

## Comprehension: evidence-grammar link

In Year 5, we learn how to find answers in stories by looking for proof, like a detective finding clues. When we spot tricky words, such as ‘because’, we know the reason is close by, just like a clue on a treasure map. We also check how sentences are built, making sure they make sense, like making sure puzzle pieces fit together correctly. When we find a proof, we can copy it from the book to show our thinking, just like a drawing to explain an idea. This helps us understand what the author wants to tell us, like sharing a secret with a friend.

## Relative clauses

In Year 5, you will learn to add extra information to your sentences using relative clauses. A relative clause often starts with words like who, which, where, or whose to tell us more about a person, place, or thing. You will practise adding these clauses to nouns to make your writing more interesting, for example, "The dog, which had brown spots, chased the ball" or "The girl who lives next door is my friend". You will learn to spot these clauses in stories and understand how they add detail without starting a new sentence. By the end of the topic, you will be able to use relative clauses to make your own sentences longer and more descriptive.

## Modal verbs

In Year 5, you will learn about modal verbs, which are special helper words that tell us about possibility, permission, or ability. Words like ‘can’, ‘should’, and ‘must’ are modal verbs because they change how we think about an action. For example, ‘I can jump high’ shows you are able to do something, while ‘You must hold hands’ tells someone what is necessary. These little words usually go before the main verb, like ‘We should share our toys’ to show it is a good idea. Modal verbs help us make rules, give advice, and talk about what we can or cannot do.

## Verb forms: perfect tense

In Year 5, you will learn about perfect tenses to talk about things that happened in the past. The present perfect tense uses 'has' or 'have' with a past participle (like 'walked' or 'eaten') to show something is finished but still important now, such as "I have finished my homework." The past perfect tense uses 'had' with a past participle to describe something that happened before another past event, like "She had already left when I arrived." These tenses help you tell stories and share events clearly by showing when things happened.

## Adverbials of number-type

In Year 5, we learn to use number adverbials to tell us how many times something happens, such as ‘once’ or ‘twice’. We can also use them to show how often, like ‘always’ or ‘never’, to make our sentences more exciting. For example, we might say ‘I brush my teeth twice a day’ to explain how often I do something. Sometimes, we use number words at the start of a sentence to show order, like ‘First, we line up quietly’. By using these words, we can share clear information about how many or how often actions happen.

## Brackets-dashes-commas

In Year 5, you learn to use brackets, dashes, and commas to add extra information to your sentences. Brackets are like two little hands holding a secret, like ‘The dog (a brown spaniel) barked loudly’. A dash works like a bracket, like ‘The cat—my best friend—was sleeping’. You use commas to separate items in a list, like ‘I bought apples, bananas, and pears’, or to add a little thought in the middle, like ‘My favourite food, chips, is yummy’. These marks help your reader understand exactly what you mean.

## Formal-informal register

In Year 5, we learn how to change our words to fit different situations, like talking to a teacher or playing with friends. We use a friendly voice with short sentences and contractions such as "can't" and "it’s" when chatting with mates. When writing to a headteacher, we use polite words like "please" and "kindly" and write full sentences without slang. We also choose the right words for our audience, like using "delicious" for a friend and "appetising" for a menu. This helps us talk and write clearly, whoever we are talking to.

## Poetry: figurative language

In poetry, we learn about special words called figurative language that make pictures in our heads. A simile compares two things using "like" or "as", such as "her hair is as shiny as the sun". A metaphor is when one thing is called something else, like "the classroom was a buzzing hive of activity". Personification gives human actions to objects, like "the wind whispered secrets in the trees". These tools help us make our writing more exciting and help us understand how poets create feelings and images.

## Narrative devices: flashback

Children learn that a flashback is a storytelling trick where we go back in time to see something that happened before the main story begins. It helps us understand why characters feel or act a certain way, like if they are sad because they miss a friend who moved away. Writers use words like "remember" or "earlier that day" to show when a flashback is happening. For example, a story might start with a child building a sandcastle, then suddenly we see a memory of their big brother helping them build one last year. Flashbacks make stories more interesting by telling us more about what has happened before.
