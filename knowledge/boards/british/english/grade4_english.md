# British Year 4 English - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Prefixes-suffixes: word building

Children learn that prefixes and suffixes are small word parts that change a word’s meaning. A prefix, like un- or dis-, goes at the start of a word to mean ‘not’, for example, ‘unhappy’ means not happy. A suffix, like -s, -ed, or -ful, goes at the end of a word to change the word type or add meaning, for example, ‘play’ becomes ‘played’ to show the action happened, or ‘help’ becomes ‘helpful’ to show a quality. By adding these parts, children can build new words and understand what they mean. This helps them read and spell longer words more easily.

## Comprehension: justify views

Children learn to explain what they think using words like “because” to give reasons. They find parts in a story that show why they like a character or think something happened. For example, they might say, “I think the wolf is cheeky because he blew the house down.” They use clues from the book to prove their ideas, like what a character did or said. They practice saying their ideas clearly and listening to others to understand different views.

## Pronouns-possession

In Year 4, you learn about special words called possessive pronouns that show who something belongs to, such as *mine*, *yours*, *his*, *hers*, *ours*, and *theirs*. For example, if you say, “This toy is mine,” you are telling everyone the toy belongs to you. These words replace names to make sentences shorter and easier, like when you say, “That bag is hers” instead of “That bag is Emma’s.” You also practise using *whose* to ask about ownership, such as “Whose coat is this?” to find out who something belongs to. By the end, you can use these words correctly to talk about things that belong to you and others.

## Verb inflections

In Year 4, children learn how verbs change to show different times, like yesterday, today, or tomorrow. They add ‘-ed’ to verbs for the past tense, for example, ‘jump’ becomes ‘jumped’ and ‘walk’ becomes ‘walked’. Sometimes, they change the spelling of the verb, like ‘run’ turning into ‘ran’ for the past tense. They also learn to add ‘-s’ to verbs when the action is happening now, like ‘he jumps’ or ‘she runs’. These small changes help us understand when actions take place.

## Punctuating direct speech

In Year 4, you will learn how to write down exactly what someone says by using speech marks. You place a pair of speech marks, like “ ”, around the spoken words to show they are being said by a character. You also need to start a new sentence after the speech marks and use a capital letter for the reporting verb, such as Sarah said. For example, you would write, “I love reading books,” said Tom. This helps the reader understand who is speaking. Finally, you will remember to use a comma before the closing speech mark to separate the spoken words from the rest of the sentence.

## Apostrophes: plural possession

At the end of a word that belongs to more than one person or thing, we add an apostrophe and an ‘s’ to show it is plural possession. For example, ‘the dogs’ collars’ means the collars that belong to the dogs. When a plural word already ends with an ‘s’, like ‘cats’ or ‘boys’, we just add an apostrophe after the ‘s’ to show what they own, such as ‘the cats’ toys’ or ‘the boys’ football’. This helps us understand who something belongs to when there are many people or things involved.

## Standard-Non-Standard English

In this topic, pupils learn that Standard English is the formal way we talk and write for school, like saying "I have a question," while Non-Standard English is the informal way we might chat with friends, such as "I got a question." They explore how to swap informal words for formal ones, like changing "gonna" to "going to," and how to use full sentences in writing, such as "She is playing football" instead of "She playing." By comparing the two, children understand when each is used, helping them speak and write clearly for different situations, like writing a story or chatting with a friend.

## Poetry: comparison

In this poetry comparison topic, children learn to spot similarities and differences between two poems by looking at themes, feelings, and language choices. They practice identifying how poets use devices like repetition or rhyme to create effects, such as making a sound or showing an emotion. For example, they might compare a poem about a storm with a calm nature poem, noticing how words like ‘roar’ and ‘howl’ make one loud, while ‘whisper’ and ‘giggle’ make the other gentle. By discussing these features, children build skills to explain which poem they like and why, using simple evidence from the text.

## Narrative: settings

In this topic, children learn that a setting is where a story happens, like a sunny beach or a dark castle. They explore how to describe a setting using their senses to make it feel real, such as hearing the waves or smelling the flowers. Pupils practise writing sentences that tell the reader if a place is big or small, hot or cold, and busy or quiet. They also understand that the setting can change as the story moves along, like leaving a sunny garden to enter a spooky forest. By choosing exciting words, children help their readers imagine the world they have created.

## Persuasive writing

Children learn to write convincing words to make others agree with them. They use special tricks like saying ‘should’, ‘must’, or ‘let’s’ to persuade people. A simple way to start is by writing a big idea, then giving three reasons why it is good, like “School trips should be longer because we see more things, learn lots, and have fun with friends.” They also learn to write a strong beginning and ending to make their point clear, using words like ‘I think’ and ‘please agree’.
