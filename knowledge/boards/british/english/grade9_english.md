# British Year 9 English - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Reading: unseen fiction analysis

In this topic, you will learn how to read and understand stories you have never seen before by looking for clues in the text. You will practise identifying characters’ feelings and motives, such as why a character might feel nervous before a big race, by spotting words that describe their actions. You will also explore how settings, like a dark and stormy forest, can create a mood or atmosphere in the story. Finally, you will learn to structure your answers by making clear points, such as "The writer makes the character seem brave by using strong verbs like 'charged' and 'dashed'," and then explaining how these words help you understand the story.

## Writing: imaginative-craft

In Year 9, you will learn how to write engaging imaginative stories by using exciting vocabulary and varied sentence structures to build tension and atmosphere. You will practice creating believable characters and detailed settings to make your readers feel part of the action. You will also explore different story openings, such as starting with dialogue or a mysterious description, to hook your audience from the beginning. By planning your plot with a clear beginning, middle, and end, you can ensure your story has a satisfying resolution. For example, you might describe a spooky forest using words like ‘gnarled’ and ‘shadowy’ to create a scary mood.

## Grammar: sophisticated punctuation

In Year 9, you learn to use more advanced punctuation like semicolons to join closely related independent clauses and colons to introduce a list or an explanation. You also practise correctly placing commas in complex sentences to separate clauses and avoid confusion. For instance, a semicolon can link two short, related sentences about the same idea, such as "The sun began to set; the sky turned a brilliant orange." Mastering these sophisticated marks makes your writing clearer and more professional.

## Vocabulary: ambitious choices

In Year 9, you will learn to choose ambitious vocabulary that makes your writing more exciting and precise. This means picking words that are more interesting than simple ones, like using 'elated' instead of 'happy' or 'gigantic' instead of 'big'. You will explore synonyms and decide which word best fits the context, avoiding boring or overused words. Understanding the subtle differences between similar words helps you express your ideas more clearly and effectively, making your work stand out. For example, choosing 'determined' instead of 'stubborn' can change how your reader feels about a character.

## Speaking: presentations-debate assessment

In this topic, you will learn how to plan and deliver a short presentation, using a clear structure like an introduction, main points with examples, and a conclusion. You will practice speaking clearly and confidently, making eye contact with your audience and using gestures to emphasise important points. During debates, you will learn to listen carefully to others and share your opinions politely, using phrases such as “I agree because…” or “I disagree and here’s why…” to support your arguments with reasons. You will also understand the importance of respecting different views and taking turns to speak fairly.

## Poetry: comparing two poems

In this topic, you learn how to compare two poems by looking at their similarities and differences. You explore the poet’s use of language, such as similes, metaphors, and personification, to see how they create effects like mood or meaning. For example, you might compare how both poets use nature to show feelings but use different words to do it. You also examine the poem’s structure, like how the number of lines or rhyming patterns affect the reader. Finally, you build your own answers by using evidence from the poems to support your thoughts.

## Shakespeare: extract-essay skills

You will learn how to write a strong essay about a short piece of Shakespeare’s work, like a speech or a scene. First, you must pick out the most important ideas from the extract, such as what the character is feeling or saying. Next, you will build your essay using a clear structure: an introduction with your main point, middle paragraphs that explain your ideas with examples from the text, and a conclusion that sums up what you have discovered. Finally, you will practice using key words to describe the language, like 'metaphor' or 'rhyme', to show you understand how Shakespeare uses words to create effects.

## Viewpoint writing: articles-speeches

In Year 9, you will learn to write articles and speeches that clearly share your opinion on a topic. You will plan your piece with a strong title to grab attention, an introduction that explains your viewpoint, and paragraphs with evidence to back it up, like a fact or an example. You must remember to write in a persuasive way using words such as ‘should’ or ‘must’, and to make your conclusion clear so readers understand your final thoughts. For example, you might write a speech to persuade your headteacher to have longer break times.

## Non-fiction: letter-report

In Year 9, you will learn how to write formal letters and reports using a clear structure. For letters, you must include your address and the date at the top, followed by the recipient’s details and a polite subject line like ‘Complaint about Playground Equipment’. Reports need a title, subheadings to organise information, and short paragraphs with facts, such as ‘Findings: The slide is broken’ and ‘Recommendation: It should be fixed for safety’. You will use formal language and make sure all information is accurate and easy to read.

## Exam-skills: timing-planning

In Year 9, you learn how to plan your time wisely for GCSE English exams. A key rule is to divide your exam time by the number of marks available for each question, spending more time on longer answers. You practise using a simple structure, like five minutes to jot down ideas and one minute to check your work at the end. For example, if you have a 30-minute question worth 12 marks, you might plan to write for 25 minutes and leave 5 minutes to review. This helps you answer every question fully without rushing.
