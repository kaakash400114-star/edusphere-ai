# British Year 10 English - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Reading: fiction-unseen-extract analysis

In this unit, you learn to analyse a short, unseen fiction extract by carefully reading the text and noticing the writer’s choices. You will identify key language devices, such as similes or personification, and explain how they help create effects like tension or characterisation, for example, a sentence describing a character’s hands as "like ice-cold claws" to show their fear. You also practise making inferences—using clues in the text to suggest what might happen next or how a character feels, even if it’s not directly stated. Finally, you structure your analysis by focusing on specific words, phrases, and structural features to build clear and organised responses.

## Writing: descriptive-narrative craft

In Year 10, you will learn to blend descriptive and narrative writing to create vivid stories. You will master ‘show, don’t tell’, using sensory details like the crunch of autumn leaves or the smell of baking bread to make scenes feel real. Your stories will follow a clear structure: an engaging opening to hook the reader, a build-up with rising action, a climax where tension peaks, and a resolution that ties up events. For example, instead of saying ‘she was scared’, you might describe ‘her hands shook as she heard the creak of the floorboard behind her’. By focusing on precise language and logical sequencing, your writing will become more immersive and compelling.

## Grammar: sophisticated control

In this topic, you learn to vary your sentence structures for a more sophisticated effect, including using subordinate clauses to add detail and complex sentences to combine ideas. You will practise correctly using punctuation like semicolons, colons, and dashes to link related ideas and create a fluent, mature style. Furthermore, you develop control over subject-verb agreement with tricky collective nouns and indefinite pronouns, ensuring your writing is grammatically precise. For example, you learn to write, “The team celebrated their victory” instead of the incorrect “The team celebrated its victory,” to show that multiple people were involved.

## Vocabulary: ambitious-precise

In Year 10, you will learn how to use ambitious and precise vocabulary to make your writing more interesting and clear. Ambitious words are more impressive than simple ones, such as using ‘determined’ instead of ‘wanting’ or ‘gigantic’ instead of ‘big’. Precise words are exact and specific, like using ‘whispered’ instead of ‘said’ or ‘fragile’ instead of ‘breakable’. You will practise choosing the best words to describe characters, settings, and feelings, ensuring your writing is vivid and effective. By expanding your vocabulary, you can avoid repeating words and make your work more engaging for the reader.

## Poetry: anthology-comparison essays

In this poetry topic, you learn to compare two poems from your anthology by thinking about their similarities and differences, focusing on language, structure, and feelings. You practise planning an essay using a clear structure: introduction, paragraphs comparing specific quotes, and a conclusion that sums up your thoughts. For example, you might compare how a poem about sadness uses dark words with one that uses bright images to show happiness. You also learn to use key words like 'imagery', 'tone', and 'stanza' to talk about the poems properly. This helps you write a strong answer that shows the examiner you understand both poems well.

## Shakespeare: whole-text essay skills

In this topic, you learn how to write a whole-text essay about a Shakespeare play by planning a clear structure with an introduction, three body paragraphs, and a conclusion. You will practise using the PEE paragraph method to explain your points, with evidence from the play such as a character’s speech or an important event, and then explain how that evidence proves your idea. You will also focus on analysing the writer’s craft, looking at how Shakespeare uses techniques like powerful language or dramatic tension to create effects on the audience. Finally, you will learn to end your essay by summarising your main ideas and thinking about the bigger picture of the play’s themes.

## Viewpoint writing: article-letter-speech

In this topic, you will learn how to write for different audiences and purposes using formal letters, articles, and speeches. Each format has its own rules: letters begin with your address and date, articles grab attention with a catchy headline, and speeches use persuasive language and rhetorical questions. You will practice organising your ideas with clear paragraphs and a strong conclusion to share your viewpoint effectively. For example, you might write a letter to your headteacher about school lunches or a speech on why recycling is important. The key is to tailor your writing style and structure to suit the task and audience.

## Non-fiction: 19th-21st century comparison

In this topic, you will learn to compare how non-fiction texts are structured and written across the 19th and 21st centuries, focusing on how authors present information and persuade their readers. You will explore features like formal 19th-century reports and modern blogs, noting differences in language and layout. You will also analyse how authors use facts, opinions, and persuasive devices to influence the audience. For example, a 19th-century letter about a new invention might use very formal language, while a modern online review would use more direct and informal words. The goal is to understand how these techniques have changed over time.

## Speaking-listening: endorsed presentation

In the endorsed presentation unit, you learn to plan and deliver a clear talk to your classmates, using formal language and keeping to a time limit. You must structure your speech with an engaging introduction, well-organised main points supported by facts or examples, and a strong conclusion to summarise your ideas. You will also practise active listening by asking relevant questions and giving constructive feedback to others, showing respect for different viewpoints. For example, you might present a project on your favourite book or a current issue, ensuring your voice is clear and your eye contact helps you connect with the audience. This skill helps you build confidence in sharing ideas clearly and listening carefully to others.

## Exam-skills: AO mapping-timing

In this topic, you learn how to match your answers to the Assessment Objectives (AOs) by carefully reading the question instructions, which tell you exactly what exam markers are looking for, like summarising information (AO1) or analysing language (AO2). You practice planning your time by dividing the total exam time by the number of questions, leaving a few minutes at the end to check your work, so you don’t rush or leave anything blank. For example, if you have 30 minutes for a question that asks you to 'compare' two texts, you might spend 5 minutes planning, 20 minutes writing, and 5 minutes reviewing to ensure you’ve covered both similarities and differences as required. This helps you stay organised and show the examiner you can follow instructions precisely.
