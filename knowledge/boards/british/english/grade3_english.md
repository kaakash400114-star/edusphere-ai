# British Year 3 English - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Word families-prefixes

In English, we learn about prefixes, which are little letters we add to the beginning of words to change their meaning. A prefix like ‘un-’ makes a word mean the opposite, so ‘happy’ becomes ‘unhappy’. Another prefix is ‘re-’, which means ‘again’, so we can ‘read’ a story ‘again’ to enjoy it for a second time. Understanding prefixes helps us become better readers and writers, as we can work out the meaning of new words by looking at the part added at the start. By knowing these small word parts, we build our confidence and skills in language.

## Comprehension: inference

In Year 3, you learn to be a detective by using clues to work out what is happening in a story. This means reading the words and thinking about what the character might be feeling or doing next. For example, if a character looks very happy and is running fast, you can infer they might be going to a party. You also practise making predictions by thinking about what might happen based on what you have already read. This helps you understand the story better and become a super reader.

## Nouns-pronouns-adjectives

Children in Year 3 learn to understand the difference between nouns, pronouns, and adjectives. A noun is a naming word for a person, place, thing, or idea, like 'teacher', 'playground', 'book', or 'happiness'. A pronoun is a small word that takes the place of a noun to avoid repetition, such as 'he', 'she', 'it', or 'they'. An adjective is a describing word that tells us more about a noun, for example, a 'big' dog, a 'blue' kite, or a 'happy' smile. By practising with these words, children can build clearer sentences.

## Verbs: past-present-perfect

In Year 3, you learn about verbs that show when things happen, like in the past, now, or before now. For the past, we often add '-ed' to a verb, such as 'walk' becoming 'walked' to show it is finished. The present tense shows something happening right now, like 'I jump' or 'she sings'. The perfect past tense uses 'have' or 'has' with a special verb, like 'I have jumped' to show an action is completed. These help us tell exciting stories about things that happened, what we are doing, and what we have already done.

## Direct speech punctuation

In Year 3, children learn to write down exactly what someone says using special punctuation called speech marks or inverted commas. These little commas go before and after the spoken words, like putting a frame around the sound. They also need to use a capital letter at the start of the speech and a full stop or question mark at the end, just like a regular sentence. For example, if they want to write that Leo said hello, it would look like this: Leo said, “Hello.” This helps readers know who is speaking and what they said.

## Fronted adverbials + comma

In Year 3, we learn to use fronted adverbials to start our sentences with more detail. A fronted adverbial is a word or phrase that comes before the main clause to describe when, where, how, or how often something happens. We always use a comma after the fronted adverbial to separate it from the rest of the sentence. For example, we might write, “Yesterday, I played football in the park,” or “Quietly, the tiptoeing mouse explored the house.” This helps make our writing clearer and more interesting for readers.

## Paragraphs

A paragraph is a group of sentences about the same idea. Sentences in a paragraph work together to explain one thing clearly. Children learn to start each new paragraph with a little indent, a small space at the beginning. This helps readers know when a new idea starts. For example, a paragraph about your pet cat will have sentences all about playing and purring, while a new paragraph can be about feeding the cat.

## Poetry: imagery

In poetry, we learn about imagery, which means using words to create pictures in our mind. We look at how poets use describing words to show what things look, sound, feel, or smell like, such as “the golden sun” for sight or “the buzzing bee” for sound. We also find out that imagery helps us feel happy, sad, or excited when we read a poem, like feeling calm when we imagine “the soft, white snow.” By spotting these pictures, we can better understand what the poem is about and how the writer wants us to feel.

## Fiction: dialogue

In Year 3, children learn to write dialogue using speech marks to show exactly what a character says, like “Can I play too?” They also use a new line when a different character speaks to make their writing clear and easy to read. Children discover how to add action words, such as whispered or shouted, to make the speech more exciting. Punctuation like commas, exclamation marks, and question marks helps to show how a character is feeling, for example, “Watch out!” shouted Ben. By practising these skills, pupils can write stories that sound like real conversations.

## Reports-instructions

In Year 3, you will learn to write simple reports and instructions. A report gives information about something real, like an animal or a place, and starts with a title. Instructions tell someone how to do something in the right order, using words like ‘first’ and ‘next’, for example, how to make a sandwich. You must use clear sentences and start each instruction on a new line. Good reports and instructions are easy for others to read and follow.
