# British Year 8 English - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Reading: 19th-century texts intro

In this topic, you will learn how to explore old stories and poems from the 1800s. You will look at the language used long ago and find words that sound different today, like 'thee' instead of 'you'. You will also find out how writers made their stories exciting by using lots of detail and making characters face big problems, for example, a character trying to survive on a desert island. By learning these simple rules, you will be able to understand what happens in the story and how the writer made it so interesting.

## Analytical paragraphs: PEEL

In Year 8, you learn to build strong analytical paragraphs using the PEEL structure: Point, Evidence, Explanation, and Link. Your Point is the main idea for that single paragraph, like "The character feels lonely". You must back this up with a piece of Evidence from the text, such as a short quotation or specific detail. The Explanation is where you write about how the evidence proves your point, explaining its meaning. Finally, a Link sentence connects your paragraph back to the bigger question you are answering.

## Grammar: clauses-tense consistency

In this topic, you learn to keep your verb tenses the same so your writing is clear and easy to follow. If you start a story in the past tense, like "The dog chased the ball," you must keep all the other verbs in the past tense, too, for example, "The dog barked as it ran away." You also learn to spot when you mix tenses and change them to make sense, such as changing "My brother eats cake yesterday" to "My brother ate cake yesterday" because the event is finished. This helps your reader understand exactly when things happened.

## Vocabulary: advanced-spelling strategies

In this topic, you will learn spelling strategies to help you tackle tricky words. You will explore prefixes and suffixes, such as ‘un-‘ and ‘-ing’, and how they change a root word like ‘happy’ to ‘unhappy’ or ‘happening’. You will also learn about homophones, words that sound the same but have different meanings and spellings, like ‘their’, ‘there’, and ‘they’re’, so you can choose the right one in your writing. Another important skill is using mnemonics, such as “big elephants can always understand small elephants” to remember how to spell ‘because’. These strategies will make you a more confident and accurate speller.

## Speaking: formal presentations

In Year 8, you will learn how to give a formal presentation using a clear structure, such as an introduction to grab the audience’s attention, middle paragraphs with main points and evidence, and a conclusion that summarises what you have said. You will practise speaking clearly and at a steady pace, making eye contact with your audience and using formal language, for example, by saying "Good morning, everyone" instead of "Hi, mates". You will also learn how to use visual aids, like posters or slides, to help explain your ideas and make your presentation more interesting for others to listen to.

## Poetry: anthology-style analysis

In this poetry unit, you will learn how to compare two different poems by finding similarities and differences in their language, mood, and themes. You will study the features of poetic devices such as similes, metaphors, and personification, and explain how they create effects for the reader. For example, you might compare the structure of a sonnet to a free verse poem to see how the form changes the way the message is delivered. You will also practise finding evidence in the text to support your ideas about what the poet is trying to say.

## Shakespeare: language-stagecraft

In Year 8, you will learn how Shakespeare used special techniques to make his plays exciting. You will explore iambic pentameter, a bouncy rhythm like a heartbeat, and identify how he created rhyming couplets to signal important moments. You will also discover how characters speak in soliloquies, sharing their thoughts directly with the audience, as when Macbeth debates his dark plans. Finally, you will analyse how Shakespeare built tension through dramatic irony, where you, the audience, know more about a situation than the characters on stage.

## Writing: viewpoints-persuasion

In English, you will learn to write persuasively by sharing your own opinions with strong reasons and examples. You must structure your work with a clear introduction stating your viewpoint, then paragraphs that each explain a different reason, followed by a conclusion that reminds the reader of your main point. For example, you might argue for a longer lunch break, giving reasons like having more time to eat properly and talk with friends. You will use powerful words like "brilliant" and "terrible" to make your feelings clear. The goal is to convince your reader that your opinion is the right one.

## Non-fiction: article-speech craft

In Year 8, you learn to build your own non-fiction articles and speeches by using a clear structure, starting with a catchy title to grab your reader’s or audience’s attention. You will explore how to organise your ideas into paragraphs, each with a main point and facts or examples to back it up, like explaining how to look after a pet with a section on feeding and another on exercise. You also discover the power of persuasive language, learning to use words that make people feel excited or agree with you, such as describing a park as "amazing" instead of just "nice". Finally, you practise editing your work to make sure your sentences are clear and easy to understand, so your message comes across strongly.

## Research: credible sources

In Year 8, you will learn how to find trustworthy information for your research by checking if a source is credible. This means looking for websites from experts, like museums or universities, and checking who wrote the information and when it was published. For example, a book by a famous scientist or a news report from a well-known newspaper are often reliable. You will also learn to be careful with sources like social media or personal blogs, which might not always be true or fair. Your teacher will show you how to use these skills to write better projects and make sure your work is accurate.
