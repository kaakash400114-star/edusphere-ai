# British Year 2 Mathematics - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Place value to 100

Children learn to understand the value of each digit in a two-digit number, recognising that the first digit represents tens and the second represents ones. For example, in the number 54, the digit 5 stands for 5 tens (50), and the digit 4 stands for 4 ones (4). They use this knowledge to partition numbers into tens and ones, such as breaking down 72 into 70 and 2. This helps them to compare and order numbers up to 100, understanding that 36 is less than 53 because 3 tens are fewer than 5 tens. By using practical equipment like Numicon or straws, children build a secure foundation in place value.

## Addition-subtraction two 2-digit

In this topic, learners explore adding and subtracting two 2-digit numbers, focusing on column methods to keep calculations neat and accurate. They start by writing numbers in tens and ones columns, adding or subtracting from right to left, remembering to exchange 10 ones for 1 ten when adding, or regroup 1 ten for 10 ones when subtracting. For example, 34 + 27 is set out with 4 + 7 = 11 (write 1, carry over 1 ten) and 3 + 2 + 1 = 6, giving 41. Similarly, 58 – 23 is solved by 8 – 3 = 5 and 5 – 2 = 3, resulting in 35. The aim is to build confidence in handling two-step calculations accurately.

## Multiplication tables 2-5-10

In this topic, children learn to recall and use multiplication facts for the 2, 5, and 10 times tables, understanding that multiplication is repeated addition. They explore patterns, such as numbers ending in 0 or 5 in the 5 and 10 tables, and even numbers in the 2 table, to help with memorisation. For example, they know that 2 × 7 = 14, 5 × 3 = 15, and 10 × 4 = 40, and can use these facts to solve simple word problems involving equal groups or arrays. By the end, they should be able to quickly recall these tables to support mental calculations and future maths learning.

## Division as sharing

Children learn to divide by sharing a total amount into equal groups. They start with simple problems like sharing 10 sweets between 2 friends to find each gets 5. They use objects or drawings to make equal groups and count how many are in each. The division sentence shows the total amount shared and how many groups are made, for example, 10 ÷ 2 = 5. This helps them understand division as splitting fairly and finding the value in each part.

## Fractions: 1/2-1/4-1/3

In Year 2, you learn to recognise and find fractions like 1/2, 1/4 and 1/3 of shapes, objects, lengths and quantities. You understand that a fraction represents equal parts of a whole, for example, 1/2 of a pizza is one slice out of two equal slices. You practise dividing objects into halves and quarters, such as folding a paper square into four equal parts to find 1/4. You also find simple fractions of small sets, like finding 1/3 of six counters by sharing them into three equal groups. This helps you see how fractions work in everyday situations.

## Money: making amounts-change

In this topic, children learn to make different amounts of money using coins and notes, combining values to reach a total. They practice adding coins, such as 20p, 10p, and 5p, to make 35p, and find change by subtracting the cost from the amount paid, like giving 50p for a 30p item and getting 20p back. Pupils understand how to use pounds and pence (£ and p) correctly, recognising that 100p equals £1. They solve simple real-life problems, such as buying two items costing 45p each and paying with £1 to calculate the change. This builds confidence in handling money and understanding basic arithmetic.

## Measurement: cm-m-kg-ml

In this unit, children learn to measure length using centimetres (cm) and metres (m), understanding that 100 cm make 1 m. They measure weight in kilograms (kg), using scales to find how heavy items are, and capacity in millilitres (ml), pouring liquids to see how much a container holds. Pupils practise choosing the right unit for a job, like using m for a playground and ml for a drink bottle. They compare objects using these measurements, such as finding which book is longer or which jug holds more. By linking numbers to real-world objects, children build a strong foundation for understanding measurement.

## Time: quarter past-to

In this topic, children learn to tell the time using analogue clocks, focusing on ‘quarter past’ the hour. When the big hand points to the 3, it means it is a quarter past the hour, like 2:15. They practise reading times such as quarter past 1, quarter past 4, and quarter past 7. This helps them understand that a quarter is one of the four equal parts of an hour. By recognising this position, they can confidently identify the time for everyday activities.

## Shapes: properties-symmetry

In this topic, children learn to identify and describe 2D and 3D shapes by their properties, such as the number of sides, corners, and faces. They explore symmetry by recognising and drawing lines of symmetry in simple shapes, like squares and circles, which divide the shape into two matching halves. Pupils also use the language of position, direction, and motion to describe how shapes fit together and can be turned or flipped. For example, they might identify that a rectangle has two lines of symmetry or that a triangle may have one or none. This work helps them understand how shapes are organised and related in the world around them.

## Statistics: tally-blocks

In Class 2, you learn to count and record information using tally blocks. Each tally mark represents one item, and every fifth mark is drawn across the first four to make a group of five, which is easier to count. For example, if you see seven apples, you draw four vertical lines and one diagonal line across them to show five, then two more vertical lines for the extra two. Tally blocks help you organise numbers when you are counting things like favourite colours or animals in a survey. This makes it simple to see which group has the most or the least quickly.
