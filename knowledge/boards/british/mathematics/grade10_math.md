# British Year 10 Mathematics - National Curriculum/GCSE

## GCSE: number-recap-indices-surds

In this topic, you will recap and extend your knowledge of indices (or powers), learning rules like multiplying powers by adding their numbers (for example, \( 2^3 \times 2^4 = 2^{7} \)) and dividing powers by subtracting (such as \( 5^6 \div 5^2 = 5^4 \)). You will also simplify surds, which are numbers with square roots that cannot be simplified further, like \( \sqrt{3} \) or \( \sqrt{7} \). Additionally, you will learn to rationalise denominators, such as turning \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \) into \( \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \), and use index laws with negative and fractional powers, including working out \( 16^{\frac{1}{2}} \) which equals 4. These skills build a strong foundation for more complex algebra and problem-solving in mathematics.

## Algebra: quadratics-simultaneous-inequalities

In this topic, you will learn how to solve quadratic equations by factorising and using the quadratic formula, such as finding the solutions to \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0\). You will also explore simultaneous equations with one linear and one quadratic equation, like solving \(y = x^2\) and \(y = 2x + 3\) by substitution. Additionally, you will work with quadratic inequalities, such as \(x^2 - 4x + 3 > 0\), by sketching graphs to find the values of \(x\) that satisfy the inequality. These skills help you understand how algebra connects to graphs and real-world problems.

## Sequences-functions-graphs

In this topic, you will learn to spot and describe number patterns, such as sequences where numbers increase by the same amount each time, like 4, 7, 10, 13. You will also explore functions, which are rules that link one number to another, for example, a rule that says "add 3 to the input number to get the output". You will practise plotting these relationships on graphs, using coordinates to draw straight lines and see how rules can be shown visually. For instance, a simple function like y = x + 2 would be plotted as a line going upwards on a grid. By the end, you will be able to connect rules, numbers, and pictures together.

## Ratio-proportion-growth-decay

You will learn how to share amounts using ratios, like splitting £20 between two people in a 1:2 ratio. Ratios show how numbers compare, and you can simplify them, just like fractions. For example, a 3:6 ratio simplifies to 1:2. Proportion means when two ratios are equal, so if 2 apples cost £1, then 6 apples cost £3. You will also explore growth and decay, which is how numbers change over time, such as money increasing in a savings account or populations decreasing. To work this out, you use formulas to find the new amount after a certain percentage change.

## Geometry: pythagoras-trigonometry

In this topic, you will learn to use Pythagoras’ theorem to find missing sides in right-angled triangles, remembering that a² + b² = c² where c is the longest side. You will also explore trigonometric ratios—sine, cosine, and tangent—to calculate unknown angles and sides, using formulas like SOH CAH TOA. For example, if you know an angle and one side, you can find another side using tan = opposite/adjacent. These skills help solve real-life problems, such as finding the height of a tree or the distance across a river. You will practise applying these rules to both simple and multi-step problems to build confidence.

## Circle theorems

In this topic, you will learn about circle theorems and the special rules they follow. You will discover that the angle at the centre of a circle is twice the angle at the circumference when both angles are standing on the same arc. Another rule is that angles in the same segment of a circle are equal to each other. For example, if two angles are both on the circumference and looking at the same part of the circle, they will have the same size. You will also learn that the angle in a semicircle is always a right angle, which means it is 90 degrees.

## Probability-trees-venn-diagrams

In this topic, you learn to use probability trees to show all possible outcomes of an event, like flipping a coin twice, and multiply probabilities along the branches to find the chance of something happening. Venn diagrams help you sort information into groups using circles, showing how things overlap, such as pupils who like football and those who like cricket. You also practise using the rules of probability, including that all probabilities add up to one and that the probability of something not happening is one minus the probability of it happening. For example, if the chance of rain is 0.3, the chance of no rain is 0.7. These tools make it easier to understand and solve problems with different events.

## Statistics-sampling-distributions

In this topic, you will learn about different ways to collect data, like asking every person in a group (a census) or questioning a smaller number of people (a sample). You will explore how to pick a fair sample, for example by using random numbers to choose students from a school list to avoid bias. You will also understand that different samples can give different results, which is why we use the mean and range to describe how spread out the data is. For instance, if you measure the heights of five friends, their average height might be different from the average height of the whole class. Finally, you will learn to make predictions about a whole group using the information from a sample.

## Area-volume-cones-spheres-frustums

In this topic, you will learn how to find the area and volume of cones, spheres, and frustums. For a cone, you use the formula \( \frac{1}{3} \pi r^2 h \) to calculate its volume, while a sphere’s volume is \( \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 \). A frustum is a cone with the top cut off, and its volume can be found by subtracting the smaller cone’s volume from the larger one. You will also practise calculating the surface area of spheres and curved surfaces of cones, using formulas like \( 4 \pi r^2 \) for a sphere. These skills help solve real-life problems, such as finding the amount of material needed to make a tent or the volume of a toy ball.

## Trigonometric graphs-identities intro

In this topic, you will learn to recognise and sketch the three main trigonometric graphs—sine, cosine, and cosine—which repeat in a regular wave-like pattern called a cycle. You will discover that the sine graph starts at zero, reaches a maximum of 1, and returns to zero, while the cosine graph starts at its maximum value of 1. You will also explore how the graphs can be shifted up and down or stretched by changing numbers in their equations, such as in *y = sin(x)* and *y = 2cos(x)*. Understanding these patterns helps you solve problems involving heights, distances, and repeating events, like tides or swings.
