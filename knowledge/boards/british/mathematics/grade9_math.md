# British Year 9 Mathematics - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Number: surds-indices-reciprocal

In this topic, you learn about special numbers called surds, which are square roots that can’t be simplified, like √2. You also explore indices, which are small numbers used to show how many times a number is multiplied by itself, for example, 3² means 3 × 3 = 9. Another key concept is the reciprocal, which is the opposite of a number found by flipping it upside down, so the reciprocal of 5 is 1/5. You will practice multiplying and dividing indices using simple rules, such as adding them when multiplying powers of the same number, like 2³ × 2² = 2⁵. These skills help you work with numbers in different ways and solve problems more easily.

## Algebra: factorising-quadratics-inequalities

In algebra, you learn to factorise quadratic expressions like x² + 5x + 6 by finding two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to 5, giving you (x + 2)(x + 3). You also solve quadratic equations by setting expressions to zero, such as x² + 5x + 6 = 0, and solving for x. When dealing with inequalities, you follow similar rules to equations but remember to flip the inequality sign if you multiply or divide by a negative number, like in -2x > 6 becoming x < -3. These skills help you solve problems and understand how numbers relate to each other in different ways.

## Linear graphs-real contexts

In Year 9, you learn to draw and understand straight-line graphs that show real-life situations, like how the cost of a taxi ride changes with distance. You use the equation y = mx + c to find the steepness (gradient, m) and where the line crosses the y-axis (intercept, c). For example, if a phone plan costs £5 plus 10p per minute, the graph’s y-intercept is 5 and the gradient is 0.1. You also plot graphs from information in tables, showing how two things are connected, such as the number of hours worked and money earned. This helps you spot patterns and make predictions using straight lines.

## Geometry: trigonometry intro-pythagoras

In this topic, you learn about right-angled triangles and how to find missing lengths and angles using Pythagoras’ theorem and trigonometric ratios. Pythagoras’ theorem states that for a right-angled triangle, the square of the longest side (hypotenuse) equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides: \( a^2 + b^2 = c^2 \). You also explore sine, cosine, and tangent (SOH CAH TOA) to calculate sides or angles, such as finding the height of a ladder leaning against a wall. These methods help solve real-life problems, like measuring distances or heights, by understanding the relationships within triangles. Mastering these skills prepares you for more advanced geometry and problem-solving at GCSE level.

## Ratio-proportion compound measures

In this topic, you learn about ratios, which compare amounts using numbers like 2:3 to show how things share proportionally. You also explore compound measures, which combine different units, such as speed (measured in miles per hour) and density (grams per cubic centimetre). You will practise solving problems by scaling ratios up or down, for example, doubling a recipe that serves 4 people to serve 8. You will also use formulas like distance = speed × time to find missing values, making sure units match correctly to get the right answer.

## Probability: trees-venn

In Year 9, you learn to use probability trees to show all possible outcomes when things happen one after the other, like flipping a coin then picking a card. You multiply the probabilities along the branches to find the chance of two events happening together, for example, getting heads and then a king. You also compare this with Venn diagrams, which use circles to show how different groups of things overlap, helping you spot what things have in common or are unique. A key rule is that all the probabilities on a tree diagram or within a Venn diagram must add up to one. Finally, you practise deciding whether to use a tree diagram or a Venn diagram to solve different probability problems.

## Statistics: sampling-histograms intro

In Year 9, you will learn that a sample is a small group chosen from a larger population to help us understand the whole. You will explore how to draw fair samples, making sure everyone in the population has a chance of being picked, like choosing names from a hat. You will also be introduced to histograms, which are special bar charts used to show data in groups, or classes, where the bars touch each other to show continuous data, like the heights of pupils in your class. When drawing histograms, the area of each bar represents the frequency, so you might use wider bars for groups with a larger range of data. This helps you spot patterns, such as which group is the most common or how the data is spread out.

## Angles: circle theorems intro

In Year 9, you will learn about special angles inside circles, like the angle at the centre and the angle on the circumference. You will discover that when two angles stand on the same arc, the angle at the centre is always twice as big as the angle on the circumference. Another key rule is that the angle in a semicircle is always a right angle, which is 90 degrees. For example, if a triangle is drawn inside a circle with its longest side as the diameter, the angle opposite that side will be 90 degrees. These circle theorems help you solve problems involving points, lines, and shapes within circles.

## Area-surface-volume: cones-spheres

In Year 9, you will learn how to find the surface area and volume of cones and spheres. For a cone, the volume is one-third of the area of the circular base times the height, and the surface area includes the curved part. For a sphere, you use the formula for volume (four-thirds of pi times the radius cubed) and surface area (four times pi times the radius squared). You will practise using these formulas to solve problems, such as finding the amount of space inside a toy cone or the material needed to cover a ball. Remember to use the correct units, like centimetres cubed for volume and square centimetres for area.

## Equations: algebraic fractions intro

In this topic, you will learn how to solve equations that contain algebraic fractions, which are fractions with letters in them. The main rule is to find a common denominator to combine the fractions, just like with regular numbers, so you can simplify the equation. For example, in an equation like \( \frac{x}{2} + \frac{1}{3} = 1 \), you would multiply each term by 6 (the common denominator) to eliminate the fractions. Once the fractions are cleared, you can solve for \( x \) using the same methods you already know. It’s all about making the equation simpler step by step.
