# British Year 5 Mathematics - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Place value to 1,000,000

In this topic, children learn about place value with numbers up to one million, understanding that each digit has a special value depending on where it sits. They explore how numbers are made of ones, tens, hundreds, thousands, ten thousands, hundred thousands, and millions, using a place value chart to see this clearly. For example, the number 456,782 has 4 hundred thousands, 5 ten thousands, 6 thousands, 7 hundreds, 8 tens, and 2 ones. They also practice reading and writing large numbers in both words and digits, making sure they know the order of the place values from biggest to smallest. By breaking down numbers like 203,450, they see that zero acts as a placeholder, showing there are no thousands in that number.

## Column methods large numbers

In Year 5, you learn to add and subtract big numbers using the column method, writing numbers carefully in ones, tens, hundreds, and thousands columns. When adding, you start from the ones column, adding the numbers together and exchanging ten ones for one ten if you need to. For subtracting, you also begin with the ones column and exchange a ten for ten ones if a number is too small to take away. For example, when adding 456 and 278, you add 6 and 8 to make 14, writing down 4 and exchanging 1 to the tens column. This helps you solve sums with large numbers accurately and neatly.

## Multiplication: 4-digit × 2-digit

In Year 5, children learn how to multiply a 4-digit number by a 2-digit number using a written method, starting by breaking the smaller number into tens and units. They first multiply the 4-digit number by the units, then multiply it by the tens and write this result one place to the left, before adding the two parts together. For example, to calculate 3452 × 24, they would first work out 3452 × 4 and then 3452 × 20, adding the two answers to get the final product. This method helps them understand place value and how to handle larger numbers step by step.

## Short division-fractions as division

In Year 5, you learn that fractions can show sharing or division, like ½ means one lot of two things shared equally. When you see a fraction such as ¾, you can think of it as 3 divided by 4, helping you solve problems like sharing 3 pizzas between 4 friends. This makes big numbers easier to handle by breaking them into smaller, equal parts. For example, 6 shared into 3 equal parts is the same as the fraction ⅖, where you find how many are in each bit. Practising this helps you understand how division and fractions are connected.

## Fractions: unlike denominators

When working with fractions that have different denominators, we learn to find a common denominator so we can add or subtract them easily. To do this, we look for the smallest number that both denominators can divide into, which we call the lowest common multiple (LCM). For example, to add ⅓ and ¼, we find the LCM of 3 and 4, which is 12, and then change both fractions to have 12 as the denominator: ⅓ becomes ⁴⁄₁₂ and ¼ becomes ³⁄₁₂. We can then add them together: ⁴⁄₁₂ + ³⁄₁₂ = ⁷⁄₁₂. This helps us work with fractions that are not the same size but still find the correct answer.

## Decimals-percentages-fractions links

In Year 5, you learn that fractions, decimals, and percentages are all different ways to show parts of a whole. One half is the same as 0.5 or 50%, and one quarter is the same as 0.25 or 25%. You will find equivalent fractions, like two fifths and four tenths, which can both be written as 0.4 or 40%. You also practise converting between them, such as turning three quarters into 0.75 and 75%. Understanding these links helps you solve problems involving sharing and measuring in everyday life.

## Negative numbers

In Year 5, you learn about negative numbers, which are numbers less than zero. You find them on a number line, with zero in the middle and negative numbers going to the left, like -1, -2, and -3. You learn to count forwards and backwards through zero, so from 2 down to zero and then to -1. When you add a negative number, it’s like taking away, so 5 + (-2) is the same as 5 - 2, which is 3. You also learn to compare negative numbers, and you know that -3 is colder than -1 because it’s further from zero.

## Measure conversions

In Year 5, you learn about changing one unit of measure into another, like turning centimetres into metres or grams into kilograms. You find out that there are 100 centimetres in one metre, so to change centimetres to metres, you chop off the last two digits, like turning 150cm into 1.50m. For kilograms, you also use 100, so 2,500 grams is the same as 2.5 kilograms because you move the decimal point two places to the left. You also practise with litres and millilitres, knowing that 1,000 millilitres make 1 litre, so 3,000ml is equal to 3 litres. These help you measure things accurately in real life, like knowing how tall you are or how much water is in a bottle.

## Perimeter-area of compound shapes

In this topic, children learn to find the perimeter and area of shapes made by putting simpler shapes together, like a rectangle with a square attached. To find the perimeter, they add up all the outer sides, being careful not to count any hidden lines inside the shape. For area, they split the compound shape into smaller rectangles or squares, find the area of each part (length × width), and then add them together. For example, an L-shape might be split into two rectangles, and their areas are added to find the total. They also practise measuring sides carefully to make sure their answers are correct.

## Volume of cuboids

In Year 5, you learn about the volume of cuboids, which is the space inside a 3D shape like a shoebox or a brick. To find the volume, you multiply the length, the width, and the height together. For example, if a cuboid is 5 cm long, 3 cm wide, and 2 cm tall, you calculate 5 × 3 × 2 to get a volume of 30 cubic centimetres. This means the cuboid can hold 30 little cubes that are each 1 cm long, 1 cm wide, and 1 cm tall. You use this formula to measure how much space different cuboids take up in real life.

## Angle rules: straight-around a point

When learning about angles, we find that angles on a straight line add up to 180 degrees, like two pieces of a biscuit making a whole line. Around a point, all the angles join together to make a full turn, which is 360 degrees, just like a circle. If one angle on a straight line is 110 degrees, we can find the other by taking it away from 180, so it must be 70 degrees. These rules help us solve problems by adding or subtracting angles to find missing ones, making maths puzzles easier to solve.

## Statistics: line graphs

In this topic, children learn to read and draw simple line graphs to show how things change over time, like temperature or the height of a plant. They practise labelling the axes with numbers and words, such as "days of the week" or "height in centimetres", and plotting points accurately. They also learn to join the points with straight lines to see patterns, such as whether something increases or decreases. For example, a line graph might show how many books are read each week in class, helping everyone see the trend clearly.
