# British Year 6 Mathematics - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Place value to 10,000,000

In this topic, children learn to read, write, and understand numbers up to 10 million, recognising the value of each digit in a number, such as 3,456,789 where the 3 is in the millions place and the 5 is in the hundred thousands place. They practise partitioning numbers into parts, for example, 2,100,000 can be split into 2,000,000 + 100,000, to show the value of each digit. Children also learn to order and compare numbers, knowing that a number with more millions is larger, like comparing 7,000,000 and 5,000,000. Using a place value chart helps them see the value of digits clearly, making it easier to say and write large numbers correctly.

## Four operations mastery

In Year 6, you will become confident using all four operations to solve problems. You will learn to use efficient methods for addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division, including using column for bigger numbers. This means being able to quickly and accurately add two three-digit numbers, multiply two-digit numbers by a one-digit number, and divide with remainders. For example, you will calculate 478 + 356 and 34 x 7. You will also use these skills to solve one-step and two-step word problems, choosing the correct operation to find the answer.

## Common factors-multiples-primes

In Year 6, we learn to find common factors and multiples, which are numbers that divide into or can be made from other numbers. We find factors by looking for numbers that divide into a given number without a leftover, like the factors of 12 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 12. Multiples are what you get when you count up in a certain number, such as the multiples of 5 are 5, 10, 15, 20, and so on. We also identify prime numbers, which are special numbers only divisible by 1 and themselves, like 2, 3, 5, and 7. We use these skills to solve problems and make working with numbers easier.

## Order of operations

In Year 6, you learn how to solve number sums in the right order using the rule: Brackets first, then Orders (like squared or cubed numbers), then Multiplication and Division (from left to right), and finally Addition and Subtraction (from left to right). This helps you get the correct answer every time. For example, in the sum 3 + 4 × 2, you must do the multiplication (4 × 2 = 8) before the addition (3 + 8 = 11). If the sum has brackets, like (3 + 4) × 2, you do the part inside the brackets first (3 + 4 = 7), then multiply (7 × 2 = 14). These steps are very important for solving maths problems correctly.

## Fractions: all four operations

In Year 6, you will learn how to add, subtract, multiply, and divide fractions. To add or subtract fractions, they must have the same denominator, like adding ⅓ and ⅓ to make ⅔. When multiplying, you multiply the top numbers and the bottom numbers, such as ¼ × ½ = ⅛. For dividing, you turn the second fraction upside down and multiply, so dividing ½ by ¼ is the same as ½ × 4, which equals 2. You will also find fractions of amounts, like ⅓ of 9, by dividing the number by the bottom number and multiplying by the top.

## Decimals-percentages conversion

In this topic, you learn how to change numbers between decimals and percentages. To turn a decimal into a percentage, you multiply it by 100 and add the % sign, so 0.25 becomes 25%. To turn a percentage into a decimal, you divide it by 100, so 50% becomes 0.50. You also see that percentages and decimals both show parts of a whole, like 0.75 or 75% being three-quarters of a pizza. Practising this helps you understand money, discounts, and sharing things fairly.

## Ratio-proportion

In Year 6, children learn about ratio to compare quantities, like saying there are 3 apples for every 2 oranges in a fruit bowl. They discover that ratios can be written in different ways using a colon, such as 3:2, and can be simplified by dividing both numbers by the same amount, just like fractions. Pupils use their times tables to find equivalent ratios, for example, 1:2 is the same as 3:6 if you multiply both numbers by 3. They also solve simple problems sharing items in a given ratio, such as splitting 10 sweets between two friends in a 1:1 ratio so each gets 5. This helps them understand how to make fair comparisons and share things equally.

## Algebra: sequences-expressions-equations

In Year 6, children learn to spot patterns in number sequences and continue them by finding the rule. They practise writing simple rules using letters called variables, for example, “n + 3” to add 3 to any number. Pupils also write short maths sentences called expressions, like “2 × 7” or “10 − c”, to show a calculation with an unknown number. They solve simple one-step equations by working out the missing number, such as finding the answer to “a + 4 = 10” by subtracting 4. These skills help children make sense of problems with unknown values.

## Measure: converting units

In this topic, you learn how to change units of measurement, like turning centimetres into metres or grams into kilograms. You discover that there are 100 centimetres in one metre, so if something is 150 cm long, it is also 1.5 m long. For weight, you find out that 1,000 grams make one kilogram, so a bag of flour that weighs 2 kg is the same as 2,000 g. You also practise converting between litres and millilitres, knowing that 1 litre equals 1,000 millilitres, so a big bottle of juice might be 2 L or 2,000 ml. These conversions help you understand and compare measurements in everyday life.

## Perimeter-area-volume

In Class 6, you will learn to find the perimeter, area, and volume of shapes. Perimeter is the total length around a shape, like adding up the sides of a square. Area measures the space inside a 2D shape, such as counting the squares in a rectangle. For volume, you will discover how much space a 3D shape takes up, like building with cubes and counting them. You will use simple rules, like adding for perimeter or multiplying for area, to solve problems with real-life examples like fencing a garden or filling a box.

## Angles: rules-geometry problems

In Year 6, you learn to spot angles in shapes and lines, like right angles (which are always 90°) and angles on a straight line (which add up to 180°). You also discover that angles inside a triangle add up to 180°, so if you know two angles, you can find the third one by adding them together and taking the answer away from 180. For example, if a triangle has angles 60° and 50°, the last angle must be 70° because 60 + 50 = 110, and 180 – 110 = 70. You will practise using these rules to solve simple problems, like finding missing angles in squares or along straight lines.

## Statistics: pie charts-mean

In statistics, you will learn to draw and interpret pie charts to show how parts make up a whole. A pie chart is divided into slices, where each slice represents a fraction of the total data. To find the size of each slice, you calculate the fraction of the total and then work out the angle by multiplying by 360 degrees. For example, if half the children in a class like apples, the apple slice in the pie chart will be half of the circle, which is 180 degrees. Pie charts help you see how different groups compare at a glance.
