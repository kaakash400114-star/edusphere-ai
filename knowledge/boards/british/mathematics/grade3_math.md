# British Year 3 Mathematics - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Place value to 1000

Children learn to recognise and understand the value of digits in numbers up to 1000, focusing on hundreds, tens, and ones. They use place value charts to partition numbers, such as 342, into 3 hundreds, 4 tens, and 2 ones. Pupils compare and order numbers by looking at the hundreds digit first, then the tens, and finally the ones if needed. They also practise writing numbers in words and figures, like 525 as "five hundred and twenty-five". This helps them build a strong foundation for working with larger numbers in later learning.

## Add-subtract with exchanges

In Year 3, children learn to add and subtract numbers when exchanging is needed, such as when a column adds up to more than 9 or when the top number is smaller than the bottom one in a subtraction. They learn to exchange one ten for ten ones when adding, for example, 37 + 25 requires exchanging to make 62. In subtraction, they exchange one ten for ten ones, like in 52 - 38, where they borrow to find the answer 14. They use methods like partitioning and regrouping to solve these problems accurately, ensuring they understand place value. Children also solve word problems using these skills, helping them apply the concepts to real-life situations.

## Multiplication-division facts

In this topic, children learn to recall multiplication and division facts for the 2, 5 and 10 times tables. They understand that multiplication is the same as repeated addition, for example, 3 × 4 is the same as 4 + 4 + 4. They also learn that division is the inverse of multiplication, so if they know that 2 × 6 = 12, they also know that 12 ÷ 6 = 2. Children use these facts to solve simple problems, like sharing 10 sweets equally between 2 friends, which means each friend gets 5. They practise writing number sentences for both multiplication and division using these facts.

## Two-digit multiplication

In Year 3, you will learn how to multiply two-digit numbers by a single-digit number using a formal written method. First, you multiply the units digit of the two-digit number by the single-digit number. Next, you multiply the tens digit of the two-digit number by the single-digit number, making sure to write the answer in the correct column. For example, to calculate 24 × 5, you first do 4 × 5 = 20, then 20 × 5 = 100, and finally add the two parts together to get the answer of 120.

## Fractions on number line

In Year 3, children learn to place fractions on a number line by dividing the line into equal parts. They locate fractions like 1/2, 1/4, and 3/4 by counting the segments from the start, understanding that the denominator shows how many equal parts the whole is split into. For example, a number line from 0 to 1 divided into four equal parts helps them find 1/4 by counting one segment from 0. They also compare fractions by their position on the number line, seeing that larger fractions appear further along. This visual method helps them grasp fractions as numbers and understand their size relative to each other.

## Equivalent fractions

Equivalent fractions are different fractions that show the same amount, like ½ and 2/4. To find equivalent fractions, you multiply or divide the top number (numerator) and the bottom number (denominator) by the same number, such as doubling both parts of ¼ to get 2/8. You can check if fractions are equivalent by making sure the top number divided by the bottom number gives the same answer, like 3 ÷ 6 = 0.5 and 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5. Simplifying fractions means making them smaller by dividing both numbers by their biggest common factor, like turning 4/8 into ½. This helps you compare and order fractions easily.

## Money problems

In Year 3, children learn to add and subtract amounts of money to solve simple problems, such as finding the total cost of two items or working out change from a pound. They use pounds (£) and pence (p), understanding that 100p makes £1, to write money in different ways, like £2.50 or 250p. Pupils practise counting money to find the total and compare prices to decide which item costs more or less. For example, they might find the total of a comic costing £1.25 and a book costing £3.75, or work out the change from £10 if they buy a £7.20 toy.

## Length-mass-capacity add-subtract

In this topic, children learn to add and subtract lengths, masses, and capacities using practical objects and standard units like centimetres, grams, and millilitres. They solve simple word problems, such as finding the total mass of two parcels or how much liquid is left after pouring some out. Measurements are recorded using symbols (cm, g, ml) and children are encouraged to check if answers make sense, like confirming a sum of lengths is longer than the original pieces. The focus is on understanding addition and subtraction in real-world contexts, building fluency with basic calculations involving one or two steps.

## Perimeter

In Year 3, we learn about the perimeter by measuring the distance all the way around a shape. To find the perimeter, we add up the length of all the sides. For example, a rectangle with sides of 5 cm and 3 cm has a perimeter of 5 + 3 + 5 + 3 = 16 cm. We use rulers and string to measure the sides of squares, rectangles, and triangles to calculate their total perimeter. This helps us understand how long the edge of a shape is.

## Time: 12h-24h

In maths, you learn about different ways to tell the time using both 12-hour and 24-hour clocks. A 12-hour clock uses am for the morning and pm for the afternoon or night, like 7:30 am when you wake up. The 24-hour clock counts all 24 hours in one day, so 7:30 am is 07:30 and 7:30 pm is 19:30, which is 12 plus 7. You can change between the two clocks by adding or subtracting 12 for pm times, like 2:00 pm becoming 14:00. This helps you understand timetables and plan your day, like knowing when school starts at 08:45.

## Angles: right angles

In this topic, children learn to recognise and identify right angles, which are exactly like the corner of a square or rectangle. They use a small square piece of paper or a set square to check if an angle is a right angle, as it will fit perfectly inside. Pupils compare angles to determine if they are smaller than, larger than, or equal to a right angle, using the terms acute (smaller) and obtuse (larger). They practise finding right angles in everyday objects, such as book pages, doors, and windows, to understand their importance in shapes and the world around them. By the end, children can confidently spot and describe right angles in various contexts.

## Statistics: bar charts

In this topic, children learn to read and interpret simple bar charts, where bars show information using different heights. They understand that each bar represents a category, such as favourite colours or pets, and the height of the bar tells us how many things are in that group. Children practise counting the squares to find the total for each category and compare bars to see which has more or less. For example, they might use a bar chart to find out which fruit is most popular in their class. They also learn to create their own bar charts by counting objects and drawing bars to match the numbers.
