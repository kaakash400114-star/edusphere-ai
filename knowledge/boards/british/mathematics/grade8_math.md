# British Year 8 Mathematics - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Number: indices-standard form

In Number, you will learn about indices and standard form to work with very big and very small numbers. Indices, like 2³, show a number multiplied by itself, so 2³ means 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. You will use rules such as multiplying indices (like 3² × 3³ = 3⁵) and dividing indices (like 5⁴ ÷ 5² = 5²). Standard form helps write large numbers, like 3,000 as 3 × 10³, and small numbers, like 0.004 as 4 × 10⁻³. This makes calculations and comparisons much easier in your maths work.

## Percentages-change-reverse

In this topic, you learn how to find the original amount before a percentage change was applied, like working out the starting price of a jumper that has been reduced in a sale. To do this, you reverse the percentage change: if something has increased by 20%, you divide the new amount by 1.20 to find the original price. For example, if a computer costs £360 after a 20% increase, you calculate £360 ÷ 1.20 = £300, which was the starting price. You also solve problems where something has decreased, such as finding the original cost of a coat that is now £80 after a 20% discount by dividing by 0.80. This helps you understand how percentage changes work both forwards and backwards.

## Algebra: brackets-quadratics-graphs

In Year 8, you learn to expand brackets by multiplying everything inside, like turning 2(x + 3) into 2x + 6. You then move on to expanding two sets of brackets, such as (x + 2)(x + 5), by multiplying each term in the first bracket by each term in the second, giving you x² + 7x + 10. Next, you factorise simple quadratics by finding two numbers that multiply to the last number and add to the middle number, so for x² + 5x + 6, the numbers are 2 and 3, making (x + 2)(x + 3). Finally, you learn to draw straight-line graphs using equations like y = 2x + 1 by plotting points where x is given and y is worked out.

## Equations-simultaneous intro

In Year 8, you learn how to solve two equations at the same time using the elimination method. This means you look at both equations and add or subtract them to get rid of one letter, leaving you with an equation with just one letter to solve. Once you find the value of one letter, you swap it back into one of the original equations to find the other one. For example, if you have x + y = 10 and x - y = 2, adding them together gets rid of y, so you can find that x = 6. You then use this to find that y = 4.

## Geometry: angles-polygons-congruence

In Year 8, you learn about angles in shapes and how to measure them using a protractor. You explore different polygons, like triangles and quadrilaterals, and discover rules such as the sum of angles in a triangle always being 180 degrees. You also find out about regular and irregular polygons, learning that regular shapes have all sides and angles equal, like a perfect square. Congruence is introduced, where you learn that two shapes are congruent if they are exactly the same size and shape, even if they are rotated or flipped.

## Ratio-proportion-real contexts

In Year 8, you learn to write ratios using colons and simplify them to their smallest form, like turning 8:12 into 2:3. You use ratios to share amounts fairly, for example, splitting £20 between two people in a 3:2 ratio. You also explore how ratios connect to fractions, so a 1:2 ratio means one part out of three in total. Finally, you apply ratios to real-life situations like mixing paint or scaling recipes, making sure you understand that ratios compare parts, not just single numbers.

## Probability: combined events

In this topic, you learn how to find the chance of two things happening together, which we call combined events. If the first event doesn’t change the chance of the second one, you can multiply their probabilities: for example, the chance of rolling a 3 on a dice and getting heads on a coin is 1/6 × 1/2 = 1/12. Sometimes, you need to list all possible outcomes, like when picking two coloured marbles from a bag, to see how many ways an event can happen. You also learn that if events can’t happen at the same time, like rolling a 2 or a 5, you add their probabilities together. This helps you solve problems about real-life chances, like games or weather.

## Statistics: scatter-graphs

In Year 8, you learn to draw and interpret scatter graphs, which help us see if two things are connected, like height and arm span. We plot pairs of data as points on a graph, with one thing on the horizontal axis and the other on the vertical axis. If the points roughly follow a straight line going upwards, it shows a positive correlation, meaning as one thing increases, so does the other. If the line slopes downwards, it’s a negative correlation, showing one thing increases as the other decreases. We also learn to draw a line of best fit, a straight line that goes near most of the points, to help us see the pattern more clearly.

## Perimeter-area-circles

In Year 8, you learn how to find the distance around a circle, called the circumference, using the formula C = π × d, where 'd' is the diameter. You also discover that the area of a circle is worked out using A = π × r², with 'r' being the radius, which is half the length of the diameter. You will practise these formulas to solve problems, such as finding the area of a circular garden or the distance around a roundabout. Understanding π is key, as it represents the special number that links a circle's diameter to its circumference. You will use these skills to compare the size of different circles and solve real-life problems involving their perimeters and areas.

## 3D: surface-volume

In this topic, you learn how to find the volume of cubes and cuboids by multiplying their length, width, and height together. You also explore surface area by calculating the total area of all the faces on 3D shapes, like adding up the six sides of a cube. For example, a cube with 4 cm sides has a volume of 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 cm³. You might work out the surface area of a rectangular box by measuring each face and adding them up. These skills help you understand how much space shapes take up and how much material covers them.
