# British Year 1 Mathematics - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Number and place value to 100

In Year 1, children learn to count, read and write numbers to 100 in both numerals and words. They understand the concept of place value, recognising that two-digit numbers are made up of tens and ones, for example, the number 54 has 5 tens and 4 ones. Pupils learn to identify one more or one less than any given number up to 100 and are able to use the greater than (>), less than (<), and equals (=) symbols to compare numbers. They also count in steps of 2, 5, and 10 to support their understanding of number patterns and multiplication.

## Addition-subtraction to 20

Pupils learn to add and subtract numbers within 20, starting with counting on and back to find answers. They use number lines and objects like counters to help them understand that addition combines groups, while subtraction takes away. For example, 7 + 5 is found by counting on 5 from 7 to get 12, and 12 – 5 is found by counting back 5 from 12 to get 7. They also learn number bonds to 10, such as 6 + 4 = 10, to help with mental calculations. By the end, they can solve simple word problems like "I have 9 sweets and eat 3, how many do I have left?" using subtraction.

## Multiplication as grouping

In Year 1, children learn that multiplication is a way of counting objects in equal groups. They explore this by putting items into sets with the same number in each, such as three groups of two apples. The language ‘lots of’ is introduced to link addition to multiplication, so 2 + 2 + 2 becomes ‘three lots of two’. Pupils use repeated addition and visual aids like arrays to understand that multiplication makes counting groups faster. They also learn to solve simple problems involving grouping, like finding the total number of legs on four dogs, each with four legs.

## Halves and quarters

In this topic, children learn to recognise and find halves and quarters of shapes, objects, and quantities. They understand that a half is one of two equal parts, like cutting an apple into two pieces, and a quarter is one of four equal parts, such as sharing a cake with four friends. Pupils practise dividing objects into halves and quarters by folding or sharing them equally, ensuring each part is the same size. They also learn to identify half and quarter quantities, for example, finding half of six sweets or a quarter of a small set of blocks. These skills help children understand fractions as equal parts of a whole.

## Measurement: length-mass-capacity

In this topic, children learn to measure and compare length, mass, and capacity using non-standard and standard units like centimetres, grams, and litres. They use rulers and scales to measure objects accurately, for example finding that a pencil is 15 cm long or an apple weighs 150 g. Capacity is explored by filling containers with water to see how many litres they hold. Pupils learn to use terms such as longer, shorter, heavier, lighter, more, and less to describe comparisons. They also solve simple problems, like adding two lengths together to find a total distance.

## Time: o'clock-half past

In this topic, pupils learn to tell the time using analogue clocks, focusing on o'clock and half past times. They understand that at o'clock, the long minute hand points to the 12, and the short hour hand points to the hour number, such as 3 o'clock when the hour hand is at 3. For half past, the minute hand points to the 6, and the hour hand is halfway between two numbers, like halfway between 2 and 3 for half past 2. Pupils practise reading and drawing clock faces to show these times, building their confidence with time-telling skills for everyday life.

## Money: pounds and pence

In this topic, pupils learn to recognise and use pounds (£) and pence (p) to count and compare amounts of money, understanding that 100 pence equals one pound. They practise adding and subtracting simple sums of money, such as finding the total cost of two items or working out the change from a given amount. For example, they would calculate that £1 and 25p plus 75p equals £2, or that £5 minus £3 and 10p is £1 and 90p. Pupils also begin to solve practical money problems in real-life contexts, like buying items from a shop, using coins and notes to make specific amounts. By the end of this unit, children can confidently handle everyday money transactions using correct notation.

## 2D-3D shapes

Children learn to recognise and name common 2D shapes, including circles, squares, triangles, and rectangles, and describe their properties such as the number of sides and corners. They also identify everyday 3D shapes like cubes, cuboids, spheres, and pyramids, naming flat faces and curved surfaces. Pupils practise sorting and grouping shapes based on their characteristics, for example, by counting the number of sides or identifying which shapes roll. They begin to understand the difference between 2D shapes, which are flat, and 3D shapes, which have depth, using real-world objects like books and balls to help them. This foundational knowledge helps children build spatial awareness and prepares them for more complex geometry later in their studies.

## Position and direction

In mathematics, pupils learn to describe position and movement using everyday language like 'under', 'next to', and 'above'. They use words such as 'forwards', 'backwards', 'up', and 'down' to follow and give simple instructions for movement along a straight line. Children also begin to understand whole, half, and quarter turns, both clockwise and anti-clockwise, using practical objects or themselves. For example, they might turn a toy a quarter turn to the right or make a quarter turn when standing up. These fundamental skills help pupils build spatial awareness and logical reasoning for future problem-solving.
