# British Year 12 Mathematics - National Curriculum/GCSE

## A-Level prep: algebra-functions-graphs

In this topic, you will learn to work with algebraic functions, understanding how to substitute values and find composite functions like f(g(x)). You will explore different types of graphs, including linear and quadratic, and discover how their equations relate to their shapes, such as why a quadratic graph forms a parabola. Key formulas, like the quadratic equation for finding roots, will help you solve problems where graphs meet the x-axis. You will also practise transforming graphs, for example, shifting or reflecting them using simple rules. Mastering these skills prepares you for more advanced algebra and graph work at A-Level.

## Coordinate-geometry-circles-line

In this topic, you learn how to find the equation of a circle using its centre coordinates and radius, such as (x - 3)² + (y + 2)² = 25 for a circle with centre (3, -2) and radius 5. You also discover how to work out the distance from a point to a line and determine if a line is a tangent to a circle by checking if the distance from the centre to the line equals the radius. By solving simultaneous equations, you find where a straight line and a circle meet, which might give two points, one point (tangent), or no points. These skills help you solve real-world problems, like calculating the shortest path from a point to a circular path.

## Calculus: differentiation-integration-intro

In Year 12, you will learn how to find the gradient of curves using differentiation. You will apply rules like the power rule, where for a term like \(3x^2\), you multiply the coefficient by the power and reduce the power by one, giving \(6x\). Integration is then introduced as the reverse process of differentiation, allowing you to find areas under curves; for example, integrating \(6x\) gives \(3x^2 + c\). You will also explore how these concepts relate to real-world contexts, such as calculating rates of change or determining the area of irregular shapes. Understanding the connection between differentiation and integration is key to solving problems involving motion and growth.

## Sequences-series-binomial

In Sequences, you learn to find and use the nth term for arithmetic sequences, like 3, 7, 11, ..., where the rule is 4n – 1, and geometric sequences, such as 5, 10, 20, ..., where the rule is 5 × 2ⁿ⁻¹. You also explore Fibonacci sequences, where each term is the sum of the two before it, like 1, 1, 2, 3, 5. For series, you calculate the sum of the first n terms of arithmetic and geometric sequences using formulas: Sₙ = n/2 (2a + (n–1)d) for arithmetic and Sₙ = a(1 – rⁿ)/(1 – r) for geometric. Binomial expansions help you multiply out expressions like (a + b)⁴ using Pascal’s Triangle or the formula for combinations, such as (a + b)³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³.

## Trigonometry: identities-equations

In Year 12, you learn to use trigonometric identities like sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 to solve equations and prove other statements are always true. You explore how to simplify expressions by rewriting them using these rules, for example, changing tanθ into sinθ/cosθ. The unit also covers solving equations such as 2sinθ + 1 = 0 by finding all angles between 0° and 360° that work. By practising these methods, you become confident in handling more complex problems that mix different trig functions and require rearranging terms.

## Exponentials-logarithms

In this topic, you learn how to work with numbers that grow very quickly, called exponentials, and their reverse, logarithms. You will explore rules like multiplying powers (e.g., \(2^3 \times 2^4 = 2^{7}\)) and understand how to solve equations using them, such as finding \(x\) in \(2^x = 8\). Logarithms help you reverse this process, so \(\log_2 8 = 3\) because \(2^3 = 8\). You also study real-life applications, like calculating how money grows in a bank account over time using formulas such as \(A = P(1 + r)^t\). By the end, you will be confident switching between exponential and logarithmic forms to solve problems.

## Probability-statistics-large-data

In this topic, you learn to calculate probabilities for combined events using rules like multiplication for independent events, such as the chance of rolling two sixes in a row. You also explore statistical measures for large datasets, including mean, median, mode, and range, and how to represent this information using grouped frequency tables and histograms. You will analyse data to compare distributions, recognising how measures of spread, like interquartile range, show how consistent or varied results are. For example, you might compare test scores from two classes using cumulative frequency to find the median and interquartile range. Finally, you learn to judge reliability by identifying outliers and understanding how sample size affects conclusions drawn from data.

## Mechanics: kinematics-forces

In mechanics, you will learn how to describe and predict motion using key ideas like speed, velocity, and acceleration. You will explore the equations of motion to calculate how far something travels or how fast it goes when it speeds up or slows down, like a car braking on a road. Forces are also a big part of this topic; you will discover how forces like gravity and friction affect movement, such as why a ball rolling on the ground eventually stops. By analysing forces and motion together, you can understand real-world situations, like why a parachutist falls slowly or how rockets lift off into space.

## Vectors

In this topic, you will learn how to describe vectors using column notation, such as (3, 4), which means moving 3 units right and 4 units up. You will explore how to add and subtract vectors by adding or subtracting their corresponding components, for example, (2, 1) + (1, 3) = (3, 4). You will also multiply vectors by a scalar to change their size or direction, like multiplying (2, 1) by 3 to get (6, 3). Furthermore, you will discover that vectors can be used to represent directions and movements, helping you solve problems involving forces and paths. Finally, you will learn about vector magnitude, which calculates the length of a vector using Pythagoras’ theorem.

## Proof-methods

In GCSE mathematics, you learn different methods to prove mathematical statements are always true. These include direct proofs, where you use known facts to show a statement must be correct, and proof by contradiction, where you assume the opposite is true and find a mistake to show it can’t be right. You also practice proving number properties, like showing that the product of two even numbers is always even, or proving geometric theorems by using angle facts and shape rules. These skills help you build logical arguments and justify your answers in maths problems.

## Numerical-methods

In this topic, you will learn how to find approximate solutions to equations when they cannot be solved exactly. You will use methods like trial and improvement to get closer and closer to the right answer. Another key method is iteration, where you use a formula, like x_n+1 = √(6 + x_n), repeatedly to find a root of an equation. For example, you might use the formula x = 1/x + 2 to find an approximate value for a root. You will also learn how to show these methods on graphs and understand that the approximation gets more accurate with each step.

## Modelling-cycle

In the Modelling-cycle topic, you learn how to turn real-life problems, like working out the cost of a school trip, into mathematical problems you can solve. You follow a cycle: start with a real-world situation, simplify it into a mathematical model, solve the maths, check if the answer makes sense in the real world, and then improve the model if needed. For example, you might model the number of buses needed by assuming each bus holds 30 pupils and using division to find the answer. This process helps you use maths to make decisions and solve practical problems.
