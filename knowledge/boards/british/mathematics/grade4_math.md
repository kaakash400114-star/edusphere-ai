# British Year 4 Mathematics - National Curriculum/GCSE

## Place value to 10,000-rounding

In this topic, children learn to recognise the value of each digit in numbers up to 10,000, understanding that the position of a digit shows its worth, like how the 5 in 5,000 is five thousands. They practise writing numbers in words and numerals, for example, writing “four thousand two hundred” as 4,200. Pupils also learn to round numbers to the nearest 10 or 100 using simple rules: if the next digit is 5 or more, they round up, and if it’s 4 or less, they round down, like rounding 2,456 to 2,500 when rounding to the nearest 100.

## Column add-subtract

In column addition and subtraction, you learn to write numbers in columns, making sure the ones, tens, and hundreds are all in the right place. You add or subtract starting from the ones column, carrying over any extra numbers to the next column if you need to. For example, when adding 34 and 25, you add 4 and 5 in the ones column to get 9, then 3 and 2 in the tens column to get 5, so the answer is 59. When subtracting, if the top number is smaller, you borrow from the next column to make it bigger, like in 45 minus 17, where you borrow from the 4 to make the 5 a 15, so 15 minus 7 is 8, and 3 minus 1 is 2, giving 28.

## Multiplication: 3-digit × 1-digit

In this topic, children learn how to multiply a three-digit number by a one-digit number using a written method, starting with the ones and moving to the hundreds. They use place value to break down the multiplication, for example, 4 × 345 is solved by first multiplying 4 × 5 (20), then 4 × 40 (160), and finally 4 × 300 (1200), before adding the results together (20 + 160 + 1200 = 1380). This method helps them understand how to carry over numbers when the product of a digit is 10 or more. Children practise this step-by-step approach to build confidence in solving larger multiplication problems accurately.

## Division with remainders

When we divide numbers, sometimes there are not enough groups to share equally, and a leftover piece is called a remainder. We write this using the ‘R’ symbol, for example, 10 divided by 3 is 3 with a remainder of 1, because 3 groups of 3 make 9, and 1 is left over. The remainder is always smaller than the number we are dividing by, so it can never be as big or bigger. We learn to check if our answer is right by multiplying the answer by the divisor and adding the remainder to see if we get back to the starting number.

## Fractions: equivalent-add

Children learn to find equivalent fractions by multiplying or dividing both the top number and the bottom number by the same number, for example, 2/4 is the same as 1/2 because you can divide both numbers by 2. They practise adding fractions with the same bottom number, such as 1/5 + 2/5 = 3/5, by simply adding the top numbers. They also learn to add fractions with different bottom numbers by finding a common bottom number, for example, 1/2 + 1/4 becomes 2/4 + 1/4, which equals 3/4. This helps them understand fractions as parts of a whole and how to combine them correctly.

## Decimals: tenths-hundredths

In Year 4, you learn about decimals, specifically tenths and hundredths. A tenth is one part of a whole thing split into ten equal parts, like 0.1, and it means one out of ten. A hundredth is one part of a whole thing split into one hundred equal parts, like 0.01, and it means one out of one hundred. You can write tenths after a decimal point using one digit, such as 3 tenths as 0.3, and hundredths using two digits, like 25 hundredths as 0.25. These numbers help you count and measure smaller, more precise amounts in everyday life.

## Money: convert-estimate

In Year 4, children learn to change pounds into pence and pence into pounds, knowing that £1 is the same as 100p. They also practise rounding prices to the nearest pound to help them add up costs in their heads, like estimating that £2.40 and £3.60 will be about £6. Pupils explore adding and subtracting money in practical problems, such as finding the total cost of two items or how much change is left from a £5 note. They learn to write money with a decimal point, like £4.75, and check if they have enough to buy something.

## Area-perimeter of rectangles

In this topic, children learn to find the perimeter of a rectangle by adding the lengths of all four sides together, and to find the area by multiplying the length by the width. They practise measuring rectangles in centimetres and metres, using rulers to check their sides. For example, a rectangle with sides 4 cm and 6 cm has a perimeter of 20 cm (4 + 6 + 4 + 6) and an area of 24 cm² (4 × 6). Children explore how different rectangles can have the same perimeter but different areas, and the same area but different perimeters. They also solve simple problems, such as finding the missing side length when given the perimeter or area of a rectangle.

## Time-conversion units

In Year 4, you learn to convert between different units of time, such as hours, minutes, and seconds. There are 60 minutes in one hour, and 60 seconds in one minute, so to change minutes into hours, you divide by 60. For example, 120 minutes is the same as 2 hours because 120 ÷ 60 = 2. You also learn that there are 24 hours in a day and 7 days in a week. This helps you understand how time is made up of smaller parts.

## Angles: acute-obtuse

In this topic, you will learn to spot the difference between acute and obtuse angles. An acute angle is smaller than a right angle, like the pointy top of a slice of pizza. An obtuse angle is bigger than a right angle but less than a straight line, like the wide open mouth of a crocodile. You will practice using a set square to see if angles are smaller or larger than 90 degrees to help you name them correctly. This skill helps you understand shapes all around you, from the corners of your books to the designs on playgrounds.

## Symmetry

In this topic, pupils learn to find symmetry by folding shapes and seeing if both sides match exactly, like a butterfly or a butterfly. They spot lines of symmetry in squares, circles, and rectangles, where one side is a mirror image of the other. Pupils also explore rotational symmetry by turning shapes to see how many times they look the same in a full spin, like a star or a windmill. They use this to sort shapes and complete patterns by drawing the missing symmetrical part. This helps them understand balance and repetition in the world around them.

## Statistics: comparison charts

In Year 4, you will learn to collect information and show it in special charts like pictograms and bar charts. You must draw your pictograms using one picture to represent two items, making sure your key is always clear and correct. When you make bar charts, you need to measure the bars carefully and label both the axes, so people can see the information easily. For example, you might show how many children like apples or bananas by drawing tall bars next to each other. These charts help you compare information quickly, so you can see which group has more or less.
