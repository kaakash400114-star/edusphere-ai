# Finnish (FNBE) Grade 11 Science
## Quantum Physics and Atomic Structure
Students study the fundamental principles of quantum mechanics, focusing on the wave-particle duality of light and matter. The photoelectric effect demonstrates that light behaves as particles (photons), with energy E = hf, where h is Planck's constant and f is frequency. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle states that the position and momentum of a particle cannot be precisely known simultaneously (ΔxΔp ≥ h/4π). Bohr's model explains electron energy levels in hydrogen atoms; transitions between these levels absorb or emit photons with specific energies, visible as spectral lines. Finnish research at Aalto University contributes to quantum computing.

## Genetics and Biotechnology
Students explore the structure and function of DNA, the molecule carrying hereditary information. The double helix structure, with base pairs adenine-thymine and guanine-cytosine, forms the genetic code. Central to the course is the polymerase chain reaction (PCR), a method to amplify DNA segments using heat-stable enzymes from bacteria found in Finnish hot springs. Techniques like CRISPR-Cas9 allow for precise editing of genes. Examples include developing cold-resistant crops for Finnish agriculture and understanding the genetic basis of hereditary conditions prevalent in the Nordic population.

## Climate Science and Sustainable Energy
This module covers the Earth's climate system, the enhanced greenhouse effect caused by human activities, and its consequences, such as melting permafrost and changes in the Northern Lights' visibility. Students analyze energy consumption data for Finland and model the energy balance of a single-family home. They evaluate renewable energy sources, calculating the energy output of a 2 MW wind turbine in coastal Lapland and comparing it to the efficiency of a geothermal heat pump system used in Helsinki. The course emphasizes sustainable solutions using Finnish engineering and design principles.

## Materials Science and Industrial Applications
Students learn about the properties of materials, focusing on metals, polymers, and ceramics. The concept of crystal structure is central, with examples like the face-centered cubic structure of steel used in constructing sauna heaters. Phase diagrams are used to explain heat treatment processes like tempering, which increases the toughness of a steel blade. Students calculate the Young's modulus for a pine log, a material crucial for Finnish log homes, and analyse the tensile strength of aramid fibres used in modern skis and snowmobile suits, reflecting Finland's innovative material culture.

## Electromagnetic Induction and Power Systems
The course details Faraday's law of induction, which states a changing magnetic flux induces an electromotive force (EMF = -NΔΦ/Δt). Students apply this to understand the function of generators and transformers in converting voltage levels within Finland's national power grid. A key example is calculating the induced EMF in a 100-turn coil when the magnetic field through it changes from 0.2 T to 0.5 T in 0.3 seconds. The efficiency of power transmission is examined, emphasizing the role of high-voltage lines to minimize energy loss, a critical factor for supplying energy across the long distances of the Finnish archipelago.

## Cosmology and Astrophysics
Students investigate the large-scale structure and evolution of the universe. Hubble's law (v = H₀d), showing that galaxies recede from us with a velocity proportional to their distance, is fundamental for determining the universe's expansion rate. Big Bang nucleosynthesis is used to explain the observed abundance of light elements like hydrogen and helium. The lifecycle of stars is modelled, from main-sequence phases to supernovae. Finnish contribution is noted, as the largest optical telescope in the Nordic countries, located at the University of Helsinki's Kungsnäs Observatory, is used to monitor celestial events like aurorae borealis.
