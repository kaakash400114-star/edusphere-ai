# Finnish (FNBE) Grade 6 Science

## The Cell as the Basic Unit of Life
All living organisms are made of cells, the smallest structural and functional units of life. Plant cells have a rigid cell wall, chloroplasts for photosynthesis, and a large central vacuole, while animal cells lack these structures. A typical animal cell contains a nucleus, mitochondria for energy production, and a cytoplasm. In Finland, the local birch tree (*Betula pendula*) is studied for its leaf cells containing chloroplasts, which convert sunlight into energy. Students learn to identify cell organelles and understand their specific functions within the cell.

## Chemical Reactions and Compounds
Chemical reactions involve the formation of new substances with different properties. A reaction requires reactants, which transform into products. The law of conservation of mass states that mass is neither created nor destroyed. For example, burning wood (carbon) with oxygen produces carbon dioxide gas: C + O₂ → CO₂. In Finnish schools, experiments like the rusting of a nail (iron reacting with oxygen and water) are used to demonstrate chemical changes. Students also learn about water (H₂O) as a fundamental compound essential for all life.

## Forces and Motion
Motion is a change in an object's position, caused by forces such as friction, gravity, and push/pull. Newton's First Law, or the law of inertia, states that an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at a constant velocity, unless acted upon by an unbalanced force. A practical example is the frictional force slowing down a sled on the snow in Finnish winter. Students learn to calculate speed using the formula: speed = distance / time. Gravity is a constant force pulling objects toward Earth's center.

## Electricity and Magnetism
Electricity is the flow of electric charge, often carried by electrons through a conductor like copper wire. An electric circuit requires a power source (e.g., a battery), conductors, and a component to use the energy (e.g., a lightbulb). Voltage (V) is the electric potential difference, current (A) is the flow rate, and resistance (Ω) opposes the current (Ohm's Law: V = I * R). In Finland, students might explore how to complete a simple circuit to light an LED, understanding how it powers devices from a mobile phone to the sauna's heater.

## The Structure and Properties of Matter
Matter exists as solids, liquids, or gases, distinguished by the arrangement and energy of their particles. Solids have a fixed shape and volume, liquids have a fixed volume but take the shape of their container, and gases have neither. Density is a key property, calculated as mass per unit volume (ρ = m/V). For example, the density of water is approximately 1 g/cm³, while ice, despite being frozen, is less dense and floats. Students in Finland might compare the density of wood to a log used in a sauna or design a simple density column experiment.

## The Northern Lights and Earth's Magnetosphere
The Northern Lights, or aurora borealis, are a natural light phenomenon caused by charged particles from the sun colliding with Earth's magnetosphere. These particles are guided by Earth's magnetic field to the polar regions, where they excite gases in the atmosphere. Oxygen produces green or red light, while nitrogen produces blue or purple. Finland's location in the auroral zone makes it an ideal place for this study. Students learn about solar wind, the magnetosphere's role in protecting Earth, and the science behind the mesmerizing displays visible in Finnish Lapland.
