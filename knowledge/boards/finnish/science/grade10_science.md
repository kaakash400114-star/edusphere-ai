# Finnish (FNBE) Grade 10 Science
## Energy Transformations and Efficiency
Students explore the laws of thermodynamics, focusing on energy conservation and entropy. They analyse the efficiency of different systems using the formula η = (Useful Energy Output / Total Energy Input) × 100%. Examples include calculating the efficiency of a sauna heater (~95%) and a modern power plant (~45%). The concept of exergy, the maximum useful work, is introduced. Finland's combined heat and power plants, which utilize waste heat for district heating, exemplify real-world energy efficiency. Students model energy flow diagrams, quantifying losses to the environment in euros per kilowatt-hour.
## Chemical Reactions in Industrial Applications
Learners investigate equilibrium constants (K) and Le Châtelier's principle, applying them to the Haber-Bosch process for ammonia synthesis. They solve for reaction yields using K = [Products] / [Reactants]. An example is the sulphuric acid contact process, modeling the oxidation of SO₂ to SO₃. Finnish students analyze the carbon footprint of the Fiskars steelworks, calculating the reduction in CO₂ emissions through catalytic converters. Redox titration experiments are performed to determine the purity of industrial reagents using standardized solutions.
## Electromagnetism and Power Generation
This module covers Faraday's law of induction, ε = -N ΔΦ/Δt, and Lenz's law. Students calculate the electromotive force (EMF) generated in a simple generator, relating voltage to rotation speed and magnetic field strength. Finland's national power grid is studied, including the role of hydroelectric power plants like Imatrankoski. The principles of transformers are explored using Vp/Vs = Np/Ns. The environmental impact of different energy sources, measured in euros per megawatt-hour, is debated. Practical work involves building a simple electric motor.
## Genetics and Biotechnology in the Nordics
Students examine Mendelian inheritance, calculating phenotypic ratios from dihybrid crosses (e.g., 9:3:3:1). Modern techniques like CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing are explored, analyzing applications like crop modification. Finnish genetic research, such as the FinnGen project mapping population genomes for health, serves as a key example. The ethical and safety concerns surrounding genetically modified organisms (GMOs) are debated within the EU context. Learners use Punnett squares to predict inheritance patterns and interpret karyotypes to identify chromosomal abnormalities like Down syndrome.
## Climate Change and Atmospheric Physics
The greenhouse effect is modelled using Stefan-Boltzmann's law for radiation, P = εσAT⁴. Students analyse the IPCC's climate data, calculating changes in average global temperatures and CO₂ concentrations. The impact of aerosols on albedo is studied, with examples from volcanic eruptions. Climate models predict future scenarios based on emission pathways, with economic consequences measured in billions of euros. Finnish research on the impact of climate change on the Arctic, such as altering the frequency of the Northern Lights (Aurora Borealis), is central to the curriculum.
## Waves and Optics in the Northern Environment
Students apply the wave equation v = fλ to sound and light. Snell's law, n₁sinθ₁ = n₂sin₂, is used to calculate refraction in optical fibres, which are integral to Finland's telecommunications infrastructure. The phenomenon of the Northern Lights is explained through the excitation of oxygen and nitrogen atoms by solar wind particles, emitting specific wavelengths of light. Learners design experiments to measure the refractive index of materials like glass and water. Applications in medical imaging, such as ultrasound, are explored.
