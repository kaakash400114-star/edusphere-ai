# Australian Curriculum (ACARA) Grade 11 Mathematics
## Introduction to Functions
Students explore the concept of a function as a special type of relation, understanding that it pairs each input with exactly one output. They learn to use function notation, such as f(x), to represent relationships. A key skill is determining the domain and range of a function, often considering real-world constraints like the positive values for a circle's radius. Students identify different function types, including linear, quadratic, and piecewise functions, using both algebraic rules and visual graphs to describe how variables relate to each other.

## Algebraic Techniques
This topic develops students' proficiency in simplifying and manipulating complex algebraic expressions. They expand binomials to perfect squares and the difference of squares using the formulas (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² and (a - b)(a + b) = a² - b². Students factorise expressions, including quadratics, and apply algebraic division. They also practise manipulating surds, applying rules like √(a * b) = √a * √b and rationalising denominators to simplify expressions involving square roots.

## Linear Equations and Inequalities
Students solve and graph linear equations and inequalities, applying these skills to practical contexts like budgeting and resource allocation. They represent linear relationships with equations in the form y = mx + c, interpreting the gradient (m) as the rate of change and the y-intercept (c) as the starting value. For example, calculating the cost of a mobile phone plan with a fixed monthly fee plus per-megabyte charges. Students solve systems of linear equations graphically and algebraically using substitution and elimination methods.

## Quadratic Functions and Equations
This area focuses on the properties of quadratic functions, expressed in the form y = ax² + bx + c. Students use methods like completing the square or the quadratic formula (-b ± √(b² - 4ac))/2a to find roots. They analyse the effect of the coefficient 'a' on the parabola’s shape and direction. This knowledge is applied to solve optimisation problems, such as finding the maximum area of a paddock with a fixed amount of fencing, demonstrating a direct link to practical design and industry scenarios.

## Indices and Logarithms
Students extend their understanding of indices to include fractional and negative exponents, applying the laws of indices (e.g., aᵐ * aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ). They are introduced to logarithms as the inverse of exponentiation, understanding that if y = aˣ, then x = logₐy. This is crucial for solving exponential equations, like calculating the time for an investment to grow from AUD$5,000 to AUD$6,000 at a fixed interest rate. They convert between logarithmic and exponential forms to simplify complex growth and decay problems.

## Trigonometry and The Unit Circle
Students explore the unit circle to define the trigonometric functions (sin, cos, tan) for all angles, not just those found in right-angled triangles. They use the exact values for angles like 30°, 45°, and 60°. Students apply the sine and cosine rules (a/sinA = b/sinB = c/sinC, c² = a² + b² - 2ab cosC) to solve non-right-angled triangle problems. For instance, calculating the width of a river or the height of a cliff in the outback using measurements taken from different vantage points.
