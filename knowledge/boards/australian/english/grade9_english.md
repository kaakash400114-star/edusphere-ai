# Australian Curriculum (ACARA) Grade 9 English
## Analysing Persuasive Texts
Students learn to identify the persuasive techniques used in texts, including emotive language, inclusive pronouns (‘we’), rhetorical questions, and hyperbole. They analyse how these techniques are used to influence an audience and convey a particular point of view. For example, they might deconstruct a political speech, examining how terms like ‘unfair’ and ‘disaster’ are used to shape public opinion. Students also evaluate the effectiveness of these strategies, considering the context and the composer's intended purpose. They use metalanguage to describe how language choices are deliberate and impact the reader or listener.
## Structuring Argumentative Writing
Students develop skills in planning and constructing formal, multi-paragraph argumentative texts. They learn to formulate a clear, concise thesis statement that takes a definitive stance on an issue, such as ‘screen time limits are essential for adolescent mental health’. Body paragraphs are structured around a single main idea, which is supported by evidence from credible sources like government reports or scientific studies. Students practice using topic sentences, linking words (furthermore, however), and concluding sentences to ensure a logical and cohesive flow of ideas throughout the piece.
## Interpreting Literary Devices in Poetry
Students analyse how poets use devices such as imagery, symbolism, and metaphor to create meaning and evoke an emotional response. They study how juxtaposition and juxtaposition can highlight contrasts, and how the deliberate selection of words and rhythm contributes to the overall tone. For instance, a poem about the vastness of the outback might use imagery of red dust and endless horizons to create a feeling of isolation or awe. Students are required to explain not just *what* a device is, but *how* it shapes a reader's understanding of the poem's central themes.
## Developing Language Conventions and Spelling
Students consolidate their knowledge of Australian English spelling conventions, including the use of ‘-our’ (colour, honour) and the ‘-ise’ suffix (realise, organise). They refine their understanding of complex grammatical structures, including the correct use of clauses, phrases, and punctuation such as semicolons and colons. The curriculum requires students to apply these conventions accurately in their own writing. For example, they must be able to distinguish between ‘its’ and ‘it’s’ and correctly place a comma in a complex sentence involving a dependent clause, such as ‘After the footy match, we celebrated with a BBQ.’
## Using Digital and Multimodal Texts
Students learn to critically evaluate the ways information and ideas are presented in multimodal texts, such as websites, documentaries, and advertisements. They analyse the relationship between written text, images, sound, and layout to understand how meaning is constructed. For example, they might compare the presentation of information about the Great Barrier Reef on a government tourism site versus an activist group’s blog. Students assess the impact of these different modes on audience engagement and persuasion, and consider the author’s purpose and potential biases in the digital space.
## Composing Creative Narratives with Vivid Description
Students craft narrative pieces that develop engaging characters, settings, and plot structures. A key focus is the use of vivid, sensory description to immerse the reader in the story. They practice showing, not just telling, emotions and actions. For example, instead of writing ‘he was scared’, a student would describe his ‘hands shaking as he clutched the cold steel of the railing’ on a dark night in the city. They learn to use dialogue effectively to reveal character and advance the plot, and to structure their stories with a clear orientation, complication, and resolution.
