# Australian Curriculum (ACARA) Grade 6 English

## Language: Expressing and Developing Ideas
Students explore how authors use language to influence and persuade an audience. They analyse the effect of modality verbs, such as 'might', 'should', and 'must', and learn to create stronger arguments using persuasive devices like exaggeration and rhetorical questions. For example, they might write a letter to the local council suggesting better public transport links, using phrases like 'The current timetable is simply not good enough'. They learn to identify an author's perspective and distinguish between fact and opinion in a range of texts.

## Language: Text Structure and Organisation
Students learn to structure their own writing for different purposes and audiences. They understand how to use paragraphs to group related ideas and use topic sentences to introduce each paragraph. For example, when writing a report on the Great Barrier Reef, they would use a clear introduction, several paragraphs describing different aspects like biodiversity and threats, and a concluding summary. They learn to use text organisers like subheadings, bullet points, and numbered lists to make information clear and easy to follow.

## Literature: Responding to Literature
Students interpret, evaluate, and discuss texts that explore Australian identity and experiences. They analyse character development, setting, and plot, identifying how these elements create meaning. For example, when reading a story set in the outback, they would discuss how the harsh environment influences the characters' actions and decisions. Students learn to use metalanguage to express their interpretations, such as explaining how an author's choice of words creates a particular mood or atmosphere in a narrative about a family gathering for Anzac Day.

## Literature: Examining Literature
Students examine how literary devices and features contribute to the meaning of texts. They identify and analyse similes, metaphors, personification, and symbolism, explaining their effect on the reader. For example, they might analyse the simile 'as tough as old boots' in a story about a footballer to understand how it characterises his resilience. They learn to compare and contrast texts on similar themes, such as bush poetry and contemporary songs about life in a small country town, to understand different perspectives.

## Literacy: Creating Texts
Students plan, draft, and edit a range of imaginative, informative, and persuasive texts. They learn to use appropriate vocabulary, grammar, and punctuation to enhance their writing. For example, when writing a persuasive argument about the need to protect native wildlife, they would use emotive language and specific examples like the number of endangered species. They are taught to structure narratives with a clear orientation, complication, and resolution, developing characters and settings using sensory details.

## Literacy: Interpreting, Analysing, Evaluating
Students use comprehension strategies to interpret, analyse, and evaluate increasingly complex texts. They identify the main idea and supporting details in a news report about a sporting event. They learn to distinguish between literal and inferred meanings, for example, inferring a character's feelings from their actions in a story. Students evaluate the effectiveness of a text, such as an advertisement for a local footy team, by considering its purpose, target audience, and use of persuasive language. They use digital tools to find and compare information from multiple sources.
