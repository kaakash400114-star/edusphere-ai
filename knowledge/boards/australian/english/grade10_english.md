# Australian Curriculum (ACARA) Grade 10 English

## Analysing and Interpreting Texts
Students analyse and interpret a range of complex texts, including literature and media, to understand how meaning is constructed. They explore the effects of authorial choices, such as narrative structure, characterisation, and language devices, on audiences. Students examine texts from diverse cultural perspectives, including those by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander authors, to appreciate different worldviews. They use metalanguage to discuss how texts reflect and shape values and attitudes, supported by evidence from the text. For example, students might compare how a novel and a film adaptation of the same story use visual and verbal techniques to convey themes of identity.

## Creating and Presenting Texts
Students create imaginative, persuasive, and informative texts for different purposes and audiences. They plan, draft, and refine their work, using appropriate structures and language conventions to achieve their intended effect. Students experiment with a variety of forms, such as feature articles, podcasts, or multimedia presentations, to engage an audience. They incorporate research skills to develop evidence-based arguments and personal insights, ensuring their work is cohesive and polished. For instance, a student might write a persuasive blog post arguing for the preservation of the Great Barrier Reef, using AUD funding figures and ecological data.

## Language and Context
Students investigate how language varies according to context, purpose, and audience. They analyse the features of formal and informal language, including slang and idioms (e.g., "footy," "arvo"), and their appropriateness in different situations. Students examine how language shapes and reflects social and cultural identity, including Australian English spelling (e.g., colour, realise). They explore the impact of digital communication on language use and its implications for clarity and expression. For example, students might deconstruct the language used in political speeches versus social media posts to understand persuasive techniques.

## Critical Literacy and Argument
Students develop critical literacy skills to evaluate texts and arguments. They identify bias, propaganda, and logical fallacies in a range of sources, including news articles and advertisements. Students construct reasoned arguments, supported by evidence, to express and justify their own viewpoints. They learn to acknowledge and respond to counter-arguments, demonstrating a nuanced understanding of complex issues. For instance, a student might analyse a debate on climate policy, identifying key stakeholders' positions and the rhetorical devices used to influence public opinion.

## Literature and Culture
Students engage with a range of literary works to explore themes, ideas, and cultural contexts. They analyse how literature reflects and challenges societal values, including those related to identity, justice, and the environment. Students study works by Australian authors, such as Tim Winton or Alexis Wright, to appreciate local narratives and perspectives. They make connections between texts and their own lives, fostering empathy and critical thinking. For example, students might compare a novel set in the outback with a poem about urban life to discuss contrasting Australian experiences.

## Research and Inquiry Skills
Students conduct independent research to investigate topics of interest, using a range of sources, including digital databases and primary documents. They develop skills in paraphrasing, summarising, and synthesising information to create coherent responses. Students use referencing conventions, such as APA or Harvard, to acknowledge sources and avoid plagiarism. They present their findings in various formats, such as reports or oral presentations, demonstrating their ability to communicate complex ideas clearly. For example, a student might research the economic impact of tourism on a regional Australian town and present their findings in a formal report.
