# Australian Curriculum (ACARA) Grade 9 Science
## Energy Transformations and Efficiency
Students explore the law of conservation of energy, recognising that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed from one form to another. They analyse common transformations, such as chemical energy in petrol converting to kinetic energy in a car, or electrical energy in a light bulb becoming light and heat. The concept of energy efficiency is introduced as a measure of useful energy output compared to total energy input. For example, an old incandescent bulb might be 5% efficient, converting only a small fraction of electrical energy to light. Students calculate efficiency using the formula: Efficiency = (Useful Energy Output / Total Energy Input) × 100%.

## Chemical Equations and Reactivity
Students learn to interpret and construct balanced chemical equations, ensuring the number of atoms of each element is equal on both sides. They use chemical formulas to represent reactants and products in reactions, such as the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂). The concept of reactivity is examined through the reactivity series of metals, predicting displacement reactions. For instance, zinc is more reactive than copper, so zinc can displace copper from a copper sulfate solution, forming zinc sulfate and solid copper. Students use this knowledge to predict outcomes in chemical scenarios.

## Force, Motion and Newton's Laws
Students apply Newton's laws of motion to explain and predict the behaviour of objects. Newton's First Law (inertia) states that an object will remain at rest or in uniform motion unless acted upon by an unbalanced force, such as a seatbelt stopping a passenger's forward momentum during a car crash. Newton's Second Law (F=ma) is used to calculate the force required to accelerate a 60kg student at 2 m/s², resulting in a force of 120 Newtons. Newton's Third Law explains that forces always occur in pairs; a footy player kicking a ball exerts a force on the ball, and the ball exerts an equal and opposite force back on the foot.

## Genetics and Inheritance Patterns
Students investigate the mechanisms of inheritance, focusing on Mendelian genetics and Punnett squares to predict the probability of traits in offspring. They use dominant (A) and recessive (a) alleles to model inheritance patterns. For example, crossing two pea plants with the genotype Aa (heterozygous for purple flowers) can be represented with a Punnett square, predicting a 3:1 ratio of purple to white flowers in the offspring. Students apply this to understand human genetic traits and the likelihood of inheriting conditions, moving beyond simple dominant-recessive models to more complex patterns.

## Earth's Dynamic Structure
Students analyse Earth's internal structure, including the crust, mantle, and core, and how plate tectonics drive major geological events. They examine the theory of continental drift, evidenced by the matching coastlines of South America and Africa, and the distribution of fossils and rock formations. The concept of seafloor spreading at mid-ocean ridges is explored as a mechanism for plate movement. This process leads to the formation of new crust and causes earthquakes and volcanic activity, such as the Ring of Fire around the Pacific Ocean, where the Pacific Plate meets other tectonic plates.

## Ecosystems and Biodiversity
Students investigate the structure of ecosystems, including the roles of producers, consumers, and decomposers in energy flow and nutrient cycling. They use Australian examples, such as a red kangaroo (consumer) feeding on grass (producer) in the outback, while fungi and bacteria act as decomposers. The concept of biodiversity is examined, including the variety of life in the Great Barrier Reef, and how human activities like pollution or climate change can impact it. Students explore how changes to one part of an ecosystem, such as the introduction of an invasive species, can affect the entire system.
