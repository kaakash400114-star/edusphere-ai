# Japanese (MEXT) Grade 11 English
## Critical Analysis of Argumentative Texts
Students learn to identify and analyze logical fallacies and persuasive devices in complex English texts, such as opinion columns or speeches. They study common fallacies, including ad hominem attacks and false dichotomies. The skill involves distinguishing between strong, evidence-based arguments and weak, emotional ones. Students practice evaluating the credibility of sources and the use of rhetorical appeals: logos, pathos, and ethos. An example is analysing a debate on Japan's high-speed rail system, the shinkansen, to assess the validity of economic and environmental claims.
## Structured Academic Writing for University Preparation
Students master the structure of formal academic essays, including the introduction, body paragraphs with topic sentences, supporting evidence, and analysis, and a conclusion. They learn to write a clear and concise thesis statement that outlines the essay's argument. Techniques for using transitions to create a coherent flow between ideas are emphasized. Students practice writing a formal essay comparing the cultural significance of hanami (cherry blossom viewing) in Tokyo and Osaka, requiring research and proper in-text citation.
## Listening and Note-Taking from Academic Lectures
This unit develops the ability to comprehend and accurately record information from university-style lectures. Students learn to identify key points, main arguments, and supporting details in spoken English. They practice different note-taking methods, such as outlining, mind-mapping, and the Cornell system. A key focus is distinguishing between primary information and the lecturer's examples or anecdotes. An exercise involves listening to a lecture on the Japanese economy and taking detailed notes on factors influencing the value of the yen.
## Literary Analysis of Prose and Poetry
Students develop the ability to analyze and interpret literary works, focusing on themes, symbolism, character development, and narrative structure. They learn to use specific literary terminology, such as metaphor, simile, foreshadowing, and point of view. The analysis requires students to construct evidence-based arguments about a text's deeper meaning. For example, students analyze a short story set in a rural Japanese village, examining how the setting influences the characters' motivations and the story's central conflict.
## Presentation and Debate Skills
Students learn to prepare and deliver formal presentations on academic topics, using clear organization, logical progression, and effective visual aids. They practice structuring an argument, using evidence, and anticipating counter-arguments. The unit includes skills for formal debates, such as rebuttal and the use of respectful, persuasive language. A common topic is a debate on the merits of school-provided bento lunches versus students bringing their own, requiring them to present facts and defend their position logically.
## English for Global Communication and Media Literacy
This section focuses on understanding and producing English in a global context. Students analyze international news articles and advertisements, identifying different cultural perspectives and potential biases. They learn to interpret data presented in charts and graphs related to global issues. A key task is to write a formal email to an international partner regarding a cultural exchange program, demonstrating an understanding of formal business etiquette and clear, unambiguous communication in a professional setting.
