# Japanese (MEXT) Grade 1 Mathematics

## Number and Operations
Students learn numbers from 1 to 100. They count forward and backward, and understand place value of ones and tens. Students use number lines and objects like blocks to practice addition and subtraction within 20. They learn to write and read number symbols. For example, they can solve 8 + 5 = 13 using ten frames. A bento box with 9 onigiri and adding 3 more teaches this, reinforcing the concept of composing and decomposing numbers.

## Quantities and Measurements
Students understand and compare length, mass, and capacity through direct observation using non-standard units, such as paper clips for length or their own hands for capacity. They learn to order objects based on these attributes, for example, determining if a Shinkansen model is longer or shorter than a pencil. For mass, they use hefting to compare which object is heavier or lighter, such as two different types of fruit. To understand capacity, they fill containers with sand or water to see which holds more. Through these activities, they express relationships using comparative language like 'longer,' 'heavier,' and 'more full.'

## Geometric Figures
Students learn to recognize and correctly name fundamental two- and three-dimensional geometric figures, specifically triangles, squares, circles, cubes, and spheres. The curriculum mandates the identification of key attributes, such as the number of sides and corners for polygons, using physical models. Students sort and classify these figures based on their defining characteristics. Through guided play with tangram-like pieces and building blocks, they compose new figures, exploring how shapes can fit together to form larger shapes and solids. This hands-on approach develops foundational spatial reasoning skills, with concrete examples including identifying a triangular roof on a house or the spherical shape of a dodgeball.

## Patterns and Relationships
Students identify and generate repeating patterns by recognizing a core unit that recurs, such as an ABAB sequence. They learn to continue, extend, and create patterns with concrete materials like colored blocks, shapes, sounds, and number sequences, for example, 2-4-2-4. Through these activities, they understand that a pattern is a predictable arrangement governed by a specific rule. This foundational concept is applied to understand cyclical events, such as the sequence of the four seasons or the rhythm of 'clap-stomp-clap-stomp.' The curriculum emphasizes that patterns establish order and regularity in the world.

## Data Handling
Students learn to collect data through simple surveys, such as asking peers about their favourite seasonal fruits. They organize this information by sorting the data into categories, such as apple, banana, and orange. The primary method for recording and counting data is using tally marks, where every fifth mark is drawn diagonally across the previous four. This organized data is then represented visually using pictographs, where a single picture or symbol stands for one or more items in a category. For example, they might draw a picture of a bento box for each student who chose a specific item. Students then interpret these graphs to answer factual questions like 'How many students preferred the item with the most votes?' or 'How many students chose this item in total?'

## Problem Solving
Students solve simple word problems using addition and subtraction within 20. They use drawings or concrete objects to model the problems. For example, 'A train has 10 cars. 3 more are added. How many are there now?' They learn to explain their thinking process. Problems are often framed around familiar cultural contexts, like sharing sweets during hanami or counting people on a bus, ensuring the mathematics is relevant and meaningful.
