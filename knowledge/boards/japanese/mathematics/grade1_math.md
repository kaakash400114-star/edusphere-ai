# Japanese (MEXT) Grade 1 Mathematics
## Number and Operations
Students learn numbers from 1 to 100. They count forward and backward, and understand place value of ones and tens. Students use number lines and objects like blocks to practice addition and subtraction within 20. They learn to write and read number symbols. For example, they can solve 8 + 5 = 13 using ten frames. A bento box with 9 onigiri and adding 3 more teaches this, reinforcing the concept of composing and decomposing numbers.

## Quantities and Measurements
Students understand length, mass, and capacity. They compare and order objects by these attributes using non-standard units like paper clips or their hands. For instance, they compare the length of two Shinkansen toy models. They learn to express these comparisons using words like 'longer,' 'heavier,' and 'more full.' Activities involve filling containers with water or sand to understand volume, connecting mathematical concepts to tangible, everyday experiences.

## Geometric Figures
Students recognize and name basic two- and three-dimensional shapes. They learn terms like triangle, square, circle, cube, and sphere. They identify these shapes in their environment, such as a round yen coin or a square bento box. Through play, students sort shapes by attributes like the number of sides or corners. They use physical models to explore how shapes fit together and compose new figures, fostering spatial reasoning.

## Patterns and Relationships
Students identify and create simple repeating patterns using colors, shapes, sounds, or numbers. For example, they might continue a pattern like 'red-blue-red-blue' or clap a rhythm like 'clap-stomp-clap-stomp.' They learn that patterns have a unit that repeats. This understanding is linked to the seasons, such as the pattern of sakura blooming in spring, helping them see order and predictability in the world around them.

## Data Handling
Students collect, organize, and interpret simple data. They use tally marks and pictographs to represent information, such as favourite fruits or types of bento items. For instance, they might count how many students prefer tuna onigiri versus egg. They learn to answer basic questions like 'Which has the most?' or 'How many are there?' This develops early skills in sorting and categorizing information from their daily lives.

## Problem Solving
Students solve simple word problems using addition and subtraction within 20. They use drawings or concrete objects to model the problems. For example, 'A train has 10 cars. 3 more are added. How many are there now?' They learn to explain their thinking process. Problems are often framed around familiar cultural contexts, like sharing sweets during hanami or counting people on a bus, ensuring the mathematics is relevant and meaningful.
