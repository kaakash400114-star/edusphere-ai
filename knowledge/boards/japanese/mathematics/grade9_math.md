# Japanese (MEXT) Grade 9 Mathematics

## 1. Numbers and Expressions
Students explore real numbers, focusing on square roots and irrational numbers. They learn to approximate irrational numbers like √2 or π and distinguish between rational and irrational numbers. Expressions involving square roots are simplified using rules like √a × √b = √(ab). Students also solve quadratic equations by factoring, completing the square, and using the quadratic formula, x = [-b ± √(b²-4ac)] / 2a. Practical examples include calculating the side length of a square garden with an area of 50 square metres, which is an irrational number.

## 2. Functions and Graphs
Students deepen their understanding of functional relationships, specifically focusing on linear functions of the form y = ax + b, direct and inverse proportionality, and quadratic functions represented as y = ax² + bx + c. They learn to graphically represent these relationships as straight lines and parabolas, acquiring methods to determine key features such as the gradient, y-intercept, and for a parabola, its vertex, axis of symmetry, and x-intercepts. This analytical work is applied to modelling real-world phenomena, such as analysing the profit function for a bento box sale or calculating the trajectory of a model rocket, allowing students to solve practical problems and make valid predictions based on their graphical and algebraic analysis.

## 3. Geometric Figures and Proofs
Students establish the congruence of triangles using fundamental criteria such as Side-Side-Side (SSS), Side-Angle-Side (SAS), Angle-Side-Angle (ASA), and Right-angle-Hypotenuse-Side (RHS), demonstrating that corresponding sides and angles are identical. For similarity, they learn that corresponding angles are equal and corresponding sides are proportional, enabling the solution of indirect measurement problems. Students apply theorems like the Basic Proportionality Theorem to prove similarity, for instance, by analyzing similar triangles formed by a tree and its shadow to determine the tree's height. This section also covers the properties of quadrilaterals and the conditions under which they are similar, strengthening geometric reasoning and proof skills essential for higher-level mathematics.

## 4. Measurement and Mensuration
Students calculate the area and volume of more complex shapes, including prisms, pyramids, cylinders, and spheres. Formulas for the volume of a sphere (V = 4/3πr³) and the area of a sector (A = (θ/360) × πr²) are applied. They solve problems involving real-world objects, such as finding the volume of a cylindrical water tank to determine the amount of water needed for a hanami festival or calculating the surface area of a bento box to design an appropriate lid.

## 5. Probability and Statistics
Students will explore both theoretical and experimental probability, learning to calculate the likelihood of single and compound events, distinguishing between independent and dependent cases. In statistics, they construct and interpret data representations such as frequency tables, histograms, and box-and-whisker plots to analyze distributions. A core concept involves applying the formula P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) for independent events, for example, calculating the combined probability of a Shinkansen train arriving on time and a passenger catching their connection. This is contrasted with dependent events, where conditional probability is used, such as finding the chance of drawing a red card after a black one has been removed from a deck. The curriculum ensures students can analyse real-world data, like the frequency of delays on the Yamanote line, and apply these statistical methods to draw informed conclusions.

## 6. Problem Solving with Mathematical Processes
Students will master advanced mathematical processes by integrating algebraic, geometric, and statistical concepts to solve complex problems. This involves applying principles of proportional reasoning and functions to model real-world scenarios, such as calculating the minimum surface area of a rectangular prism for a given volume to minimise material costs. A key method is creating mathematical representations from contextual problems, such as setting up a system of linear equations to determine the most cost-effective travel plan between cities using different train fares. Furthermore, students will employ coordinate geometry to justify solutions for problems involving loci, and apply transformations to solve geometric congruency proofs. Throughout, the emphasis is on justifying each step of the solution with precise logical reasoning and mathematical notation.
