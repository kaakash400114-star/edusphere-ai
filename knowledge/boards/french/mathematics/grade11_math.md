# French (Éducation nationale) Grade 11 Mathematics  

## Numbers and Calculations
Students master complex numbers, written in the form *a + bi*, where *a* and *b* are real numbers and *i* is the imaginary unit (*i² = -1*). They perform addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division, converting between algebraic and trigonometric (or polar) forms. For example, the complex number *1 + i* can be expressed in trigonometric form as √2 (cos(π/4) + i sin(π/4)). Students solve second-degree equations with complex coefficients, ensuring fluency in operations essential for advanced physics and engineering applications.

## Algebra and Functions
Students analyze polynomial and rational functions, identifying roots, asymptotes, and symmetry. They study the derivative as a function, using limits to define *f'(a) = lim(h→0) (f(a+h) - f(a))/h*. Derivatives of common functions (*xⁿ*, sin(x), cos(x), eˣ*, ln(x)) are computed, with chain and product rules applied. For instance, the derivative of *f(x) = (2x + 1)³* is *f'(x) = 3(2x + 1)² × 2*. Applications include determining the speed of a cyclist during the Tour de France at any given moment.

## Geometry and Trigonometry
Students explore vectors in 2D and 3D, calculating norms, dot products, and cross products. They use vector equations to describe lines and planes. Trigonometric identities, such as *sin²(x) + cos²(x) = 1*, are used to simplify expressions. For example, finding the angle between two paths in a city like Paris using the dot product formula. Students solve triangles, applying the sine and cosine laws to problems involving distances, such as calculating the length of a métro tunnel between two known points.

## Statistics and Probability
Students learn to model bivariate data using a least squares regression line, interpreting the slope and y-intercept within a real-world context, such as the relationship between student study hours and exam scores. They calculate and interpret Pearson's correlation coefficient *r* to quantify the strength and direction of a linear association, distinguishing between correlation and causation. In probability, they master conditional probability using the formula *P(A|B) = P(A∩B) / P(B)* and apply Bayes' theorem to update probabilities given new evidence, such as determining the probability of a disease given a positive test result. These methods are fundamental for analysing data and making informed predictions across various scientific and social contexts.

## Sequences and Series
Students study arithmetic and geometric sequences, finding the *n*-th term (*uₙ = u₀ + nr* for arithmetic, *uₙ = u₀ × qⁿ* for geometric) and partial sums. They analyze convergent series, particularly geometric series with |*q*| < 1, summing them as *S = u₀ / (1 - q)*. For example, calculating the total distance traveled by a bouncing ball if it loses a fraction of its height with each bounce. Limits of sequences are introduced, foundational for calculus.

## Problem-Solving and Modelling
Students apply mathematical concepts to real-world problems. For instance, optimizing the layout of a supermarket to maximize efficiency or calculating the optimal number of bakeries needed for a population. They use functions, equations, and inequalities to model scenarios, such as budgeting a school trip. Problems often involve currency conversion, like calculating the total cost of a purchase in euros and US dollars. Emphasis is placed on logical reasoning and presenting clear, step-by-step solutions.
