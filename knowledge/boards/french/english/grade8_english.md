# French (Éducation nationale) Grade 8 English

## Language Structures: Future Tenses and Conditional Mood
Students master the formation and use of the simple future (`will` + verb) and the near future (`to be going to` + verb). They learn to express predictions (`It will rain`), spontaneous decisions (`I'll call him`) and planned actions (`I am going to study`). The conditional mood is introduced to express politeness, hypothetical situations, and past events unrealised in the present (`I would buy a baguette if we passed the boulangerie`). Students practice with phrases such as "What would you order for your birthday dinner?" and "The train will arrive at 18:02".

## Vocabulary: Daily Life and Cultural Concepts
This unit expands vocabulary related to urban and rural lifestyles in France and the UK. Key terms include `métro`, `RER`, `boulangerie`, `supermarché`, `village`, and `campagne`. Students learn expressions for discussing routines (`prendre le métro`) and making suggestions (`Allons au cinéma`). Cultural comparisons are made, for example, comparing a typical French market with a British high street. Vocabulary is contextualised with sentences like "On prend le bus pour aller à la bibliothèque du quartier" and "She usually buys fresh croissants at the boulangerie".

## Reading Comprehension: Media and Authentic Texts
Students develop skills to understand short articles from English-language youth magazines, websites, and travel guides. They identify the main idea, specific details, and the author's purpose. Texts may describe a `Tour de France` stage, a new film release in Paris, or travel tips for London. Students learn to interpret charts and graphs, for example, comparing temperatures in London and Marseille. Exercises include answering questions like "What is the main advantage of the Paris metro system according to the text?" and finding the meaning of `timetables` from context.

## Writing Skills: Formal and Informal Communication
Students learn to differentiate between formal and informal registers. They write a formal email to request information from a tourist office and an informal text message to a friend. They practice structuring a simple argument with an introduction, points, and a conclusion. Writing tasks include describing a memorable day during a school trip to London or persuading a friend that visiting the Louvre is a worthwhile activity. Key grammatical points include using linking words (`however`, `furthermore`) and varied sentence structures.

## Listening: Extracting Information from Spoken English
Students listen to short dialogues and announcements, such as a weather forecast for France, a `métro` delay notice, or a conversation about a school exchange. They focus on extracting specific details like times, dates, and locations. They identify the speakers' attitudes, feelings, and intentions. Activities include multiple-choice questions and matching speakers to opinions. For example, they listen to two students discussing a film and must identify who enjoyed it and who did not, based on key phrases like "I thought it was brilliant" versus "It wasn't my cup of tea".

## Oral Production: Presenting and Discussing Opinions
Students practice expressing and defending opinions on familiar topics like their hobbies, films, and future plans. They prepare and deliver short, structured presentations of 2-3 minutes on subjects such as "A French celebration" or "My dream holiday". They learn to use discourse markers (`firstly`, `in conclusion`) and respond to simple questions from the class. Role-plays involve situations like ordering food in a British café or discussing the advantages of a `vélo` versus a car for getting around a city.
