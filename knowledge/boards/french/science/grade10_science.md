# French (Éducation nationale) Grade 10 Science
## The Chemical Bond: Ionic and Covalent
Atoms bond to achieve a stable electron configuration, often mimicking the nearest noble gas. In ionic bonding, a metal atom transfers electrons to a non-metal atom, creating ions held by electrostatic forces. For example, sodium (Na) donates an electron to chlorine (Cl), forming sodium chloride (NaCl), common table salt. In covalent bonding, non-metal atoms share electrons. Water (H₂O) forms when oxygen shares two electrons with two hydrogen atoms, creating a molecule. The strength of the bond determines properties like melting point: NaCl (ionic) has a high melting point (801°C), while ice (covalent) melts at 0°C.

## Energy Transformations and Efficiency
Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed. This is the principle of conservation of energy. In a system, the total energy input equals the total energy output plus any energy dissipated as waste, often as heat. Efficiency is a measure of useful energy output compared to total energy input, expressed as a percentage. For instance, an old métro train converting 30% of chemical energy from fuel into kinetic motion has 30% efficiency. Improving efficiency reduces waste and cost, a key principle in designing sustainable technology for reducing carbon footprint.

## Kinematics: Describing Motion
Kinematics is the study of motion using displacement, velocity, and acceleration. Displacement (Δx) is the change in position, velocity (v) is displacement over time (v = Δx/Δt), and acceleration (a) is the change in velocity over time (a = Δv/Δt). If a cyclist accelerates from 5 m/s to 11 m/s in 3 seconds, their acceleration is (11-5)/3 = 2 m/s². The Tour de France provides real-world examples of average speed calculations and analysing changes in velocity during mountain stages versus flat parcours.

## Electromagnetism and its Applications
An electric current creates a magnetic field, a fundamental principle used in electromagnets and motors. The strength of the field increases with the current and number of coils in a solenoid. This phenomenon is applied in everyday devices. For example, a boulangerie's electric oven uses a heating element, where resistance converts electrical energy into thermal energy for baking bread. Similarly, electric motors in household appliances transform electrical energy into mechanical motion, demonstrating the direct application of electromagnetism in French industry and daily life.

## Ecosystems and Energy Flow
An ecosystem consists of all living organisms (biotic factors) and their non-living environment (abiotic factors) interacting in a specific area, such as a forest near the Alps. Energy flows through ecosystems via trophic levels, starting with producers like plants that convert solar energy into chemical energy. This energy is transferred to primary consumers (herbivores), then to secondary (carnivores), with significant energy lost as heat at each step. A food chain example is: grass → rabbit → fox. The stability of an ecosystem depends on this balance, which can be disrupted by pollution or climate change.

## The Mole and Chemical Quantities
The mole (symbol: mol) is the SI unit for the amount of a substance, defined as 6.022 x 10²³ elementary entities (Avogadro's number). It allows chemists to count particles by weighing. The molar mass (M) of a substance, in grams per mole (g/mol), is the mass of one mole of that substance. For example, water (H₂O) has a molar mass of approximately 18 g/mol (2x1 + 16). To find the amount of substance (n), the formula is n = mass / molar mass. This concept is essential in industrial chemistry for calculating reactant quantities, such as producing fertiliser for French agriculture.
