# Advanced Placement (AP) Grade 10 Mathematics

## Linear Functions and Equations
Students master writing, graphing, and solving linear equations in various forms, including slope-intercept (y = mx + b), point-slope, and standard form. They calculate slope as the rate of change, distinguishing between positive, negative, zero, and undefined slopes. This includes solving multi-step linear equations and inequalities algebraically and graphically on a coordinate plane, finding x- and y-intercepts, and writing equations of parallel and perpendicular lines using their slopes and a given point.

## Systems of Linear Equations and Inequalities
Students analyze systems of two or three linear equations by applying algebraic methods like substitution and elimination, as well as graphical representation to find the point of intersection, which represents the unique solution. For systems of linear inequalities in two variables, students graph each boundary line and use a test point to determine the solution region, known as the feasible region. This foundational concept is extended to linear programming, where real-world problems are modeled by finding the maximum or minimum value of a linear objective function, such as profit or cost, subject to the system of inequalities as constraints.

## Introduction to Quadratic Functions
This topic covers the definition and properties of quadratic functions, expressed as f(x) = ax² + bx + c. Students graph parabolas by identifying the vertex, axis of symmetry, and x- and y-intercepts. They use factoring, completing the square, and the quadratic formula to solve quadratic equations and find roots. The connection between the discriminant (b² - 4ac) and the nature of the roots (real, complex, repeated) is established, providing a deeper understanding of polynomial behavior.

## Polynomial and Rational Functions
Students perform arithmetic operations on polynomials, such as multiplying (x² + 2x)(x – 3) to get x³ – x² – 6x, and dividing using long or synthetic division. They apply the Remainder Theorem, where P(2) is the remainder of P(x) ÷ (x – 2), and the Factor Theorem, which states that (x – c) is a factor if and only if P(c) = 0. For rational functions, students identify domain restrictions by setting the denominator to zero, as in f(x) = 1/(x² – 1), where x ≠ ±1. They simplify expressions like (x² – 9)/(x + 3) to x – 3, for x ≠ –3, and solve equations such as (x/x-2) + 1 = 4, discarding extraneous solutions that violate the domain.

## Exponential and Logarithmic Functions
Students explore exponential functions, f(x) = a·b^x, distinguishing between growth and decay based on the base value 'b'. They define logarithms as the inverse of exponential functions and understand the properties of logarithms that allow for the expansion and condensation of logarithmic expressions. Skills include solving exponential and logarithmic equations using common (log) and natural (ln) bases, and applying these concepts to real-world problems involving compound interest and population growth.

## Introduction to Probability and Counting Principles
Students calculate the probability of events using the fundamental rule P(A) = (number of favorable outcomes) / (total number of possible outcomes), distinguishing between mutually exclusive and independent events. They compute conditional probability using P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B) to determine the likelihood of an event given that another has occurred. The unit introduces multiplication and addition principles for counting, with permutations (nPr = n! / (n-r)!) used for ordered arrangements like race finishes, and combinations (nCr = n! / (r!(n-r)!)) for unordered selections like committees. These methods provide essential tools for quantifying chance and systematically solving diverse problems in probability and statistics.
