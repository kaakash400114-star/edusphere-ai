# Advanced Placement (AP) Grade 1 Mathematics

## Numbers and Counting
Students understand numbers up to 120, starting at any number less than 120. They count to 120, forward and backward, and can read and write numerals. Students use place value to understand that a two-digit number is composed of tens and ones. For example, the number 34 represents 3 tens and 4 ones. They can count a collection of up to 120 objects, such as pennies or blocks, and represent the total with a written numeral. This foundational skill is essential for developing number sense and prepares for more complex arithmetic.

## Addition and Subtraction within 20
Students develop fluency in adding and subtracting within 20. They use strategies like counting on, making ten (e.g., 8 + 5 is 8 + 2 + 3 = 13), and decomposing numbers. Students understand subtraction as an unknown-addend problem, such as finding the unknown number in 10 - 6 = ? by thinking 6 + ? = 10. They solve word problems involving addition and subtraction with real-world contexts, like finding the total number of apples or how many are left after some are eaten, using drawings or equations to model the situation.

## Measurement and Data
Students measure lengths indirectly by comparing three objects. They order three objects by length and use a third object as a intermediary to compare. Students express the length of an object as a whole number of length units, using multiple iterations of a shorter object. They organize, represent, and interpret data with up to three categories. For example, they might sort and count objects by color and create a picture graph to show how many are in each category, learning to ask and answer questions about the data represented.

## Shapes and Their Attributes
Students build and draw two-dimensional and three-dimensional shapes. They identify shapes as two-dimensional (lying in a plane) or three-dimensional (solid). Students analyze and compare shapes by their attributes, such as the number of sides or vertices. For example, they can distinguish between triangles (three sides) and rectangles (four sides). They compose simple shapes to form larger shapes; for instance, they can use two trapezoids to make a hexagon. This hands-on work develops spatial reasoning and geometric vocabulary.

## Place Value with Two-Digit Numbers
Students extend their understanding of place value to two-digit numbers. They can identify the tens and ones in a number, such as recognizing that 57 is 5 tens and 7 ones. Students compare two-digit numbers using symbols (>, <, =). For example, 68 is greater than 63. They can add a two-digit number and a one-digit number, and a two-digit number and a multiple of 10, using models or drawings to explain their reasoning. This forms the critical basis for multi-digit arithmetic in later grades.

## Problem Solving with Money and Time
Students identify and count the value of pennies, nickels, dimes, and quarters, knowing that 1 nickel equals 5 pennies and 1 dime equals 10 pennies. They can solve word problems involving money, like finding the total cost of two items. Students tell and write time to the hour and half-hour on analog and digital clocks, understanding terms like "o'clock" and "half past." They relate the time to daily routines, such as knowing that lunch is at 12:00 PM, building practical life skills through mathematical contexts.
