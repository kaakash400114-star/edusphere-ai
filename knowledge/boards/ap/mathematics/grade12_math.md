# Advanced Placement (AP) Grade 12 Mathematics

## Limits and Continuity
Students explore the foundational concept of a limit, denoted as lim(x→c) f(x), which describes the value a function approaches as x nears a point c. They learn to evaluate limits algebraically by direct substitution, factoring, and rationalizing expressions. Graphically, limits are understood by observing function behavior from the left and right. The concept of continuity is defined by three conditions: the function is defined at c, the limit exists at c, and the limit equals the function's value at c. Students apply this definition to identify points of discontinuity in piecewise and rational functions, such as identifying vertical asymptotes where limits approach infinity.

## Derivatives and Their Applications
Students learn that the derivative, f'(x), represents the instantaneous rate of change or slope of the tangent line to a curve at a point. They master differentiation rules, including the power, product, quotient, and chain rules, to find derivatives of polynomial, trigonometric, exponential, and logarithmic functions. Applications involve solving related rates problems, such as determining how the radius of a balloon changes as it inflates, and optimization problems like finding the dimensions of a rectangle with a fixed perimeter that maximize its area. The Mean Value Theorem is used to verify the existence of points where the instantaneous rate equals the average rate of change over an interval.

## Integrals and the Fundamental Theorem
The integral is introduced as the limit of Riemann sums, representing the accumulation of area under a curve. Students learn to compute definite integrals using basic antiderivatives and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, which connects differentiation and integration. They practice techniques like u-substitution to evaluate integrals of composite functions. Applications include calculating the area between curves, such as finding the area bounded by y = x² and y = x, and determining displacement from velocity functions. The concept of an antiderivative is used to solve simple differential equations and to compute definite integrals with variable limits.

## Differential Equations
Students study differential equations, which relate a function with its derivatives, focusing on first-order equations. They learn to solve separable differential equations by isolating variables and integrating both sides, such as modeling exponential growth or decay in populations. Slope fields are used to visualize the family of solutions to a differential equation without solving it explicitly. Applications include modeling real-world phenomena like Newton's Law of Cooling, which describes the temperature change of an object as it approaches ambient temperature. Initial value problems are solved to find particular solutions from general solutions.

## Sequences and Series
Students analyze sequences, which are ordered lists of numbers, and series, which are the sums of sequences. They study the convergence or divergence of infinite geometric and arithmetic series. The Ratio Test and other tests are applied to determine the convergence of more complex series. Taylor series are introduced to represent functions as infinite polynomials, such as the expansion of e^x or sin(x) centered at x = 0. Students use series to approximate functions and evaluate definite integrals, understanding how the number of terms affects accuracy. This knowledge is foundational for understanding power series and their radii of convergence.

## Parametric, Polar, and Vector Functions
Students extend their understanding of functions to parametric equations, which define x and y in terms of a third variable, t, often used to describe motion along a path. Polar equations, where points are defined by radius and angle, are graphed and converted to Cartesian coordinates. Vector functions describe the position of an object in space, with derivatives yielding velocity and acceleration vectors. Applications include calculating the arc length of parametric curves and finding the area enclosed by polar graphs, such as a limaçon. The dot and cross products are used to solve problems involving work, angles between vectors, and the torque on a lever arm.
