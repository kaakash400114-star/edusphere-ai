# Advanced Placement (AP) Grade 3 English

## Elements of a Story
Students identify and describe the fundamental story elements. Setting is identified as the time and place where a story occurs, such as a small town during winter. Characters are the people or animals in the story, and students practice describing traits like 'brave' or 'curious'. The plot is identified as the sequence of events: beginning (where characters are introduced), middle (the main problem or conflict), and end (the resolution). Students analyze stories to identify these key components.

## Grammar: Sentence Structure
This grade introduces the complete subject and complete predicate. A simple sentence must have a subject (who or what the sentence is about) and a predicate (what the subject is or does). For example, in 'The cat slept on the mat', the complete subject is 'The cat' and the complete predicate is 'slept on the mat'. Students practice writing their own simple sentences about familiar subjects, ensuring each has a subject and a predicate to express a full thought.

## Building Vocabulary with Context Clues
Students are taught to identify and analyze five primary types of context clues: definition, synonym, antonym, example, and general sense. When encountering an unfamiliar word, they learn to locate a direct explanation, such as in the sentence, "The arboreal squirrel spends most of its time in trees," where 'arboreal' is defined by its function. Another method is recognizing contrasts with antonyms, like in, "Unlike a recluse, the social butterfly enjoys parties," which clarifies 'recluse' as a solitary person. Students also practice identifying synonyms, for instance, "The little cottage was diminutive, or very small," where 'diminutive' is explained by 'very small.' Through these systematic strategies, students develop the skills to deduce word meanings independently, enhancing their overall reading comprehension and fluency.

## Reading Comprehension: Sequencing Events
Students develop skills to sequence events in the order they happen within a story. They use transition words like 'first', 'next', 'then', and 'finally' to retell the plot. For example, after reading a story about baking a cake, a student might sequence the events: 'First, gather the ingredients. Next, mix them together. Then, pour the batter into a pan. Finally, bake the cake.' This skill demonstrates a clear understanding of the story's structure.

## Reading Fluency
Fluency is demonstrated through accurate, automatic, and prosodic reading. Students practice by reading grade-level texts aloud with speed, ensuring smooth transitions between words, and using correct phrasing. Instruction includes modeling expressive reading, such as raising pitch for questions and lowering it for statements, to convey meaning. Repeated reading of the same passage is a key method, as it builds automaticity with words, freeing cognitive resources for comprehension. A concrete example is reading a dialogue from a story multiple times, improving expression for character voices and punctuation marks like exclamation points. Progress is measured by words read correctly per minute, alongside qualitative assessments of prosody.

## Writing: Informative Paragraphs
Students learn to write a simple informative paragraph about a topic with a topic sentence, supporting details, and a concluding sentence. The topic sentence introduces the main idea, such as 'Dogs are excellent pets'. Supporting sentences provide specific examples, like 'A dog can learn tricks to play with its owner'. The concluding sentence restates the main idea, for example, 'That is why dogs make wonderful companions'. This structure forms the basis for all future academic writing.
