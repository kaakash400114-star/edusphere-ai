# Advanced Placement (AP) Grade 7 English

## Close Reading and Annotation
Students learn to perform a close reading of a text to analyze its literal and figurative meaning. They use annotation methods to mark key passages, identify central ideas, and trace the development of arguments. Students pinpoint rhetorical strategies such as ethos, pathos, and logos within a text. For example, in an excerpt from "The Gettysburg Address," they would annotate the use of allusion, parallelism, and anaphora to evaluate how these devices enhance the speech's persuasive power and emotional impact on the audience.

## Argumentative Writing
Students construct a clear and logical argumentative claim with supporting evidence from credible texts. They practice crafting a thesis statement that is debatable and arguable. Students learn to incorporate counterclaims and effectively refute them. For instance, a student might write an essay arguing whether school uniforms should be mandatory, using statistics from a U.S. Department of Education report as evidence for one claim and addressing the counterclaim of student self-expression by discussing dress code policies from various state education boards.

## Analytical Essay Structure
Students master the five-paragraph analytical essay structure as a foundational framework for argumentation. The introduction includes a hook, context, and a thesis statement. Body paragraphs begin with a topic sentence, present evidence with analysis, and end with a concluding sentence. The conclusion restates the thesis in new words, summarizes key points, and provides a final thought. Students practice organizing their analysis of a character's motivations from a novel like *The Outsiders* using this structured approach.

## Rhetorical Appeals and Devices
Students identify, define, and evaluate the persuasive effect of rhetorical appeals and devices. They analyze ethos (credibility), pathos (emotion), and logos (logic) in speeches, advertisements, and historical documents. Students also study rhetorical devices such as simile, metaphor, hyperbole, and understatement. For example, in Martin Luther King Jr.'s "I Have a Dream" speech, a student would analyze how King uses pathos through vivid imagery of his children to create an emotional connection and build support for his argument.

## Textual Evidence Integration
Students learn the P.E.A. (Point, Evidence, Analysis) method to integrate textual support into their writing. They make a specific point, introduce a direct quote or paraphrase with proper in-text citation, and explain how the evidence proves their point. Students differentiate between summary and analysis, ensuring their writing focuses on interpretation. A student analyzing a poem would state a point about the theme of nature, provide a line from the text, and then analyze the specific word choice and imagery to support their interpretation.

## Research and Citation
Students are introduced to basic research skills, including evaluating source credibility, paraphrasing, and quoting. They begin to learn how to use an MLA-style works cited page and parenthetical citations to avoid plagiarism. Students practice finding and citing a reliable source, such as a peer-reviewed article or a government website, to support a claim in an essay about the environmental impact of a specific species, learning to format the citation correctly according to established guidelines.
