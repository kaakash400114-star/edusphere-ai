# Advanced Placement (AP) Grade 9 English
## Rhetorical Analysis and the Appeals
Students learn to identify and analyze the rhetorical strategies authors use to create meaning and persuade audiences. They study the three classic modes of persuasion: ethos (establishing credibility through authority or virtue), pathos (appealing to the audience's emotions), and logos (using logic, facts, and data). Students practice dissecting texts, from political speeches to personal essays, to pinpoint these appeals. For instance, they might analyze how Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.'s "Letter from Birmingham Jail" uses all three appeals to support his argument for nonviolent protest, building his ethos as a clergyman, evoking pathos through descriptions of injustice, and structuring a logical case for his actions.

## close Reading and Annotation
This section focuses on the foundational skill of close reading, a method for thoroughly examining a text's details. Students learn to annotate a text by marking significant passages, noting the author's word choice (diction), sentence structure (syntax), and literary devices such as metaphor, simile, and symbolism. They identify patterns of repetition and contrast to uncover the text's underlying themes and arguments. For example, while reading F. Scott Fitzgerald's *The Great Gatsby*, students would annotate descriptions of the green light to trace its evolving symbolic meaning, from Gatsby's hopes to the broader themes of the American Dream's corruption.

## Argumentation and the Thesis Statement
Students master the craft of building a coherent and evidence-based argument. They learn to formulate a clear, arguable thesis statement that presents a debatable claim requiring support. The process involves structuring an argument with a claim, evidence (such as quotations from a text or factual data), and warrant (the explanation of how the evidence supports the claim). Students practice writing argumentative essays on topics like whether technology enhances or detracts from human connection, ensuring their thesis is specific and guides the development of their entire piece of writing.

## Literary Elements and Narrative Structure
This unit explores the fundamental elements of fiction and poetry used to construct narratives. Students study character development (protagonist, antagonist, foil), plot structure (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution), setting, point of view, and conflict. In poetry, they analyze elements like speaker, tone, and form (e.g., sonnet, free verse). Students apply this knowledge to dissect texts, such as how Harper Lee uses the trial of Tom Robinson in *To Kill a Mockingbird* to explore themes of racial injustice and moral growth through a first-person child's perspective.

## Grammatical Conventions for Academic Writing
Students review and apply advanced grammatical rules essential for formal, academic prose. Instruction focuses on complex sentence structures, including dependent and independent clauses, correct use of punctuation (commas, semicolons, colons), and parallelism. They learn to avoid common errors like sentence fragments, comma splices, and faulty modifiers. This practical application ensures students can write with clarity and precision. For instance, they learn to use a semicolon to join two closely related independent clauses, as seen in the sentence: "The experiment yielded unexpected results; the team decided to redesign the methodology."

## Research and Integration of Sources
Students learn the process of conducting academic research, from generating a research question to integrating sources effectively. They practice using databases like the JSTOR or the school library's online catalog to find credible sources, including peer-reviewed articles and scholarly books. Students focus on integrating quotes and paraphrases using the Modern Language Association (MLA) format, which requires in-text citations and a works cited page. A key skill is blending sources with their own analysis to support a thesis, rather than merely summarizing, to build a sophisticated and evidence-based argument.
