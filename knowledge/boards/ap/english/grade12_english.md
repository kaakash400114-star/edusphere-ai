# Advanced Placement (AP) Grade 12 English
## AP English Language and Composition: Rhetorical Analysis
Students deconstruct nonfiction texts to analyze an author's rhetorical strategies. They identify and evaluate an author's use of ethos, pathos, and logos, as well as syntax, diction, and imagery. Students learn to assess how these choices work together to persuade a specific audience. For example, they might examine a 2016 speech by Michelle Obama, explaining how her personal anecdotes (pathos) and established credibility (ethos) combine with parallelism to create a powerful message for young people.
## AP English Language and Composition: Argumentation
Students develop evidence-based arguments using effective rhetorical strategies. They learn to construct a clear, defensible thesis and support it with relevant evidence, logical reasoning, and rhetorical appeals. Students must acknowledge and refute counterarguments to strengthen their own position. A student might argue for the expansion of AP Computer Science courses, using data from the College Board on participation rates and post-secondary success as primary evidence, while refuting the counterargument of budgetary constraints.
## AP English Literature and Composition: Close Reading of Poetry
Students engage in a close reading of poetry, analyzing its formal elements. They learn to identify meter, rhyme scheme, and stanza structure to determine a poem's effect. Students explore how figurative language, including metaphor, simile, and personification, contributes to a poem's theme and tone. For instance, they would analyze the iambic pentameter and extended metaphors in Robert Frost's "The Road Not Taken," explaining how the form reinforces the poem's theme of individual choice.
## AP English Literature and Composition: Character and Narration in the Novel
Students analyze the development of characters and the function of narration in prose fiction. They examine how an author's choices regarding point of view, including first-person, third-person limited, and omniscient, shape the reader's understanding of characters and events. Students explore how characters are revealed through direct characterization, dialogue, and actions. A unit on *The Great Gatsby* would focus on the unreliable narration of Nick Carraway, analyzing how his perspective influences the reader's judgment of Jay Gatsby.
## AP English Literature and Composition: Analyzing the Tragedy in Drama
Students analyze the conventions and purpose of tragic drama, drawing from foundational texts. They identify elements of a tragic hero as defined by Aristotle, including hamartia (a fatal flaw) and anagnorisis (a moment of critical discovery). Students explore how conflict drives the plot and how thematic ideas are developed through character downfall and dialogue. A core text might be Shakespeare's *Hamlet*, with students debating whether Hamlet's tragic flaw is indecision, pride, or melancholy, and how it leads to the play's catastrophic end.
## AP English Literature and Composition: The Literary Argument (Synthesis Essay)
Students write a coherent, evidence-based literary analysis essay. This culminating task requires students to formulate a complex thesis about a work's meaning and support it with well-chosen evidence from the text itself. Students synthesize literary elements like plot, character, and theme into a unified argument. For example, a student might write an essay arguing that the symbolic imagery of light and dark in *Macbeth* reflects the characters' moral decay and the play's central theme of the corrupting nature of unchecked ambition.
