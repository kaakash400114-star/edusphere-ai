# Advanced Placement (AP) Grade 10 English
## Reading and Analyzing Diverse Literary Works
Students study a range of literary works, including American and global texts from the 16th to the 21st century. They analyze how authors use literary elements like characterization, setting, and symbolism to convey themes. For example, in F. Scott Fitzgerald's *The Great Gatsby*, students examine the symbolic meaning of the green light and how character interactions reveal critiques of the American Dream. This analysis requires close reading of both explicit and implicit textual evidence to support written arguments.

## Rhetorical Analysis and Argumentation
Students deconstruct nonfiction texts to identify an author's purpose, argument, and rhetorical strategies. They analyze how diction, syntax, and imagery work together to persuade an audience. For instance, a student might analyze Abraham Lincoln's "Gettysburg Address" to determine how its concise language and historical references build credibility and emotional resonance. Students then construct their own evidence-based arguments in essays, mirroring the skills assessed on the AP English Language and Composition exam.

## Developing and Supporting Thesis Statements
A primary skill is formulating a specific, arguable thesis that guides analytical and argumentative writing. Students learn to position their thesis as a claim that requires defense through evidence. For example, a thesis for *To Kill a Mockingbird* might be: "Through the character of Atticus Finch, Lee argues that moral courage is more powerful than social conformity." Students then use this thesis to structure body paragraphs, each starting with a claim and supported by quoted and summarized evidence from the text.

## Writing for Synthesis
This section focuses on the synthesis essay, a key component of the AP exam. Students engage with multiple sources—academic articles, speeches, and visual texts—to explore a central issue. They must identify relationships between the sources and create their own position, incorporating evidence from at least three texts to support their argument. For example, a synthesis prompt on environmental policy might require students to integrate perspectives from a government report, an activist's speech, and a scientific study to build a cohesive response.

## Mastering Multiple-Choice Reading Comprehension
Students practice the specific question types found on the AP English Language multiple-choice section, including rhetoric and composition analysis. They learn to distinguish between questions about meaning, structure, and purpose. For example, a question might ask, "The author includes the anecdote in lines 10-15 primarily to..." Students must select the best answer by eliminating distractors and grounding their choice in specific textual evidence, demonstrating a nuanced understanding of the author's craft.

## Structuring Timed Analytical Essays
This section prepares students for the free-response portion of the AP exam, emphasizing the need for efficient organization under time constraints. Students learn to structure essays with a clear introduction containing a thesis, multiple body paragraphs, and a conclusion. They practice allocating time for planning, writing, and editing a complete essay in approximately 40 minutes. For example, given a prompt on a poetry passage, they must quickly identify key poetic devices and formulate a central argument to support a coherent and insightful response.
