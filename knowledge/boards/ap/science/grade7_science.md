# Advanced Placement (AP) Grade 7 Science

## Introduction to Scientific Inquiry
Scientific inquiry is a systematic process for acquiring knowledge. It involves posing questions, conducting observations, and designing experiments. Students learn to form testable hypotheses and identify variables in an investigation. They differentiate between qualitative and quantitative data. A key skill is creating a control group to establish a baseline for comparison. For example, in an experiment testing plant growth under different light conditions, the control group receives standard light while the experimental groups receive varied light intensities to measure the effect.

## Structure and Properties of Matter
Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. Atoms, the basic building blocks, consist of protons, neutrons, and electrons. Elements are pure substances made of one type of atom. Compounds form when two or more elements chemically bond. The physical states of matter are solid, liquid, and gas, determined by the arrangement and energy of particles. For instance, water (H₂O) is a compound; at 0°C, it freezes into a solid (ice), at 25°C it is a liquid, and at 100°C it becomes a gas (steam).

## Energy: Forms and Transformations
Energy is the capacity to do work and occurs in various forms, including kinetic, potential, thermal, and chemical. The law of conservation of energy states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed. Students calculate kinetic energy using the formula KE = ½mv², where m is mass and v is velocity. Potential energy is calculated as PE = mgh, with g as gravity (9.8 m/s²) and h as height. For example, a roller coaster at the top of a hill has maximum potential energy, which transforms into kinetic energy as it descends.

## Forces and Motion
Motion is a change in an object's position over time, described by speed, velocity, and acceleration. Newton's laws of motion govern this. The first law states an object at rest stays at rest unless acted upon by an unbalanced force. The second law (F = ma) shows force equals mass times acceleration. The third law states for every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. A 10 kg object accelerating at 2 m/s² experiences a net force of 20 Newtons, demonstrating the direct proportionality between force and acceleration for a constant mass.

## Earth's Systems and Resources
Earth's systems include the geosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, and biosphere, interacting through cycles like the water cycle. The rock cycle involves igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rock formation. Plate tectonics explain earthquakes and volcanoes. Humans impact these systems; for example, burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas that contributes to climate change. The water cycle includes evaporation from the Pacific Ocean, condensation into clouds, and precipitation over the Rocky Mountains, demonstrating a continuous natural process.

## Biodiversity and Environmental Interactions
Biodiversity is the variety of life in an ecosystem, including species, genetic, and ecosystem diversity. Organisms interact through predation, competition, and symbiosis. A food web illustrates energy transfer, such as grass being consumed by rabbits, which are prey for foxes. Carrying capacity is the maximum number of individuals an environment can sustain. Human activities like deforestation in the Amazon rainforest reduce biodiversity by destroying habitats, disrupting these intricate relationships and reducing genetic variation essential for ecosystem resilience.
