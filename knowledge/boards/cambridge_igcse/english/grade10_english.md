# Cambridge IGCSE Grade 10 English

## Critical Analysis of Non-Fiction Texts
Students learn to identify and analyse the writer's purpose, audience, and viewpoint in non-fiction texts. They examine the use of language and structure to achieve specific effects, such as persuasion or information. Key techniques include analysing the tone, diction, and rhetorical devices. For example, they would evaluate how a travel article uses evocative language and vivid imagery to create a sense of desire in a potential tourist. Students must provide textual evidence to support all interpretations, demonstrating a close and critical reading of the source material.

## Unseen Poetry Analysis
Students develop the skill of analysing a previously unseen poem, focusing on its thematic concerns, imagery, structure, and sound devices. They learn to consider the poet’s choice of form (e.g., sonnet, free verse) and how it contributes to meaning. For example, a student might explore the contrast between the rigid structure of a sonnet and its content about love to argue for a complex, constrained passion. The response requires a detailed, line-by-line analysis to explore how the poet’s craft shapes the reader’s understanding and emotional response.

## Writing for Purpose and Audience
Students practise producing a range of transactional and discursive writing pieces tailored to a specific purpose and audience. This includes writing formal letters, reports, articles, and speeches. Each piece requires careful consideration of tone, register, and format. For instance, a letter of complaint to a multinational company must be formal and factual, while a magazine article promoting environmental activism for a youth audience should use engaging, persuasive language and rhetorical questions. The core is adapting style to context effectively and accurately.

## The Study of a Prose Text
Students undertake an in-depth study of one prescribed prose text from the Cambridge syllabus. They analyse the novel's or short story's narrative structure, characterisation, setting, and themes. For example, when studying a novel like *The Book Thief*, students would explore how the character of Death as a narrator influences the reader’s perception of events. They must use relevant quotations to support their analysis of how literary devices contribute to the text's overall meaning and impact on a reader.

## Speaking and Listening Skills
Students engage in formal speaking and listening activities, including individual presentations and group discussions. They learn to structure arguments clearly, use appropriate rhetorical devices, and adapt language for a formal context. For example, in a debate on globalisation, a student must present a coherent case, use evidence effectively, and respond to counter-arguments with clarity and respect. The emphasis is on the clear articulation of ideas, effective use of standard English, and active listening to build upon or challenge the points of others.

## Transactional and Creative Writing Development
This section hones the ability to write for specific real-world contexts and for creative expression. Students learn to compose emails, reports, and proposals with precision and clarity. Simultaneously, they develop their creative writing skills by crafting narratives, descriptions, or monologues. For example, a creative piece might use sensory language to describe a bustling market, while a transactional piece could be a structured proposal outlining a plan for a new school club. Both forms require careful planning, drafting, and attention to linguistic detail and accuracy.
