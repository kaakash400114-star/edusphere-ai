# Cambridge IGCSE Grade 2 Mathematics

## Number and Place Value
Students learn to count forwards and backwards to at least 100, starting from any number. They read and write numerals to 100 and understand place value for two-digit numbers, recognising that the digits represent tens and ones. For example, in the number 47, the digit 4 represents 4 tens. They compare and order numbers from 0 to 100 using the greater than (>), less than (<), and equals (=) symbols. Students also identify odd and even numbers up to 100 and use number lines and hundred squares to support their understanding of number patterns and sequences.

## Addition and Subtraction
Students develop methods for adding and subtracting two-digit numbers. They use concrete objects, number lines, and drawings to solve problems. A key method is partitioning: for 45 + 23, they add 40 + 20 to make 60, then 5 + 3 to make 8, giving a final answer of 68. They also learn to add and subtract multiples of 10. Students use the inverse relationship between addition and subtraction to check their answers, for example, solving 50 - 22 and using 22 + 28 = 50 to confirm. Real-world problems involve simple calculations with items like fruits or toys.

## Multiplication and Division
Students understand multiplication as repeated addition, for example, 3 x 4 is 4 + 4 + 4. They learn the 2, 5, and 10 multiplication tables and recall related division facts, such as 30 ÷ 5 = 6. They use arrays and groups of objects to visualise these concepts. A practical example is finding how many legs there are on 5 chairs, which involves calculating 5 x 4. For division, they share items into equal groups, such as sharing 12 pencils between 4 children. Students solve simple, one-step problems using these operations, using concrete apparatus to model the situations.

## Measurement
Students measure and compare lengths, mass, and volume using standard units like centimetres (cm), grams (g), and litres (L). They use rulers, scales, and measuring jugs. For example, they might measure the length of a pencil in cm and a book in metres, or find the mass of an apple on a balance scale. Students also learn to read the time to the hour, half-hour, and quarter-hour on an analogue clock, using terms like 'o'clock', 'half past', and 'quarter past'. They sequence events in chronological order and use calendars to identify days and weeks.

## Geometry
Students identify and name common 2D shapes, including squares, rectangles, circles, and triangles, and describe their properties (e.g., number of sides and corners). They use these shapes to create patterns and pictures. For 3D shapes, they name cubes, cuboids, spheres, and pyramids and relate them to real-world objects, such as a cube-shaped dice or a ball (sphere). Students learn to describe the position of an object using terms like 'above', 'below', 'next to', and 'in front of', following and giving simple directions on a grid or in a small area.

## Handling Data
Students learn to collect, sort, and organise data into simple categories, such as sorting classroom objects by colour or size. They create and interpret pictograms, where a picture represents a number of items. For example, a pictogram might show the favourite fruits of the class, using one apple picture for every two students who chose apples. They answer simple questions about the data, such as 'Which fruit is the most popular?' and 'How many more children chose bananas than oranges?'. This introduces them to the basic concepts of presenting and understanding information.
