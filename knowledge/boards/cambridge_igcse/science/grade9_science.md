# Cambridge IGCSE Grade 9 Science  
## Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table  
Atoms consist of a central nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons in energy levels. The atomic number equals the number of protons, defining the element. The mass number is the sum of protons and neutrons. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. The periodic table arranges elements by atomic number into groups (vertical columns) with similar chemical properties and periods (horizontal rows) showing trends in atomic radius, ionisation energy, and electronegativity. For example, sodium (Na, atomic number 11) has one valence electron and is highly reactive, while neon (Ne, atomic number 10) has a full outer shell and is inert.  

## Chemical Bonding and Structure  
Compounds form when atoms bond to achieve stable electron configurations. Ionic bonding occurs through the transfer of electrons, creating positive and negative ions that attract, such as in sodium chloride (Na+Cl-), forming a giant ionic lattice. Covalent bonding involves sharing electron pairs between non-metal atoms, like in water (H₂O) where oxygen shares one electron with each hydrogen atom. Metallic bonding results in a 'sea' of delocalised electrons holding metal cations in a lattice, giving properties like electrical conductivity. The type of bonding and structure determines the physical properties of a substance, such as melting point and hardness.  

## Energy and Chemical Reactions  
Chemical reactions involve energy changes, either absorbed (endothermic) or released (exothermic). The energy change can be measured using calorimetry, where the temperature change of a known mass of water is recorded. For example, burning one mole of methane (CH₄) releases 890 kJ of energy. Activation energy is the minimum energy required for a reaction to occur. Catalysts speed up reactions by lowering this energy without being consumed. Collision theory explains that reactions happen when particles collide with sufficient energy and correct orientation, which is why increasing temperature or concentration typically increases reaction rates.  

## Waves and the Electromagnetic Spectrum  
Waves transfer energy without transferring matter and are characterised by amplitude, wavelength, frequency, and speed. The wave equation is speed = frequency × wavelength. The electromagnetic spectrum includes radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays, ordered by increasing frequency and decreasing wavelength. Visible light ranges from approximately 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red). Different types of waves have different properties; for instance, X-rays have high penetration and are used in medical imaging, while microwaves are absorbed by water and used in cooking. Wave behaviours include reflection, refraction, diffraction, and interference.  

## Forces and Motion  
Forces cause changes in motion, described by Newton’s laws. Newton’s first law states that an object remains at rest or in uniform motion unless acted upon by a net force. Newton’s second law is F = ma, where force equals mass times acceleration. For example, a 2 kg object accelerating at 3 m/s² experiences a force of 6 N. Newton’s third law states that for every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction, such as a rocket pushing gases downward to move upward. Friction is a force that opposes motion, and air resistance increases with speed. Understanding forces is essential for analysing motion from falling apples to planetary orbits.  

## Electricity and Magnetism  
Electric current is the flow of charge, measured in amperes (A), and is driven by a potential difference (voltage), measured in volts (V). Ohm’s law states that V = IR, where R is resistance in ohms (Ω). For instance, a circuit with a 12 V battery and a 6 Ω resistor has a current of 2 A. Magnetism arises from moving charges or the alignment of electron spins in materials. Electromagnets are created by passing current through a coil, with the strength increasing with current or turns. Fleming’s left-hand rule determines the force direction on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field. Generators convert mechanical energy to electrical using electromagnetic induction, while motors do the opposite.
