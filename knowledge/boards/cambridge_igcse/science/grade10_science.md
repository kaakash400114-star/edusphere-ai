# Cambridge IGCSE Grade 10 Science

## Atomic Structure and Periodic Table
Students learn that atoms consist of a central nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by electrons in shells. The atomic number defines the number of protons, which determines an element's identity. The mass number is the sum of protons and neutrons. Atoms are arranged in the periodic table in order of increasing atomic number. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties because they have the same number of outer-shell electrons. For example, sodium (Na) and potassium (K) in Group 1 both have one outer electron and are highly reactive metals.

## Chemical Bonding
Students understand that chemical bonds form to achieve a stable electron configuration. Ionic bonding occurs when electrons are transferred from a metal to a non-metal, creating oppositely charged ions that attract, such as sodium chloride (NaCl). Covalent bonding involves the sharing of electron pairs between non-metal atoms to form molecules, like in water (H₂O). The properties of the resulting substance are determined by the type of bonding; ionic compounds are typically crystalline solids with high melting points, while covalent molecules can be gases, liquids, or low-melting solids.

## Electricity and Magnetism
Students explore current electricity as the flow of charge, measured in amperes (A). Voltage (potential difference) is the driving force, measured in volts (V), and resistance opposes the current, measured in ohms (Ω). Ohm’s Law states that current = voltage / resistance. In series circuits, the total resistance is the sum of individual resistances, and the current is the same throughout. In parallel circuits, the total resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance, and the voltage across each component is the same. For example, a 12 V battery connected to a 4 Ω resistor results in a current of 3 A.

## Energy, Work, and Power
Students learn that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred or transformed. Work is done when a force causes an object to move, calculated as work = force × distance, measured in joules (J). Power is the rate of doing work or transferring energy, calculated as power = work / time, measured in watts (W). For example, a student lifting a 20 N book a height of 1.5 m does 30 J of work. If this takes 2 seconds, the power output is 15 W. Efficiency is the ratio of useful output energy to total input energy.

## Forces and Motion
Students study Newton’s laws of motion. An object will remain at rest or in uniform motion unless acted upon by a resultant force. Force equals mass times acceleration (F = ma). For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. Speed is distance travelled per unit time, while velocity includes direction. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. For example, a 1,000 kg car accelerating at 2 m/s² requires a forward force of 2,000 N. Friction opposes motion and can be calculated using the coefficient of friction and the normal reaction force.

## Waves
Students understand that waves transfer energy without transferring matter. They are classified as transverse (oscillations perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer) or longitudinal (oscillations parallel). The wave equation is speed = frequency × wavelength. Examples include light (transverse) and sound (longitudinal). Light travels at 3 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum. Sound travels at approximately 340 m/s in air. Reflection follows the law that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. Refraction occurs when waves pass from one medium to another, changing speed and direction.
