# American Grade 6 Science - NGSS/Common Core

## Matter: atoms-molecules

Everything around you is made of tiny, tiny bits called atoms, just like bricks are the tiny bits that build a big house. When atoms stick together, they form groups called molecules, which are like teams of bricks building a special shape; water is a molecule made of two small atoms and one bigger one. Scientists know that matter has a size and takes up space, so you can’t fit a giant book into a tiny pocket because it’s too big. Different kinds of atoms make different molecules, like salt is made of two atoms stuck together, and air is made of many tiny molecules all moving around.

## Cells: basic unit of life

All living things are made of tiny parts called cells, like bricks building a house. You can see different types of cells under a microscope, such as the skin cells on your hand or the ones that make up a leaf. A cell has a thin outside wall called a membrane to protect it and control what goes in and out, and inside is a gel-like material called cytoplasm. Inside the cytoplasm is a smaller part called the nucleus, which acts like the cell’s brain and tells the cell what to do. Cells are the smallest units that can be alive on their own or work together to make bigger things like plants and animals.

## Body systems

Students learn that the human body is made of many systems that work together to keep us alive and healthy. They explore the skeletal system, which is our body’s frame made of bones that protect our organs and help us move. The muscular system is made of muscles that pull on our bones to let us walk, run, and pick things up. The circulatory system acts like a delivery truck, with the heart pumping blood through vessels to carry oxygen and nutrients to all parts of the body. Students also discover how the digestive system breaks down food into small pieces the body can use for energy and growth.

## Forces and motion

In sixth grade, you learn about forces and how they make things move or stop. Forces are pushes and pulls, like when you kick a ball or pull a wagon. Objects need a force to start moving or to change how they move. Gravity is a force that pulls everything down, keeping you on the ground and apples falling from trees. Friction is a force that slows things down, like when you slide and your shoes stop you. If no forces are acting on something, it will keep moving in a straight line forever.

## Energy transfer

Energy is the ability to make things move or change, and it moves from warmer places to cooler places. Light energy from the sun warms the Earth, and heat energy travels from your warm hands to a cold glass of water, warming it up. Energy can also change from one form to another, like how a battery’s stored energy turns into light and sound in a flashlight. You can see energy transfer when you push a toy car; the energy from your hand moves to the car, making it go. These changes follow rules that help us understand how energy works in the world around us.

## Weather and climate patterns

Students learn to observe and measure basic weather components like temperature, wind speed, and precipitation. They use simple tools such as thermometers to find the temperature, anemometers to measure how fast the wind blows, and rain gauges to see how much rain falls. Students distinguish between weather, which changes daily, and climate, which is the typical weather pattern in a place over many years. For example, a rainy day is weather, but a region that is usually rainy is its climate. They also identify patterns in the seasons, understanding that seasons are caused by the tilt of the Earth as it orbits the Sun.

## Earth's layers

Students learn that Earth is like a big ball with different layers inside. The outer layer, the crust, is the hard part we live on, and it is made of rocks and soil. Below the crust is the mantle, which is thick and made of hot, sticky rock that can move slowly. The core is the very center of Earth and is made of two parts: the outer core is liquid metal, and the inner core is solid metal because it is very hot. Scientists learn about these layers by studying earthquakes and how their waves move through Earth.

## Human impact: resources

Students explore how people use and change Earth’s natural resources, like water, trees, and soil. They learn that resources can be renewable, such as solar power, or nonrenewable, like coal, which can run out. The class identifies ways people both help and harm the environment, for example, by recycling paper or by polluting rivers. They also discover how individuals can make a difference by conserving water or planting trees. These activities help students understand why protecting resources is important for the future.
