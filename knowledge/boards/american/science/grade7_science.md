# American Grade 7 Science - NGSS/Common Core

## Cells to systems

Students learn that all living things are made up of tiny parts called cells, which are the basic building blocks of life. They explore how cells group together to form tissues, like skin or muscle, and how tissues work together to create organs, such as the heart or stomach. Students understand that organs make up systems, like the digestive system breaking down food or the respiratory system helping you breathe. They compare plant and animal cells, noticing plant cells have a stiff wall for support and use sunlight to make food through a process called photosynthesis. This topic helps students see how small parts work together to keep living things functioning.

## Chemical reactions basics

In seventh grade science, students learn that a chemical reaction happens when substances mix and change into new ones, like when baking soda and vinegar create fizzing bubbles. They discover that chemical reactions need reactants to be present and follow the rule that matter is never created or destroyed, just rearranged, like when iron and oxygen make rust. Students identify signs of reactions, such as color changes, gas production, or temperature shifts, and learn to write simple word equations to show what happened, like "vinegar + baking soda → carbon dioxide + water." They explore how energy is either taken in or given off during reactions, just like when a campfire gives off heat to warm us up. These ideas help us understand how the world works, from cooking a meal to how our bodies use food for energy.

## Energy in ecosystems

In science class, you learn how energy flows through an ecosystem starting from the sun. Plants, like grass and flowers, use sunlight to make their own food in a process called photosynthesis. When animals, such as rabbits or insects, eat the plants, they get that energy, and when other animals, like foxes, eat those animals, the energy moves to them. Energy also moves when living things break down, like when fungi and bacteria break down dead leaves, returning nutrients to the soil. This energy flow follows a path called a food chain, and all living things depend on each other to get the energy they need to grow and survive.

## Rock cycle-plate tectonics

Students learn that rocks change in a cycle over very long time periods through processes like weathering, melting, and pressure. They explore how Earth’s surface is broken into giant moving sections called plates, which shift and cause mountains to form and earthquakes to happen. This movement, called plate tectonics, can push rocks deep underground, where they melt into magma, or push them up to form new land, like volcanoes. For example, when plates crash together, they can create a mountain range or cause an earthquake. The rock cycle shows how old rocks can become new rocks through these continuous, slow changes.

## Forces-motion Newton's laws

In seventh grade, you learn about how forces make things move or stay still. Newton’s first law says an object will not move or keep moving unless a force, like a push or pull, acts on it. Newton’s second law explains that a bigger force makes an object move faster; for example, kicking a soccer ball harder makes it go faster. You also discover that forces always happen in pairs: when one object pushes another, like your foot pushing the ground to walk, the ground pushes back equally. Finally, friction is a force that slows things down, like how the ground stops a rolling skateboard.

## Thermal energy

Students learn about thermal energy, which is the energy of moving particles that makes things feel hot or cold. They discover that heat always moves from warmer objects to cooler ones, like when your warm hand makes a cold cookie warmer. Thermal energy can change matter by causing it to melt (ice to water) or freeze (water to ice), and it can make things expand (like a balloon in the sun) or contract (like a metal lid in cold water). Students explore how different materials, like metal and wood, transfer heat at different speeds, helping us understand why a metal spoon gets hot faster in a cup of soup.

## Human impact: climate

Students learn how human activities, like burning fossil fuels for cars and factories, release gases that trap heat in the air, causing the planet to get warmer. This warming is called climate change, and it leads to big problems like melting ice at the poles, which makes sea levels rise and floods coastal cities. Changes in the weather become more common, with stronger storms, longer heatwaves, and less rain in some areas, making it hard for plants and animals to survive. We can help fight climate change by using less energy, recycling, and planting more trees to clean the air.

## Engineering design

In science class, you will learn to solve problems like an engineer by following a special design process. First, you think of a problem and imagine a solution, like building a bridge for toy cars to cross a gap. Then, you plan your design and draw it on paper before you start building. You use materials like paper, tape, or blocks to make a model of your idea and test it to see if it works. If your bridge falls, you figure out why and make it stronger before testing it again. This process helps you create better solutions just like real engineers do every day.
