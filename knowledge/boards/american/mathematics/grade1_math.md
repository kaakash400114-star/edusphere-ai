# American Grade 1 Mathematics - Common Core

## Counting to 120

Students learn to count by ones to 120, starting at any number less than 120, and can read and write numerals to represent a quantity of objects. They understand that the numbers 10 to 100 are built from tens and ones, for example, 23 is made of 2 tens and 3 ones. Students practice counting forward and backward from any number within the range, reinforcing the order and sequence of numbers. They also learn to compare numbers by determining which of two numbers up to 120 is greater than or less than the other.

## Place value: tens and ones

In first grade, students learn that two-digit numbers are made of tens and ones, understanding that the first digit shows how many groups of ten are in the number and the second digit shows how many leftover ones there are. For example, in the number 25, there are 2 tens and 5 ones, and they can represent this using objects like linking cubes or base-ten blocks to build the number visually. Students practice breaking numbers apart into tens and ones, such as recognizing that 36 is 3 tens and 6 ones, and they also learn to compare numbers by looking at the tens place first—if the tens are the same, they then look at the ones. By the end of this topic, children can read, write, and model two-digit numbers using their tens and ones, building a strong foundation for future place value work.

## Addition within 20

First-grade students learn to add and subtract within 20, building on skills from numbers to 10. They use strategies like counting on to solve problems, such as starting at 7 and counting up 4 to find 7 + 4. Children also understand that addition is putting groups together, like finding the total number of apples when you combine a group of 3 and a group of 5. They practice fluently adding numbers to make 10, a key skill for solving larger problems, such as knowing 8 + 2 equals 10. Through hands-on activities, students apply these concepts to real-world situations, like adding 7 stickers to a page that already has 8.

## Subtraction within 20

First graders learn to subtract numbers within 20 using objects, drawings, and equations. They understand subtraction as taking away or finding the difference, and they practice using strategies like counting back to solve problems. For example, to solve 8 - 3, a student can start at 8 and count back three numbers to find the answer, 5. Students also learn that subtracting zero from any number leaves the number unchanged, such as 9 - 0 = 9. They work on fluently subtracting within 10 and then use this understanding to subtract within 20.

## Word problems: add and subtract

First graders learn to solve word problems that involve adding and subtracting numbers within 20. They understand that addition combines two groups to find a total, like "I have 7 apples, and I get 3 more. How many apples do I have now?" Subtraction means taking away from a group, such as "I had 10 cookies, and I ate 2. How many are left?" Students use objects, drawings, or equations to show their thinking. They also practice figuring out if a problem requires adding or subtracting by looking for keywords like "in all" for adding or "left" for subtracting.

## Shapes and their attributes

Students learn to name and describe shapes like circles, squares, triangles, and rectangles. They identify the number of sides and corners each shape has, such as a square having four sides and four corners. Children also distinguish between defining attributes, like the number of sides, and non-defining attributes, like color or size. For example, a triangle always has three sides, but it can be any color or size. Students build and draw these shapes, focusing on their key characteristics to understand their basic properties.

## Measurement: length comparison

First graders learn to compare the lengths of two objects directly by placing them side by side to see which is longer or shorter. They also learn to indirectly compare lengths using a third object, such as knowing that a pencil is longer than a crayon because the pencil is longer than a ruler and the crayon is shorter than the same ruler. Students use measurement words like longer, shorter, longer than, and shorter than to describe the relationships between objects. For example, they can say that the book is longer than the eraser. They do not yet use standard units like inches or centimeters for these comparisons.

## Time to the hour and half-hour

In first grade, students learn to tell time to the hour and half-hour using analog clocks. They understand that the short hand, or hour hand, points to the number for the current hour, while the long hand, or minute hand, points to 12 for the hour and to 6 for the half-hour. For example, when the long hand is on 12 and the short hand is on 3, it is 3:00 o'clock. When the long hand is on 6 and the short hand is halfway between 3 and 4, it is 3:30. This foundational skill helps children connect the clock's face to the concept of time passing.

## Organize and compare data

First graders learn to sort objects into groups based on one attribute, such as color, shape, or size. They then organize these sorted groups into simple picture graphs and tally charts to display their data. Students compare the groups using words like “more,” “less,” and “the same,” and count how many objects are in each group to find the difference. For example, they might sort red and blue blocks and compare to see if there are more red blocks than blue blocks. This work helps children understand how to collect, organize, and talk about information in a clear way.
