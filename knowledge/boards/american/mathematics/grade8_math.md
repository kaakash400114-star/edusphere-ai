# American Grade 8 Mathematics - Common Core

## Irrational numbers-exponents

In eighth grade, you learn about irrational numbers, which are numbers that cannot be written as simple fractions, like pi or the square root of 2. You also explore exponents, including the rules for multiplying and dividing powers with the same base, such as 2³ × 2⁵ = 2⁸. You practice writing very large or small numbers in scientific notation, like 5,000 written as 5 × 10³. Additionally, you use square roots to solve problems and understand that some numbers, like √2, go on forever without repeating. These concepts help you see how numbers work in real-world situations.

## Scientific notation

In eighth grade, you learn how to write very big or very small numbers using scientific notation, which has two parts: a number between 1 and 10 and a power of 10. For example, 3,000 is written as 3 × 10³ because you move the decimal point three places to the left, and 0.004 is written as 4 × 10⁻³ because you move the decimal point three places to the right. When you multiply or divide numbers in scientific notation, you multiply or divide the first numbers and add or subtract the powers of 10. This helps you compare and compute huge numbers, like the distance between planets, quickly and easily. Scientific notation also makes it simpler to read and write measurements, such as the weight of a tiny grain of sand.

## Slope and linear equations

In Grade 8, students learn to find the slope of a line, which tells us how steep it is, using the formula "rise over run." They practice finding the slope from graphs, tables of values, and pairs of points. Students connect slope to the constant rate of change in real-world situations, like how far you travel over time. They also learn to write linear equations in different forms, such as slope-intercept form (y = mx + b), to represent a line’s slope and where it crosses the y-axis. For example, the equation y = 2x + 3 describes a line with a slope of 2 and a y-intercept at 3.

## Systems of equations

In eighth-grade math, students learn to solve systems of two linear equations with two variables, which means finding the point where two lines meet. They use three methods: graphing to find the intersection point, substitution to replace one variable, and elimination to add or subtract equations and eliminate a variable. For example, a problem might ask for the number of apples and bananas bought if apples cost $1 each and bananas cost $2, spending a total of $8, with a total of five fruits bought. Students practice writing these equations from real-world situations and checking if their solutions work in both original equations.

## Functions: linear-nonlinear

In eighth grade, you learn to see how things change and grow using functions. A function is a special rule that connects two sets of numbers, called inputs and outputs, so each input has exactly one output. You will discover linear functions, which have straight lines and change at a steady rate, like adding 2 apples every hour. You will also explore nonlinear functions, which have curves and change in different ways, like how a plant grows taller and faster over time. By studying both types, you can figure out patterns and make predictions about how things will change in the world around you.

## Transformations: congruence

Students learn about transformations that move shapes without changing their size or shape, making them congruent. They explore translations, which slide a shape in any direction; reflections, which flip a shape over a line; and rotations, which turn a shape around a fixed point. Using graph paper, students can slide a triangle up 3 spaces, flip a square over a vertical line, or turn a rectangle 90 degrees clockwise. They also discover that a shape and its transformed image are congruent because they have the same length sides and same angle measures.

## Pythagorean theorem

In 8th grade, students learn the Pythagorean theorem, which says that in a right triangle, the square of the long side (the hypotenuse) is equal to the sum of the squares of the other two sides. This means if a right triangle has sides of length *a* and *b*, and the longest side is *c*, then the rule is *a*² + *b*² = *c*². Students use this formula to find the length of any missing side when they know the other two; for example, if one side is 3 and another is 4, then 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25, so the longest side must be 5. They also solve real-world problems, like figuring out the length of a ladder leaning against a wall. By working with these ideas, students build a foundation for understanding more advanced math.

## Volume: cylinder-cone-sphere

In 8th grade, students learn how to find the volume of cylinders, cones, and spheres using specific formulas. For a cylinder, they use V = πr²h, where r is the radius of the circular base and h is the height. For a cone, they use V = 1/3πr²h, which is one-third the volume of a cylinder with the same base and height. For a sphere, they use V = 4/3πr³, where r is the radius. They practice calculating these volumes with real-world examples, like finding the space inside a can of soda (cylinder), an ice cream cone, or a basketball. Students also compare volumes to understand how these shapes relate to one another.

## Bivariate data-scatter plots

In Grade 8, students learn to use scatter plots to understand relationships between two sets of data called bivariate data. They practice creating these graphs by plotting paired numerical data as points on a coordinate plane to see if a pattern or trend, like clustering or a straight line, emerges. Students look at the direction of the association to describe if variables increase together (positive association), decrease together (negative association), or show no relationship at all. For example, they might plot hours studied per week against test scores to see if more hours are linked to higher scores.
