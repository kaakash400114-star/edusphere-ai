# American Grade 3 Mathematics - Common Core

## Multiplication facts 0-10

In third grade, students learn multiplication facts from 0 to 10, understanding that multiplication is a quick way to add equal groups. They explore the concept that any number multiplied by 0 equals 0, and any number multiplied by 1 stays the same. For example, 3 x 0 = 0 and 5 x 1 = 5. Students also practice facts like 2 x 4 = 8, which means adding 4 two times (4 + 4). By the end of the unit, they recognize patterns and relationships between numbers, such as 5 x 3 = 15 and 3 x 5 = 15, helping them build fluency and confidence.

## Division facts

In third grade, students learn to solve division problems by breaking a total number into smaller, equal groups. They understand division as the inverse operation of multiplication, using facts like 3 × 4 = 12 to find 12 ÷ 4 = 3. Children practice dividing numbers up to 100 by single-digit divisors, recognizing that division means sharing equally or finding how many groups can be made. For example, they might determine that 18 divided by 3 equals 6 because 18 can be split into 6 groups of 3. Students also explore the relationship between division and multiplication to check their answers.

## Fractions as numbers

In Grade 3, students learn that fractions are numbers that represent parts of a whole. They understand a fraction has a numerator, which tells the number of parts being considered, and a denominator, which tells the total number of equal parts the whole has been divided into. For example, in the fraction 3/4, the 3 is the numerator and the 4 is the denominator, showing 3 parts are taken from a whole split into 4 equal parts. Children learn to identify and write fractions that represent a specific part of a shape, like coloring three out of four equal parts of a circle. They also explore fractions on a number line, seeing how they fill the space between whole numbers.

## Fraction comparison

Third graders learn to compare fractions using symbols like <, >, and =. They compare fractions with the same denominator by looking at the numerators—for example, 3/4 is greater than 1/4 because 3 is bigger than 1. They also compare fractions with the same numerator, such as 2/3 and 2/5, recognizing that the smaller the denominator, the larger the fraction. Using visual models like fraction strips or circles, they understand why 1/2 is greater than 1/3. Students practice ordering fractions and solving simple word problems involving comparison.

## Time to the minute, elapsed

Students learn to tell and write time to the minute using analog and digital clocks. They identify the hour and minute hands and understand that each number on the clock face represents five minutes for the minute hand. To find elapsed time, students use a number line or a clock to count forward or backward in minutes and hours. For example, if a movie starts at 3:15 and ends at 4:45, students count the minutes from 3:15 to 4:00 and then to 4:45 to find that two hours and thirty minutes have passed.

## Mass and volume

Third graders learn to measure and compare the mass of objects using grams and kilograms, discovering that a small paperclip has a mass of about 1 gram while a dictionary might have a mass of 1 kilogram. They also explore volume by measuring the amount of liquid a container can hold using liters and milliliters, recognizing that a small juice box holds about 250 milliliters while a large water bottle holds about 1 liter. Students practice using tools like scales for mass and graduated cylinders for volume to find exact measurements. They compare the mass and volume of different objects to understand which is heavier or lighter and which holds more or less. Through hands-on activities, they build a strong foundation for understanding these important measurement concepts.

## Area of rectangles

In third grade, students learn to find the area of a rectangle by counting the number of square units that cover it without gaps or overlaps. They discover that multiplying the length by the width gives the area, for example, a rectangle 4 units long and 3 units wide has an area of 12 square units. Students use this understanding to solve real-world problems, like finding the area of a tiled floor or a piece of paper. They practice with concrete materials, such as square tiles or grid paper, to build a strong foundation in measuring area. The goal is to help students connect multiplication to the concept of area using clear, hands-on examples.

## Perimeter

In Grade 3, students learn to find the perimeter of a shape by adding the lengths of all its sides together. They discover that the perimeter is the total distance around the outside of a square, rectangle, or other polygons. For example, a square with sides of 4 inches each has a perimeter of 16 inches because 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 16. Children practice solving real-world problems, like finding the perimeter of a fence or a picture frame. They also compare perimeters to see which shapes have a longer or shorter distance around them.

## Data and graphs

Third graders learn to create and interpret different types of graphs to show information. They make bar graphs using a scale where each bar represents a specific number of objects or events, like counting how many students like each fruit. Pictographs are introduced, where pictures or symbols stand for data, such as drawing one apple for every student who likes apples best. Students also analyze line plots to measure and display information, like finding the lengths of pencils in inches. They practice reading these graphs to answer simple questions about the data, such as which item has the most or least votes.

## Rounding to 10 and 100

Third graders learn to round whole numbers to the nearest ten and one hundred using a number line to identify the closest ten or hundred. When rounding to the nearest ten, they look at the ones digit—if it is 5 or greater, they round up; if it is less than 5, they round down. For example, 47 rounds to 50 because the ones digit 7 is 5 or greater, while 42 rounds to 40 because the ones digit 2 is less than 5. When rounding to the nearest hundred, they check the tens digit—numbers like 250 round up to 300 because the tens digit 5 is 5 or greater, while 240 rounds down to 200 because the tens digit 4 is less than 5. This skill helps them estimate and work with larger numbers more easily.
