# American Grade 11 Mathematics - Common Core

## Algebra II: functions-quadratics

In Algebra II, students explore quadratic functions, which are rules that describe parabolas, or U-shaped graphs, and can be written in forms like standard (ax² + bx + c) and vertex form (a(x-h)² + k). They learn to solve quadratic equations using methods like factoring, completing the square, and the quadratic formula, which helps find the roots or x-intercepts of the function. For example, with the equation x² - 5x + 6 = 0, students can factor it to (x - 2)(x - 3) = 0, showing the roots are x = 2 and x = 3. Students also analyze key features such as the vertex, axis of symmetry, and maximum or minimum values to understand how the function behaves. These skills help model real-world situations, like calculating the height of a ball thrown into the air over time.

## Polynomial functions-rational expressions

In Grade 11 Mathematics, students learn to simplify, add, subtract, multiply, and divide rational expressions, which are fractions with polynomials in the numerator and denominator. They apply the same rules as for numerical fractions, such as finding a common denominator for addition and multiplication of numerators with multiplication of denominators. For example, to simplify (x² - 9)/(x + 3), students factor the numerator to (x + 3)(x - 3) and then cancel the common factor (x + 3), resulting in (x - 3). Students also solve rational equations by eliminating the denominators through multiplication and solving the resulting polynomial equation, checking for extraneous solutions that make any denominator zero.

## Radical-exponential functions

In eleventh-grade mathematics, students learn to rewrite expressions involving radicals and rational exponents using the properties of exponents, such as converting a square root to a power of one-half. They solve simple equations where a variable is inside a radical, like √x = 4, by squaring both sides to isolate x. Students also explore how graphs of radical functions, such as the square root of x, show a curve that starts at the origin and increases slowly. By understanding these connections, they can see how exponents and radicals are different forms of the same idea. This work helps prepare for solving more complex problems in advanced algebra and science.

## Logarithmic functions

Students learn that logarithms are the inverses of exponential functions, allowing them to solve for unknown exponents. They explore the key property that if \( b^y = x \), then \( \log_b x = y \), where \( b \) is the base. Students practice using the logarithm rules, like the product rule \( \log_b (xy) = \log_b x + \log_b y \) and the power rule \( \log_b (x^y) = y \log_b x \), to simplify expressions. They also convert between logarithmic and exponential forms, for example, rewriting \( \log_2 8 = 3 \) as \( 2^3 = 8 \). Finally, students apply these concepts to solve real-world problems involving exponential growth and decay.

## Trigonometric functions-identities

In Grade 11, students learn to use trigonometric identities to simplify and solve complex equations. They explore fundamental Pythagorean identities like sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 and learn how to derive new rules such as tan²θ + 1 = sec²θ. Students practice verifying identities by rewriting expressions using substitution and factoring, for example, proving that (1 + sinθ)(1 - sinθ) = cos²θ. They also apply sum and difference identities to calculate exact values, such as finding sin(45° + 30°). By mastering these relationships, students build tools to analyze periodic functions and solve real-world problems involving angles and distances.

## Sequences-series

In eleventh-grade math, students explore sequences and series, which are ordered lists of numbers that follow a specific pattern. They learn to identify and write explicit formulas for arithmetic sequences, where you add the same number each time, like 3, 7, 11, 15, and to find the sum of a finite arithmetic series using the formula n/2 times the first term plus the last term. For geometric sequences, students discover how each term is found by multiplying the previous term by a constant number, such as 2, 6, 18, 54, and they calculate the sum of a finite geometric series with the formula a1 times (1 - r^n) divided by (1 - r). This work builds a strong foundation for understanding functions and patterns used in advanced mathematics.

## Probability-statistics

In Grade 11, students learn to calculate probabilities using rules like addition and multiplication, understanding that probability ranges from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). They use the formula P(event) = number of favorable outcomes / total number of outcomes for simple cases, like finding the chance of picking a red marble from a bag. Students also analyze independent events where one outcome doesn’t affect another, like flipping a coin twice, and dependent events where the outcome changes, like drawing cards without replacement. They build on this to calculate compound probabilities and use random sampling to make predictions about larger groups, such as surveying a few students to guess the most popular school lunch.

## Conic sections

In eleventh grade, students learn about conic sections, which are special curves made by slicing a cone with a plane. They focus on circles, ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas, learning how to write their equations and graph them. For parabolas, students discover they have a vertex and a focus point, like how satellite dishes use parabolas to catch signals. They also explore how ellipses work with two foci and are used in building arches and explaining the paths of planets. The class uses coordinate geometry to connect algebra rules to these shapes, helping them see how math describes the world around them.
