# American Grade 6 Mathematics - Common Core

## Ratios and unit rates

In Grade 6, students learn that a ratio compares two quantities, like the number of apples to oranges in a fruit basket. They practice writing ratios as fractions (3/4) or with a colon (3:4) to show part-to-part relationships. A unit rate is a special ratio that compares a quantity to one unit of another, such as 60 miles per hour or $3 per pound. Students use division to find unit rates, like finding out how many minutes they take for each lap they run. They apply these skills to solve real-world problems, like doubling a recipe or comparing prices at the store.

## Divide fractions

In sixth grade math, you learn to divide fractions by multiplying by the reciprocal, which means flipping the second fraction upside down. For example, to divide ½ by ⅓, you multiply ½ by 3/1, which equals 3/2 or 1½. You also learn to divide whole numbers by fractions and fractions by whole numbers using the same method. The rule is simple: change the division sign to multiplication, flip the second fraction, and then multiply the tops and the bottoms. This helps you understand how many times one fraction fits into another.

## Negative numbers

Students learn that negative numbers are numbers less than zero, used to show values below a starting point, like temperatures below freezing. They practice comparing and ordering negative numbers on a number line, understanding that -5 is colder (less than) -1. Addition and subtraction with negative numbers follow specific rules: adding a negative is like moving left on the number line, while subtracting a negative is like moving right. Students also solve real-world problems using integers, such as finding the difference between temperatures or tracking scores in a game. These concepts build a foundation for understanding positive and negative relationships in math and everyday life.

## Absolute value

In grade 6, students learn about absolute value, which is a number’s distance from zero on a number line, and distance is always positive. They find the absolute value of a number by ignoring its sign, so the absolute value of -5 and 5 is both 5, because each is 5 spots from zero. Students practice writing the absolute value of a number using vertical bars, like |–3|, which equals 3. They also compare absolute values to see which number is farther from zero, such as understanding that |–8| is a larger distance than |3|. This helps them understand numbers without focusing on whether they are positive or negative.

## Expressions and equations

In grade 6, students learn to write and solve numerical and algebraic expressions, following the order of operations (PEMDAS). They explore how to use letters, called variables, to represent unknown numbers in simple equations. A key skill is writing expressions to solve real-world problems, like finding the cost of multiple items with different prices. For example, they might write 3x + 2 to represent the cost of 3 pencils and 2 erasers where x is the price of one pencil. Students practice solving one-step equations, such as x + 5 = 12, by isolating the variable to find the unknown number.

## Inequalities

In sixth grade, you learn about inequalities, which are math sentences that show when two things are not equal. You use special symbols like > (greater than), < (less than), ≥ (greater than or equal to), and ≤ (less than or equal to) to compare numbers. For example, 5 > 3 means five is more than three, and 4 ≤ 6 means four is less than or equal to six. You also practice solving inequalities to find numbers that make the sentence true, such as figuring out that x > 8 works for x in the problem x + 2 > 10. These rules help you understand how numbers relate to each other in order.

## Area: triangles-polygons

Students learn to find the area of triangles by using the formula: area equals the base times the height, divided by 2. They explore how a rectangle can be split into two equal triangles, so a triangle's area is always half the area of a rectangle with the same base and height. Learners also calculate the area of other polygons, such as quadrilaterals, by breaking them into simpler shapes like rectangles and triangles, then adding the smaller areas together. For example, a trapezoid can be divided into a rectangle and two triangles to find its total area. Students practice with real-world examples, like finding the area of a triangular flag or a playground shaped like a pentagon.

## Surface area and volume

In sixth grade, students learn to find the surface area and volume of three-dimensional shapes like rectangular prisms and cubes. They discover that surface area is the total area covering the outside of a shape, like the sum of all the faces of a box. For volume, students explore how much space a shape takes up, using formulas like length times width times height to calculate the amount of space inside. Concrete examples include wrapping a gift box (surface area) or filling it with sand (volume). This helps children understand real-world uses of measuring solids.

## Statistics: mean-median-mode

In grade 6, students learn to find the mean, median, and mode of a data set. The mean is the average, found by adding all numbers and dividing by how many there are, like finding the mean of 3, 7, and 8 is 6. The median is the middle number when numbers are in order, such as the median of 2, 4, and 9 is 4. The mode is the number that appears most often, like in 5, 5, and 8, the mode is 5. Students use these tools to understand and describe data.
