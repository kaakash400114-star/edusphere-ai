# American Grade 4 Mathematics - Common Core

## Place value to 1,000,000

In fourth grade, students learn to read and write numbers up to one million, understanding that each digit in a number has a specific place value like ones, tens, hundreds, thousands, ten thousands, hundred thousands, and millions. They practice breaking down numbers into their expanded form, for example, 345,678 is 300,000 + 40,000 + 5,000 + 600 + 70 + 8. Kids also learn to compare numbers using symbols like > (greater than), < (less than), and = (equal to), such as 500,000 is less than 600,000. They use place value to round numbers to the nearest ten, hundred, or thousand, like rounding 789 to the nearest ten is 790. This helps them build a strong foundation for working with larger numbers in future math.

## Multi-digit multiplication

In fourth grade, students learn to multiply multi-digit numbers by breaking them into smaller parts using place value, like solving 14 × 16 by thinking of it as (10 × 16) + (4 × 16). They master the standard algorithm, starting with the ones place and regrouping, to find products such as 23 × 5 = 115. Using models like arrays and area helps them visualize how partial products add together, like 3 × 40 = 120. Students apply these skills to solve real-world problems, such as calculating the total cost of 7 bags of apples that cost $24 each. By the end, they fluently multiply two 2-digit numbers, including cases with regrouping.

## Long division

Fourth-grade students learn to divide bigger numbers by breaking them into smaller, easier parts using a step-by-step method called long division. They learn to divide, multiply, subtract, and bring down numbers in a special order to find the answer, or quotient. For example, to solve 84 ÷ 4, they divide 8 by 4 to get 2, multiply 2 by 4 to get 8, subtract to get 0, bring down the 4, divide 4 by 4 to get 1, and write the final answer of 21. Students also practice dividing numbers with remainders, where a small number is left over after sharing equally. This skill helps them solve real-world problems like sharing items equally or finding groups within a larger set.

## Equivalent fractions

In fourth grade, students learn that equivalent fractions are different names for the same size piece of a whole, like 1/2 and 2/4. They understand that when you multiply or divide both the top number (numerator) and the bottom number (denominator) by the same number, you get an equivalent fraction, such as 3/6 being equal to 1/2 after dividing both numbers by 3. Kids use fraction models and number lines to see and prove these fractions are the same size, like showing 4/8 and 1/2 covering the same space. They also practice simplifying fractions by finding the biggest number that divides evenly into both the top and bottom, turning 6/9 into its simplest form, 2/3.

## Add-subtract fractions

In fourth grade, students learn to add and subtract fractions with the same denominator, like 1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4. They also learn to add and subtract fractions with different denominators by first finding a common denominator, such as changing 1/2 to 2/4 to add 1/4. Students use visual models like fraction strips or drawings to help them understand how fractions combine. They solve word problems involving fractions in real-life situations, like sharing food or measuring lengths. By the end of the topic, students can confidently solve addition and subtraction problems with fractions.

## Compare decimals

In fourth grade, students learn to compare decimals by looking at the value of each digit, starting from the left. They understand that digits in the tenths place are worth more than digits in the hundredths place. For example, when comparing 0.3 and 0.28, they see that the 3 in 0.3 is greater than the 2 in 0.28, making 0.3 the larger number. Students also practice using the symbols <, >, and = to show which decimal is greater, lesser, or if they are equal. They learn that adding zeros to the end of a decimal does not change its value, so 0.50 is the same as 0.5.

## Factors and multiples

In fourth grade, students learn about factors, which are numbers that multiply together to make another number, like 2 and 3 being factors of 6 because 2 × 3 = 6. They also explore multiples, which are the products of a number and other counting numbers, such as 10, 15, and 20 being multiples of 5 because 5 × 2 = 10, 5 × 3 = 15, and so on. Children practice finding all the factor pairs for numbers up to 100 and identify multiples to recognize number patterns. Using multiplication facts, they determine whether a number is prime, having only two factors (1 and itself), or composite, with more than two factors. For example, 7 is prime, while 8 is composite because its factors are 1, 2, 4, and 8.

## Angle measurement

In fourth grade, students learn to measure angles using a protractor and identify whether angles are acute, right, or obtuse. They recognize that angles are formed by two rays sharing a common endpoint and understand that a right angle measures exactly 90 degrees, like the corner of a square. Students also explore how angles can be added together to form larger angles, such as two 45-degree angles combining to make a 90-degree angle. They compare and order angles based on their size, using words like smaller, larger, and equal. By using real-world examples, like clock hands or door hinges, students build a strong understanding of angle measurement.

## Area and perimeter formulas

In fourth grade, students learn to find the area and perimeter of rectangles using formulas. For perimeter, they add the lengths of all four sides, like finding the fence around a playground. For area, they multiply the length times the width, such as counting square tiles on a floor. They also recognize that rectangles with the same area can have different perimeters, like two rugs with the same space but different borders. Students solve real-world problems, like how much fencing is needed for a garden or how much carpet covers a room. These skills help them understand space and measurement in everyday life.

## Unit conversions

In fourth grade, students learn to convert measurements within the same system, such as changing inches to feet or cups to pints. They use multiplication and division to solve problems, like finding how many cups are in 3 gallons or how many yards are in 12 feet. Students also practice converting units of time, such as hours to minutes, using a table or chart. They work with real-world examples, like measuring the length of a room in feet and inches, to make the math meaningful. The key skill is understanding how units relate to each other and using the correct operation to change one unit into another.
