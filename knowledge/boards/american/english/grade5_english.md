# American Grade 5 English Language Arts - Common Core

## Greek-Latin roots

In fifth grade, you will learn how many English words come from tiny word parts called Greek and Latin roots. You will discover that these little roots can grow into many big words, like the root "geo" meaning "Earth" which is in words like geography and geology. You will practice breaking longer words apart to find the root and then use that root to figure out what the word means. By learning these special word parts, you can unlock the meaning of new words you read and write.

## Theme-conflict-summary

In fifth grade, students learn to identify the main conflict in a story, which is the big problem the characters face, like a character trying to overcome a fear or solve a mystery. They also explore how the conflict helps reveal the theme, or the big lesson or message the author wants to share, such as the importance of kindness. By comparing the problem and how it is solved, students can summarize the key events and understand how the characters' actions lead to the theme. For example, in a story where a character works hard to win a race, the conflict is the race itself and the theme is that practice helps you succeed.

## Compare texts

Fifth graders learn to compare and contrast two different texts, like stories or articles. They look for similarities in the main ideas, such as both texts being about friendship or problem-solving. Students also find differences in the characters, settings, or how events happen in each story. For example, they might say two books about animals both talk about survival, but one animal lives in the ocean and the other in the forest. By doing this, students understand how authors tell the same kind of story in different ways.

## Conjunctions: correlative

In fifth grade, you learn to use correlative conjunctions, which are pairs of words that work together to connect equal parts in a sentence, like words, phrases, or clauses. The common pairs are either/or, neither/nor, and both/and, and each pair needs to match in number and structure. For example, you might write, “You can either play outside or read a book,” where “either” and “or” connect two equal activities. You also learn that these pairs always work as a team, so you must use both words to make the sentence make sense, like saying, “Both my dog and my cat love to play.” This helps you build strong, clear sentences.

## Verb tense shifts

In Grade 5, you learn to keep the verb tense the same when writing or telling a story to avoid confusing your reader. This means choosing one time, like the past ("walked") or the present ("walk"), and sticking with it throughout a sentence or story. If you are writing about something that already happened, you make sure all your verbs show that past time, like "Yesterday, I played soccer and scored a goal." Mixing tenses, such as "Yesterday, I play soccer," is a mistake because it mixes up when the action happened. Keeping the verb tense clear helps your reader understand exactly when your story is taking place.

## Comma usage

In fifth grade, you learn to use commas to separate items in a list of three or more things, like apples, bananas, and grapes. You also use commas to separate the name of the city from the state when writing an address, for example, Miami, Florida. Commas help separate parts of a sentence that are joined together with words such as “and,” “but,” or “so,” like in the sentence, “I wanted to play outside, but it started to rain.” You practice using commas correctly so your writing is easy to read and understand.

## Context clues: figurative language

In fifth grade, students learn to identify and interpret figurative language, such as similes, metaphors, and personification, using context clues to understand their meaning. A simile compares two things using "like" or "as," for example, "The cake was as light as a feather," and students use the clue "as light as" to picture something soft and airy. A metaphor directly states one thing is another, like "The classroom was a zoo," and context clues such as noisy or chaotic help students realize it means messy. Personification gives human traits to objects, such as "The wind whispered secrets," and words like "whispered" help students understand the wind is making a soft sound. By paying attention to descriptive words and phrases, students can figure out what figurative language really means.

## Evidence-based responses

In Grade 5, students learn to find evidence in a text to support their ideas about what they read. They practice quoting directly from the text and explaining what their quote means. Students also learn to make a claim and find at least two details from the text to prove it. For example, after reading a story, a student might say the main character is brave because they ran into the burning house to save a puppy, and they also stood up to a bully on the playground.

## Narrative techniques

Students learn to write stories with a clear beginning, middle, and end, making sure their characters have feelings and face problems. They practice using descriptive words to show how characters look and act, such as describing a character’s “big smile” instead of just saying they are “happy.” Writers also learn to make their stories interesting by adding dialogue, using words like “he said” or “she shouted.” Finally, students plan their stories by first thinking about a main idea and then adding small details that make the story exciting to read.

## Research reports

Fifth graders learn to write research reports by gathering facts from books and websites to share information about a topic. They organize their writing with a clear introduction that states their topic, several body paragraphs with details, and a conclusion that wraps up their ideas. Students practice writing in their own words and including facts, numbers, or examples to make their reports strong and true. They also learn to create a simple bibliography to list the sources they used for their information, giving credit to the people who helped them learn.
