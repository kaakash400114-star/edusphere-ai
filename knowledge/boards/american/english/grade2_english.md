# American Grade 2 English Language Arts - Common Core

## Phonics: vowel teams

Students learn that two vowels can work together to make one long sound, like the /a/ sound in "rain" or the /ee/ sound in "team." They explore how "magic e" makes a short vowel say its name, such as in "cake" where the 'e' makes the 'a' say /ay/. Children practice reading and spelling words with vowel teams like "oa" in "boat" and "ai" in "mail." They also learn that some vowel teams can make new sounds, like the /oi/ sound in "coin." By recognizing these patterns, students can decode and spell new words more confidently.

## Fluency passages

Second graders learn to read aloud with speed and smoothness, paying attention to punctuation and using a natural voice. They practice reading grade-level texts at a steady pace to build fluency, which helps them understand better. Students also work on grouping words into meaningful phrases to improve expression, such as reading "The big brown bear" as one phrase instead of separate words. Teachers use repeated readings of short passages, like "The cat sat on the mat," to help students gain confidence and read more easily. This practice helps children focus on the story rather than struggling with individual words.

## Common and proper nouns

Students learn to tell the difference between common nouns and proper nouns. Common nouns name general things like "dog" or "school," while proper nouns name specific people, places, or things and always begin with a capital letter, such as "Max" or "Park Street." They practice identifying and sorting these nouns to understand that proper nouns give special names to things, just like their own names. This helps them see why we capitalize the first letter of a name but not for a general thing. Students learn to use these rules in their own writing to make it clear and correct.

## Past-tense verbs

Second graders learn to use past-tense verbs to show that an action already happened. They change most verbs by adding -ed to the end, such as jumping to jumped or walking to walked. For some words ending with an e, they just add a d, like hiked or baked. Students also learn to add -ing to verbs that show actions happening right now, like running or playing. These new words help them tell stories about things they did yesterday and what they are doing today.

## Adjectives and adverbs

In second grade, children learn to use adjectives to describe nouns, telling us what kind or how many something is. They also learn to use adverbs to describe verbs, telling us how someone does something or when it happens. For example, they might say "The big dog ran quickly" or "She smiled happily." Students practice choosing between adjectives and adverbs to make their sentences more interesting and clear. By the end of the year, they can identify and use these words to add details to their writing.

## Simple sentences vs fragments

In second grade, students learn that a sentence must have a subject, like "The cat," and a predicate, or what the subject does, to tell a complete thought, such as "The cat meows." A sentence fragment is a group of words that is missing either a subject or a predicate and does not make sense on its own, like "On the chair." Children practice writing full sentences by adding subjects or predicates to incomplete fragments, turning "Went to the park" into "We went to the park." They also learn to identify fragments by checking if a sentence can stand alone and make sense, like recognizing "Jumped high" is not a complete sentence without a "who" or "what" doing the jumping.

## Commas in lists

Second graders learn to use commas to separate words in a list. When you have three or more items in a row, you put a comma between each item to show they are all part of the same group. For example, you would write "I like apples, bananas, and grapes" to show the three different fruits. A small comma helps readers see each item clearly in a long list. This rule helps make your writing easy to read and understand.

## Apostrophes in contractions

In second grade, we learn that apostrophes help us write two short words as one shorter word, called a contraction. When we join words, we take out some letters and put an apostrophe in their place, like in “do not” becoming “don’t” where the apostrophe stands for the missing “o.” We also see this in “can not” turning into “can’t” and “I will” becoming “I’ll,” making our writing easier and faster. These little marks show where letters are left out so we can read and write smoothly.

## Story elements: character-setting

Students learn to identify and describe the key story elements of character and setting. They understand that a character is a person or animal in a story, like a friendly bear or a brave knight. Students also learn that the setting is where and when a story happens, such as a forest or a school at lunchtime. By looking at illustrations and listening to stories, children can point to and name the characters and settings. This helps them better understand and enjoy the stories they read.

## Informative writing: topic sentence

Students learn to write a topic sentence that tells the main idea of their writing, just like a door to a room shows what is inside. A good topic sentence comes near the beginning and uses specific words to share one clear thought, such as "My favorite animal is the lion" or "Puppies are playful and friendly." It guides the reader to understand what the rest of the writing will be about, like a map shows where to go. Students practice writing their own topic sentences about things they know well, like their family or a favorite toy. This helps them organize their ideas and share information clearly with others.
