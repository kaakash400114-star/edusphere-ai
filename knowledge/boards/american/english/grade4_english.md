# American Grade 4 English Language Arts - Common Core

## Theme and summarizing

In fourth grade, students learn to find the theme of a story, which is the big lesson or message about life they can figure out from the characters and events. They practice by reading stories and thinking, “What does the author want me to learn?” Students also get better at summarizing by telling the most important parts of a text in their own words, leaving out small details. For example, after reading about a character who tries hard to win a race, a student might summarize, “The story taught me that never giving up can help you succeed.” They learn that a good summary is short, includes the main idea, and covers the beginning, middle, and end.

## Point of view

In Grade 4, students learn to identify and explain the first-person point of view, where a narrator uses words like “I,” “me,” or “my” to tell the story from their own perspective. They can also recognize third-person point of view, where a narrator uses words like “he,” “she,” or “they” to tell the story about other people. Students practice determining whose thoughts and feelings are being shared in a text. For example, in a story starting with “I was nervous on the first day of school,” the student can identify the first-person narrator. By understanding these different viewpoints, students can better understand a story’s events and characters.

## Inference

Fourth graders learn to make inferences by using clues from the text and what they already know. An inference is a smart guess that helps us understand what the author means without being told directly. For example, if a character has a wet umbrella and is smiling, we can infer that it just stopped raining. Students practice by reading short stories and explaining why they think certain things are true, using words like "probably" or "maybe." This skill helps them become stronger readers who can think deeply about what they are reading.

## Relative pronouns

Students learn to use relative pronouns like who, whom, whose, which, and that to connect sentences and add details to their writing. These special words help describe a person, animal, place, or thing, as in "The dog that barks lives next door" or "The girl whose bike is red is my friend." They practice replacing two short sentences with one longer sentence that includes a relative pronoun, such as turning "I saw a man. He was tall" into "I saw the man who was tall." This skill makes writing more interesting and helps readers understand the connections between ideas clearly.

## Progressive verb tenses

In Grade 4, students learn to understand and use progressive verb tenses to describe actions happening now, in the past, or in the future. A progressive tense uses a form of the verb "to be" (am, is, are, was, were) combined with a verb ending in "-ing" to show that an action is ongoing. For example, "The dog is barking" shows an action happening right now, while "The dog was barking" shows it happened in the past. They also learn to talk about future actions, such as "The dog will be barking later." By using these tenses, students can tell more detailed and interesting stories.

## Modal auxiliaries

Fourth graders learn that modal auxiliaries are special helping words that change the meaning of a verb. They use these words, like can, should, will, and may, to show ideas such as ability ("She can jump high"), necessity ("You should listen carefully"), or future events ("We will play later"). Students practice choosing the right modal auxiliary to complete sentences so the meaning is clear. They also learn that these helper words always come before the main action word, like when we say "He may go outside" instead of "He may goes outside.

## Prepositional phrases

In fourth grade, you will learn that prepositional phrases start with a preposition like in, on, or under and describe where or when something is. A prepositional phrase is like a little team that tells us more about a noun, and it always ends with a noun or pronoun. For example, the phrase "in the park" tells us where the dog is playing, and "before lunch" tells us when we go outside. You will practice finding prepositional phrases in sentences to understand how they add important details about places and times.

## Capitalization rules

In fourth grade, you learn to capitalize the first word in every sentence, including questions and commands. You also capitalize the pronoun “I” and all proper nouns, such as the names of specific people, like Sarah or Mr. Johnson, places, like Texas or the Amazon River, and holidays, like Thanksgiving or Halloween. Remember to capitalize important words in titles of books, movies, and songs, like The Cat in the Hat or Frozen, but smaller words like “the” or “of” are usually not capitalized. You practice using these rules to make your writing clear and organized.

## Punctuation for dialogue

In fourth grade, students learn to correctly use punctuation to show when a character is speaking, which includes putting commas before dialogue tags like “said” or “whispered.” They also discover that capital letters start the first word of spoken words and that ending punctuation like a period, question mark, or exclamation mark goes inside the closing quotation mark. For example, a student might write, “Can we play outside?” asked Maya. Finally, they practice using quotation marks to set apart what a character says from the rest of the sentence, making their writing clear and easy to read.

## Narrative writing

In fourth grade, students learn to write structured narratives with clear beginnings, middle parts, and endings. They practice using dialogue and descriptive words to make their stories interesting for readers, like adding "the sun was hot" to describe a setting. A key rule is to organize events in a logical order, using words such as first, next, and then to show sequence. Students also learn to include a problem and a solution in their stories, such as a character losing a toy and then finding it with help. These writing skills help children share their ideas and experiences in a way that others can easily understand.
