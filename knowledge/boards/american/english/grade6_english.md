# American Grade 6 English Language Arts - Common Core

## Central idea and evidence

In sixth grade English, students learn to find the central idea of a text, which is the most important message the author wants you to understand. They practice determining the central idea by identifying key details that support it, such as who, what, where, and why in a story. For example, in a story about a team that keeps practicing to win, the central idea is "practice helps you succeed," and the evidence is the team practicing every day and not giving up. Students also learn how these ideas and details build upon each other to create the main point. Understanding this helps students explain what a text is mostly about using specific examples from the story.

## Text structure

Students learn to identify different ways information is organized in texts, like cause and effect, problem and solution, and sequence or time order. They practice spotting clue words such as "because," "first," and "then" to help them understand these structures. For example, in a story about planting a garden, a student can point out that the problem is the seeds not growing and the solution is watering them every day. By recognizing these patterns, readers can better understand how events connect and what the main ideas are. This skill helps students find key details and summarize the text more easily.

## Theme development

Students learn to find the main idea, or theme, in stories by asking what the lesson is. They identify how characters’ feelings and actions show the big message, like sharing makes friends happy. Students use details from the text, such as a character helping others, to explain the theme. They compare themes in different stories, like both stories teaching kindness. Students can write short sentences about the theme using evidence from the story to support their ideas.

## Pronouns: case

Students learn that pronouns change form based on their job in a sentence. When the pronoun is the one doing the action, we use the subject case, like "He" or "She" ("He runs fast"). If the pronoun is receiving the action, we use the object case, like "him" or "her ("I passed him the ball"). They also practice with pronouns that follow prepositions (words like to, for, with), using words like "me" or "us" ("The gift is for me"). Students practice choosing the correct pronoun form by saying the sentence aloud to hear what sounds right.

## Intensive pronouns

Students in Grade 6 learn that intensive pronouns are used to emphasize a noun or another pronoun in a sentence. These pronouns always end in –self or –selves, like myself, himself, or themselves, and they come right after the word they are renaming. For example, in the sentence, “I fixed the bike myself,” the word myself emphasizes that I did the action all by myself. The rules are simple: the intensive pronoun must rename the subject or object of the sentence, and it cannot be removed without changing the meaning. Students practice identifying and correctly using these special pronouns to make their writing clearer and stronger.

## Commas-clauses

In sixth grade, you learn to use commas correctly with introductory clauses like "After the game" to make your writing clear. A comma helps separate a short phrase that comes before the main idea, such as "Before dinner, we washed our hands." You also practice using commas with dependent clauses that cannot stand alone, like "When the bell rang" to connect it to a full sentence. These small pauses help readers understand your ideas better by showing where one thought ends and another begins. For example, "Although it was raining, we played outside" uses a comma to link the two parts.

## Figurative language

In sixth grade, students learn to identify and interpret figurative language, such as similes, metaphors, personification, and hyperbole, which help authors make writing more vivid and interesting. A simile uses “like” or “as” to compare two things, such as “Her smile is as bright as the sun,” while a metaphor directly states something is something else, like “He is a shining star.” Personification gives human qualities to non-human things, for example, “The wind whispered secrets,” and hyperbole uses extreme exaggeration, like “I’m so hungry I could eat a horse.” Students analyze how these devices enhance meaning and imagery in texts.

## Argument writing: claims-evidence

Students learn to state their opinion, which we call a claim. A claim is a clear statement like "Dogs make better pets than cats." Next, they find strong reasons to back up their claim, which are the evidence. For example, evidence could be "Dogs can learn tricks" or "Dogs like to play fetch." Students practice putting their claim and evidence together to explain their thinking. They learn to talk about why their evidence supports their claim, like saying "My evidence shows dogs can play games because I saw a dog catch a ball." This helps people understand and believe their opinion.

## Narrative craft

In Grade 6, students learn to build exciting stories by planning characters with clear motivations and problems. They practice writing a beginning that introduces the setting and characters, a middle with rising action that builds excitement, and an end where the problem is solved. Students use dialogue and descriptive words to make their stories feel real and show what characters are thinking and feeling. They also learn to organize their ideas using a clear sequence of events, making sure the story moves smoothly from one part to the next. For example, a student might write about a character who wants to win a race but faces a challenge along the way.

## Media literacy

Students in Grade 6 learn to identify different types of media, like books, websites, and videos, and understand who created them. They practice finding the main idea and key details in a news article or a short video to figure out what information is most important. Students also learn to notice if a source might be trying to persuade them by using strong words or pictures. They practice using simple search tools to find facts and check if a website is a good source for their class project.
