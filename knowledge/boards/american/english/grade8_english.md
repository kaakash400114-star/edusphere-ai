# American Grade 8 English Language Arts - Common Core

## Cite strong evidence

In eighth grade, you will learn to find strong evidence in a text to support your ideas about it. This means looking for specific details, like what a character says or does, that proves your point. You also learn to tell the difference between strong evidence, which is detailed and directly supports a claim, and weak evidence, which is vague or just an opinion. For example, if you say a character is brave, strong evidence would be describing a time they faced danger to help someone else. You will practice writing sentences that connect your idea to this specific proof.

## Theme-central idea development

Students learn to identify a story’s theme, which is the big lesson or message the author wants you to understand. They practice this by thinking about the main character’s important choices and how those choices lead to a problem and its solution. For example, in a story where a character learns to be honest, the theme might be “honesty is always the best choice.” Students also compare how different characters face similar problems to understand how authors build a central idea throughout a text. This helps them see how small details in a story connect to its bigger message.

## Analyze arguments

Students learn to identify an author’s claim and find the reasons and evidence used to support it in a text. They compare how different authors build their arguments using reasons and evidence and decide which points are the strongest. Students also examine how word choice affects the tone and persuasiveness of an argument. They practice explaining how an author’s reasons and evidence support their claim, looking for both stated and unstated assumptions. For example, students might analyze a speech about why a new playground is needed by listing the author’s reasons and the facts they use to back them up.

## Verb voice: active-passive

Students learn to identify whether a verb shows active voice, where the subject performs the action, or passive voice, where the subject receives the action. In active voice, the subject comes first and acts directly on the object, like “The cat chased the mouse.” In passive voice, the object becomes the subject, and the action is done to it using a form of “be” with the past participle, like “The mouse was chased by the cat.” Students practice changing sentences from active to passive voice to see how this change affects the focus and impact of the sentence. They also learn to recognize the appropriate contexts for using each voice to communicate their ideas clearly and effectively.

## Verb moods

Students learn that a verb’s mood shows the writer’s attitude toward a statement. The four main moods are indicative, which states facts or asks questions, such as “We play soccer.” Imperative mood gives commands, like “Sit down.” Subjunctive mood shows doubt or wishes, as in “I wish she were here.” Conditional mood expresses a result if something happens, for example, “If it rains, we will stay inside.” Mastering verb moods helps students communicate more precisely and effectively in their writing.

## Punctuation for meaning

Students in grade eight learn to use punctuation, like commas and semicolons, to show the connections between ideas in a sentence. They practice using commas to separate items in a list, to set off introductory words, and to connect two independent clauses with a conjunction. Students also learn to use semicolons to join two closely related independent clauses without a conjunction, and to use colons to introduce a list or an example. Mastering these punctuation rules helps writers make their sentences clear, organized, and easy for readers to understand.

## Analyze figurative meaning

Students learn to find figurative language, like metaphors and similes, and figure out what they really mean. A simile uses “like” or “as” to compare things, such as “her hair is as soft as a cloud,” meaning it feels very smooth. A metaphor says one thing is another thing, like “time is a thief,” meaning time steals moments from us. They also study personification, which gives human actions to things, like “the wind whispered,” to understand the feeling it creates. By identifying these tools, students can discover the deeper message and the author’s special way of describing the world.

## Write arguments: claims-counterclaims

In eighth grade, students learn to write strong arguments by first making a clear claim that states their opinion on a topic, like “Schools should start later in the morning.” They then think about what others might disagree with, which is called a counterclaim. To write a good argument, students must provide evidence and reasons to support their claim and also address the counterclaim by explaining why their opinion is stronger. For example, they might say, “Some people worry about after-school activities, but starting later helps students learn better.” This shows they can prove their point while considering other views.

## Narrative: pacing-dialogue

Students learn to use pacing and dialogue to make their stories exciting and easy to read. Pacing means making some parts of the story fast with short sentences and other parts slow with more details. Dialogue is what characters say, and it shows their feelings and moves the story along. For example, a character might shout, "Hurry up!" for fast pacing or whisper, "I'm scared," to show feelings. Writers also use action tags, like "he said" or "she asked," to show who is talking and keep the story flowing smoothly.

## Research-synthesis

In eighth grade, you learn to find information from different sources like books and websites, but you must also check that the sources are trustworthy and reliable. After gathering facts, you practice putting together what you learned into a short report or presentation that explains your main idea with clear details. A key skill is giving credit to where you found your information by writing down the titles and authors so others can check your work too. You also learn to organize your facts in a logical order, such as putting similar ideas together, so your audience can easily understand your topic.
