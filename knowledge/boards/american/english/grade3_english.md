# American Grade 3 English Language Arts - Common Core

## Prefixes and suffixes

Students learn that prefixes are small word parts added to the beginning of a base word to change its meaning, such as adding un- to happy to make unhappy. They also learn that suffixes are small word parts added to the end of a base word to change its meaning or its job in a sentence, like adding -s to cat to show more than one. A key rule for suffixes is changing the final y of a base word to i before adding -es, as in baby becoming babies. Students practice identifying base words, prefixes, and suffixes to understand how words are built.

## Main idea and details

Third graders learn to find the main idea, which is the big idea or important message in a story or article. They discover that supporting details are smaller pieces of information that explain the main idea, like clues that support the big idea. Students practice identifying the main idea by looking at the first and last sentences of a paragraph, as writers often state it there. For example, in a story about a dog learning a new trick, the main idea might be “the dog worked hard to learn how to fetch,” and details would include “the dog practiced every day” and “the dog never gave up.”

## Context clues

In third grade, students learn to use context clues to find the meaning of unknown words when they are reading. A context clue is a hint in the words around an unknown word that helps figure out its meaning. For example, if a sentence says, "The curious kitten crept silently to the yarn," the word "curious" is a clue that tells us the kitten is very interested and wants to explore. Students also learn to look for definitions, synonyms, or antonyms in the same sentence or nearby sentences to understand new words. Using these clues helps them become better readers and understand stories more deeply.

## Nouns: singular-plural-possessive

Third graders learn to identify and change singular nouns, like cat, into plural nouns, like cats. They discover that most plurals are made by adding -s to the end of a singular noun. To show that something belongs to someone, they learn to make possessive nouns by adding an apostrophe and an -s, such as the cat's toy. They practice writing sentences using singular, plural, and possessive nouns to understand the different roles nouns can play. For example, they can write about one dog, many dogs, and the dog's bone.

## Verb tenses

Students learn to recognize and use past, present, and future verb tenses to show when an action happens. They understand the present tense tells about actions happening now, like "walk" or "play." The past tense shows actions that already happened, often by adding -ed to the verb, such as "walked" or "played." The future tense describes actions that will happen later, using the word "will" before the verb, like "will walk" or "will play." Children practice choosing the right tense to make their sentences clear and accurate.

## Subject-verb agreement

In third grade, students learn that a subject and its verb must agree, meaning the verb changes to match whether the subject is singular or plural. For example, a singular subject like "the cat" takes a singular verb: "The cat jumps." A plural subject like "the cats" takes a plural verb: "The cats jump." They also practice this rule with subjects like "I" and "you," which always use plural verbs, such as "I play" or "You run." Students practice finding the subject to make sure the verb matches correctly. This skill helps them write clear and correct sentences.

## Simple and compound sentences

In third grade, students learn to write simple sentences with one subject and one predicate, like "The cat sleeps." They also learn to combine two simple sentences into one compound sentence using words such as "and," "but," or "or." Compound sentences show how two ideas are connected, as in "I like to read, and I like to play." Students practice identifying and writing both types of sentences in their own writing. This helps them express their ideas more clearly and make their writing more interesting for readers.

## Commas in dialogue

In third grade, students learn to use commas correctly when characters in a story speak. A comma is placed right before the first part of the dialogue, like in the sentence, "Let's go to the park," said Sarah. They also learn to put a comma inside the quotation marks when the dialogue tag comes first, for example: "I love to read," whispered Tom. Commas help separate the speaker's words from the rest of the sentence so it is easy to understand who is talking. Students practice finding and adding these commas in sentences to make their writing clear and organized.

## Opinion writing

Third-grade students learn to write opinion pieces that state a clear opinion, supply reasons that support the belief, and use linking words like "because" and "also" to connect ideas. They are expected to provide a sense of closure by restating their opinion. For example, a student might write, "Recess should be longer because it helps us exercise, and it is fun to play with friends." Writers practice organizing their thoughts with an introduction, at least two supporting reasons in the middle, and a conclusion. This teaches them to structure their thinking and persuade others with clear, simple evidence.

## Research skills

Third graders learn how to find information using books and websites to answer questions. They practice naming the author and title to show who created a source and where it came from. Students sort facts from opinions by checking if something can be proven true or false. They use simple tools, like a table or chart, to organize facts they learn, such as writing down animal habitats in a science project. When sharing their findings, they tell others what they discovered in their own words.
