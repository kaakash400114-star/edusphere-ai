# American Grade 9 English Language Arts - Common Core

## Close reading: theme-character

Students learn to find the main idea, or theme, of a story by paying close attention to what a character says, does, and thinks. They figure out how a character’s struggles, like facing a fear, can show the bigger lesson of the story. Students practice by reading a short story and writing down evidence, such as a character’s actions, to prove the theme is never giving up. This helps them understand that the character’s journey often reveals the story’s most important message.

## Rhetorical devices

In ninth grade, students learn to identify and analyze rhetorical devices in texts, such as metaphors, similes, and personification, which authors use to make writing more vivid and persuasive. They examine how specific word choices, like hyperbole or alliteration, create meaning and influence an audience. Students also study the structure of arguments, including claims, evidence, and counterclaims, to understand how writers build their cases. For example, they might analyze how a persuasive speech uses repetition to emphasize a key point. By recognizing these techniques, students improve their ability to interpret both written and spoken messages effectively.

## Text structures

In ninth-grade English, you will learn to recognize and analyze the different ways authors organize information in texts, such as cause and effect, compare and contrast, and problem and solution. You will practice identifying the key words and signal phrases, like "because" or "on the other hand," that reveal an author’s chosen structure. For example, you will learn to spot a problem-solution structure by finding a problem being presented and then the author's proposed answer to it. Understanding these text structures helps you better understand the author's main ideas and how the information connects.

## Grammar: clauses-phrases

In ninth grade, you learn to identify and use different kinds of clauses and phrases in sentences. A clause has a subject and a verb and can stand alone as a sentence, like “The dog barked,” or it can be part of a larger sentence, like “The dog that barked belongs to my neighbor.” A phrase is a group of words that work together but do not have a subject and a verb, such as “under the big oak tree” or “running quickly to the bus.” You also practice combining these clauses and phrases to make your sentences more interesting and detailed, making your writing stronger and clearer.

## Punctuation mastery

Students in ninth grade master advanced punctuation to clarify complex sentences and control the pace and flow of their writing. They learn to correctly use semicolons to connect two closely related independent clauses, as well as colons to introduce a list, example, or explanation that elaborates on the sentence before it. Pupils also practice integrating dashes for emphasis and parentheses for nonessential information. Additionally, ninth graders refine their use of commas with introductory elements, complex series, and coordinating conjunctions to correctly join independent clauses and avoid run-on sentences.

## Sentence variety

Students in ninth grade English learn to create sentence variety by using different structures and lengths to make their writing more interesting. This includes combining two simple sentences into one compound sentence using a comma and a conjunction like "and" or "but," such as "I finished my homework, and I played video games." They also practice adding descriptive details to their sentences to paint a clearer picture for the reader. Mastering this skill helps students move beyond simple, choppy sentences to create a smoother and more engaging flow in their writing.

## Evidence-based essays

Students learn to write evidence-based essays by stating a clear opinion, like "recess should be longer," and then finding reasons and examples from stories or articles to support that claim. They practice writing one reason per paragraph, such as "longer recess helps kids focus better," and including facts from a text to prove it. Students also learn to explain how their examples connect back to their main opinion to strengthen their argument. By following this structure, they can persuade readers using strong proof from what they have read.

## Narrative technique

Students learn to identify and analyze narrative techniques in stories. This includes recognizing how an author’s point of view, like first person (“I”) or third person (“he”), changes how a story is told. They also study the structure of a plot, learning about key events such as the exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and resolution. For example, they might see how a character’s actions reveal their personality. Students explore the use of dialogue to show how characters feel and interact, noticing the difference between what a character says and what they really mean.

## Research-synthesis

Students learn to find important facts from multiple sources like books, articles, and websites. They must put these facts together in their own words to create new information. A key rule is to always credit the original source to show where the facts came from, which prevents copying. For example, if a student learns about frogs from three different books, they would write one report that uses information from all three books. The goal is to show how different ideas connect to build a bigger picture on a topic.

## Speaking-listening skills

In grade 9, students learn to build on their speaking and listening skills by participating in collaborative discussions, both one-on-one and in groups, where they express ideas clearly and respectfully. They practice listening actively to understand different perspectives and to gather and synthesize information from various sources, such as speakers, texts, and digital media. A key focus is on analyzing the purpose, structure, and credibility of a speaker's argument by asking specific questions and evaluating evidence. For example, after listening to a community leader talk about environmental issues, students might discuss the main points and evaluate the evidence used to support their claims. Students also learn to prepare well-structured presentations using multimedia elements to present their own findings and ideas effectively to an audience.
