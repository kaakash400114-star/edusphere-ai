# German (Lehrplan) Grade 11 Science


## Quantum Mechanics and Atomic Structure
Students explore the quantum model of the atom, focusing on wave-particle duality and the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle. They calculate the energy of electron transitions using the formula E = -2.18 x 10^-18 J (Z^2/n^2), where Z is the atomic number and n is the principal quantum number. Practical examples include analysing the emission spectra of hydrogen and comparing theoretical predictions with observed spectral lines for elements like sodium, which emits a characteristic doublet at 589.0 and 589.6 nm.

## Thermodynamics and Energy Transfer
The curriculum covers the laws of thermodynamics, particularly the First Law (ΔU = Q + W) and entropy. Students solve problems involving heat engines and refrigeration cycles, applying the formula for efficiency, η = 1 - (T_cold/T_hot). German examples include calculating the energy consumption of a high-speed ICE train moving at 300 km/h and analysing the heat loss from a traditional Bavarian brewery during the winter months, emphasizing the practical applications of thermodynamic principles.

## Organic Chemistry and Polymer Science
Students analyse the fundamental classes of organic compounds and apply IUPAC nomenclature rules to systematically name monomers and polymers, including complex macromolecules. The core principles of addition and condensation polymerisation are studied, with a focus on reaction mechanisms involving initiation, propagation, and termination steps. This knowledge is applied to examine the chemical structures of materials like polypropylene, used in German banknotes for its durability, and polylactic acid (PLA), synthesised for sustainable Christmas market packaging. The relationship between a polymer's molecular structure—such as branching or cross-linking—and its macroscopic properties, such as tensile strength or thermal stability, is a central concept.

## Electromagnetic Induction and Electrical Power
The curriculum focuses on Faraday's Law of electromagnetic induction, ε = -N (dΦ/dt), and its applications in generators and transformers. Students calculate the efficiency of power transmission using the formula P_loss = I^2R and understand the role of high-voltage lines in Germany's electricity grid. Examples include analysing the voltage conversion at a transformer station supplying power to the Autobahn's rest stops and designing a simple generator to power model trains of the Deutsche Bahn.

## Genetics and Modern Biotechnology
Students apply Mendelian laws of inheritance to analyse monogenic traits, extending this to concepts of gene linkage and recombination. They utilise the formula RF = (Number of recombinant offspring / Total offspring) × 100% to calculate recombination frequencies from experimental data, constructing genetic maps to determine gene order. A case study involves analysing the inheritance pattern of polycystic kidney disease (PKD), prevalent in certain German regions, to map the associated gene. Core methods include using CRISPR-Cas9 for targeted gene editing, a technology employed in German biomedical research facilities to study genetic disorders. The curriculum requires students to evaluate the principles behind this technology and its application in experimental gene therapy.

## Astrophysics and Stellar Evolution
The curriculum covers the life cycle of stars, from nebulae to white dwarfs, supernovae, and black holes. Students apply the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram to classify stars and calculate stellar luminosity using the Stefan-Boltzmann Law, L = 4πR^2σT^4. German examples include analysing the data from the Max Planck Institute's research on Betelgeuse and studying the gravitational lensing effects observed by the European Southern Observatory in Chile, connecting theoretical models to observational astronomy.
