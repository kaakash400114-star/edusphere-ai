# German (Lehrplan) Grade 12 Science
## Thermodynamic Systems and Processes
Students analyse the first law of thermodynamics, ΔU = Q + W, quantifying energy transfer in closed and open systems. They calculate work done (W) during isobaric processes using PΔV, with V in litres and P in pascals. The efficiency of thermal engines is determined by η = W_th / Q_in, using real-world examples like the engine efficiency of a Volkswagen Passat (up to 45%) and the Carnot cycle as a theoretical maximum. Practical applications involve understanding energy loss in power plants and the design of efficient heating systems for modern German homes.

## Quantum Mechanics and Atomic Structure
This module covers the Bohr model of the hydrogen atom, calculating energy levels using E_n = -13.6 eV / n². Students derive the de Broglie wavelength, λ = h / p, for particles like electrons and protons. The Heisenberg uncertainty principle is applied as Δx · Δp ≥ ħ/2. Students interpret emission spectra of elements, using the Fraunhofer lines in solar light to identify chemical composition. The quantum mechanical model of the atom, with orbitals defined by quantum numbers (n, l, m_l), is used to explain the electronic structure of transition metals and their properties in industrial catalysis.

## Electrochemistry and Redox Reactions
Students calculate cell potential (E°_cell) using the standard reduction potential series for reactions like Zn | Zn²⁺ || Cu²⁺ | Cu. They apply the Nernst equation, E = E° - (RT/nF) lnQ, to determine potential under non-standard conditions. The Faraday constant (96,485 C/mol) is used to relate charge passed to mass deposited in electrolysis, such as copper refining with a current of 5.0 A over 2 hours. Applications include analysing the electrochemical processes in car batteries and the corrosion protection of the Autobahn's steel infrastructure using sacrificial anodes.

## Chemical Kinetics and Reaction Mechanisms
Students determine reaction orders and rate constants using integrated rate laws from concentration-time data. For a first-order reaction, the rate is k[A], and t₁/₂ = ln(2) / k. The Arrhenius equation, k = A e^(-E_a/RT), is used to calculate activation energies (E_a) from rate constants at different temperatures. They analyse multi-step mechanisms, using the slow step to determine the rate law. An example is the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂) catalysed by iodide ions, demonstrating how catalysts affect E_a and reaction rates in industrial processes.

## Waves and Optics: Diffraction and Interference
Students analyse double-slit interference patterns, calculating fringe spacing using Δx = λL/d, where λ is the wavelength of light, L is the screen distance, and d is the slit separation. Diffraction gratings are used to solve d sinθ = nλ for unknown wavelengths. The application of wave optics is examined in the design of anti-reflective coatings for solar panels, minimizing reflection through destructive interference. This knowledge is crucial for understanding the precision optics used in medical devices and telecommunications, such as fibre optics for high-speed internet across German cities.

## Genetics and Molecular Biology
This section explores gene expression and regulation in prokaryotes and eukaryotes. Students analyse the lac operon model, explaining how inducers like allolactose control the transcription of genes for lactose metabolism. The central dogma is applied to the process of protein synthesis from a given DNA sequence. Techniques like PCR are used for DNA amplification, with applications such as forensic analysis and paternity testing. The curriculum uses real-world examples, such as the genetic modification of crops for yield and resistance, linking molecular concepts to current issues in biotechnology and food security in the EU.
