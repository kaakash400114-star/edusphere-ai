# CBSE Class 10 Science - NCERT

## Chemical reactions and equations
In this topic, students learn how chemical substances change into new substances through reactions, represented by balanced equations. They understand the law of conservation of mass, meaning atoms are neither created nor destroyed, so equations must have equal numbers of each atom on both sides. Students classify reactions into types like combination, decomposition, displacement, and double displacement, identifying their unique characteristics. For example, when iron rusts, it is a combination reaction forming iron oxide (Fe₂O₃), and when zinc displaces hydrogen from hydrochloric acid, it is a single displacement reaction producing zinc chloride and hydrogen gas. They also learn to write and interpret balanced chemical equations using symbols and formulas.

Writing and balancing; types: combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement; redox preview (oxidation-reduction); effects: colour change, gas, temperature; corrosion and rancidity.

## Acids, bases and salts
Students learn that acids like hydrochloric acid (HCl) and citric acid taste sour and turn blue litmus paper red, while bases such as sodium hydroxide (NaOH) feel soapy and turn red litmus blue. They understand that neutralisation reactions occur when acids and bases mix to form salt and water, like when hydrochloric acid reacts with sodium hydroxide to make sodium chloride salt. The topic covers the pH scale, which measures acidity from 0 to 14, with acids below 7 and bases above 7. Students also study salt formation from various acid-base combinations, such as copper sulphate from sulphuric acid and copper hydroxide.

Reactions with metals, carbonates, bases; pH scale and importance; neutralisation in daily life; family of salts; chlor-alkali, baking soda, washing soda, bleaching powder, plaster of Paris.

## Metals and non-metals
Metals are generally hard, malleable, ductile, sonorous, and good conductors of heat and electricity, like iron, copper, and aluminium. Non-metals are generally soft or brittle, non-sonorous, and poor conductors of heat and electricity, such as sulphur and phosphorus. The reactivity series of metals helps us understand which metal can displace another metal from its salt solution, like zinc displacing copper from copper sulphate. When metals react with oxygen, they form metal oxides, such as magnesium oxide when magnesium burns in air. Most metals react with dilute acids to produce a salt and hydrogen gas, for example, zinc reacting with hydrochloric acid to form zinc chloride and hydrogen.

Physical-chemical properties; reactivity series; metal oxide vs non-metal oxide nature; corrosion and prevention; alloys: steel, brass, bronze, solder; extraction metallurgy (roasting, calcination, refining).

## Carbon and its compounds
Students learn about carbon and its many compounds, which form the basis of life. Carbon has a unique ability to form bonds with itself and other atoms, leading to a huge variety of substances. They study the structure of hydrocarbons like methane (CH₄) and ethane (C₂H₆), and understand the difference between saturated and unsaturated compounds. The chapter explains the properties of functional groups, such as alcohols and carboxylic acids, with examples like ethanol and vinegar. Students also learn about the versatile nature of carbon in daily life, from fuels to medicines.

Covalent bonding; allotropes; hydrocarbons: alkanes, alkenes, alkynes; functional groups: alcohol, aldehyde, ketone, carboxylic acid; ethanol and ethanoic acid; soaps-detergents micelle action.

## Periodic classification
Students learn about the periodic classification of elements, which organises all known elements based on their properties. The modern periodic table arranges elements in order of increasing atomic number and groups them with similar properties, such as alkali metals and halogens. Elements in the same vertical column, called a group, have the same number of valence electrons, which determines their chemical behaviour. For example, sodium and potassium in Group 1 are both reactive metals, while fluorine and chlorine in Group 17 are non-metals that form salts. The periodic table also shows how metallic character decreases and non-metallic character increases as we move from left to right across a period.

Mendeleev to Modern periodic law; periods and groups; trends: atomic size, metallic character, valence electrons; position-based reasoning.

## Life processes
In class 10 science, you learn about life processes, which are activities living things do to stay alive. These processes include nutrition, where plants make their own food through photosynthesis using sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide, while animals eat other organisms for food. Respiration is another process where all living things break down food to release energy using oxygen. You also study transportation, which in humans, involves the heart pumping blood through blood vessels to carry oxygen and nutrients to all parts of the body. Additionally, you learn about excretion, how plants and animals remove waste products like carbon dioxide and urea from their bodies.

Nutrition: autotrophic (photosynthesis steps), heterotrophic; respiration: aerobic-anaerobic, human respiratory system; transportation: heart, blood, xylem-phloem; excretion: nephron, dialysis idea.

## Control and coordination
In Class 10 Science, you learn about how our body controls and coordinates actions with the help of the nervous system and hormones. The nervous system uses nerve cells called neurons to send messages quickly from the brain to other parts of the body, like when you pull your hand away from a hot object. Hormones are chemical messengers produced by glands such as the pancreas, which regulates blood sugar by releasing insulin. You also study how plants respond to stimuli like light, gravity, and touch through growth movements, such as a plant’s roots growing downward into the soil and its stems growing upward toward sunlight.

Nervous system: neuron, reflex arc, brain parts; endocrine: hormones of pituitary-thyroid-adrenal-pancreas-testis-ovary; tropisms in plants; auxin-ghibberellin-cytokinin-abscisic acid.

## How do organisms reproduce
Organisms reproduce to create new individuals of their own kind. In asexual reproduction, a single parent produces offspring without the involvement of sex cells, like in budding in yeast or fragmentation in spirogyra. Sexual reproduction involves two parents and their sex cells, gametes, to form a zygote, for example, the fusion of male and female gametes in flowering plants to produce seeds. The process of reproduction ensures the continuity of life on Earth by replacing dying organisms with new ones. Different organisms have unique methods suited to their body design and surroundings.

Asexual modes; sexual reproduction in flowering plants (pollination, fertilisation, seed); human reproductive system; menstrual cycle; reproductive health, contraception methods; DNA-copying and variation idea.

## Heredity and evolution
In Class 10, we learn how traits are passed from parents to children through genes in our DNA. Mendel’s laws of inheritance explain how dominant and recessive traits determine whether we have features like attached or free earlobes. We study how variation and natural selection lead to evolution, as seen when bacteria become resistant to medicines over time. Evidence from fossils and anatomy, like the similar bone structure in human hands and bat wings, supports the theory of evolution. Genetic drift and speciation are also discussed to understand how new species form.

Mendel's experiments: dominant-recessive, 3:1 and 9:3:3:1; sex determination (XX-XY); traits acquired vs inherited; speciation; evolution by stages; homologous-analogous organs; fossils; human evolution sketch.

## Light: reflection and refraction
In this topic, we learn about how light travels and changes when it hits different surfaces. We study two main ideas: reflection, where light bounces back like a mirror following the rule that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, and refraction, where light bends when moving from air to water, like a straw appearing bent in a glass. We learn to draw ray diagrams for both flat and curved mirrors to show how images are formed. For lenses, we understand how convex lenses converge light to form real images and how concave lenses diverge light to form virtual images. Finally, we use formulas like the lens formula to calculate image distances and magnification, which helps us understand how our eyes and devices like cameras work.

Mirror formula idea and ray diagrams (concave-convex); magnification; refraction through glass slab; lens formula idea; power of a lens; sign conventions.

## The human eye and the colourful world
The human eye is a remarkable sense organ that helps us see the world around us. It has a lens that focuses light on the retina, which contains light-sensitive cells called rods and cones that send signals to the brain. We learn about nearsightedness (myopia) and farsightedness (hypermetropia), which are vision problems that can be corrected using concave and convex lenses, respectively. When light passes through a prism, it splits into seven colours to form a spectrum, like a rainbow in the sky. This happens because different colours of light bend at different angles, showing that white light is actually made of many colours.

Eye structure and working; defects: myopia, hypermetropia, presbyopia and corrections; dispersion by prism; scattering: blue sky, reddish sun; atmospheric refraction, twinkling.

## Electricity
In the chapter on Electricity, students learn about electric current, voltage, and resistance, and how they are related by Ohm’s Law, which states that V = I × R. They understand that electric circuits need a continuous path for current to flow and are made of components like cells, wires, switches, and bulbs. A simple circuit with a cell, connecting wires, a switch, and a glowing bulb shows how electricity flows. Students also learn to draw circuit diagrams using standard symbols for components like the cell and bulb. The concepts of series and parallel circuits are introduced, showing how components are connected differently and how current flows in each type.

Current, potential, resistance definitions; Ohm's law with V-I graph; series-parallel resistances; heating effect: Joule's law, applications; electric power and kWh bills.

## Magnetic effects of current
Students learn that when an electric current flows through a wire, it creates a magnetic field around it, which can be detected using a magnetic compass. They study the Right-Hand Thumb Rule to determine the direction of the magnetic field for a straight current-carrying conductor and the Right-Hand Grip Rule for a solenoid. An electromagnet is made by coiling insulated copper wire around a soft iron core, which becomes magnetic only when current flows through it, like in an electric bell. Fleming’s Left-Hand Rule helps us find the direction of force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field, which is used in electric motors. This topic also covers how an electric current can be induced in a coil by moving a magnet near it, as in generators that produce electricity.

Magnetic field of a current: straight, loop, solenoid; force on conductor (left-hand rule); electric motor; electromagnetic induction (right-hand rule); generator; domestic circuits: fuse, earthing, live-neutral.

## Our environment
Students learn about the environment as the sum of all living organisms (biotic components) and non-living factors (abiotic components) that interact in a place. They understand how energy flows through an ecosystem, starting from producers like plants to consumers like animals and decomposers like bacteria. A key concept is food chains and webs, for example, grass being eaten by a rabbit which is then eaten by a hawk. Students also study how harmful chemicals like pesticides move up the food chain in a process called biological magnification. The importance of conserving our natural resources, like water and forests, to protect the environment is also a major part of the lesson.

Ecosystem: biotic-abiotic; food chains-webs; ten percent law; ozone depletion (CFCs); waste management: biodegradable vs non; 3Rs; individual responsibility.

## Sustainable management of natural resources
In this topic, students learn that natural resources like water, soil, and forests need careful use to last for all people. They study how our growing population increases the need for resources, leading to problems like pollution and deforestation. For example, we protect forests by following rules that stop cutting too many trees. Students also understand the importance of rainwater harvesting, a method used in many parts of India to collect and save rainwater for later use. The key idea is that we must manage resources wisely so that future generations also have them.

Forests, wildlife, water (dams pros-cons), fossil fuels; stakeholder conflicts; Amrita Devi Bishnoi story; sustainable development practices.
