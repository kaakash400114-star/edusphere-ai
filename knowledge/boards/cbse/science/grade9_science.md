# CBSE Class 9 Science - NCERT

## Matter in our surroundings
In Class 9 Science, you will learn that all things around us are made of matter, which exists in three states: solid, liquid, and gas. You will understand that solids have a fixed shape and volume because their particles are tightly packed, like the table you sit at. Liquids take the shape of their container because their particles can move freely, like the water in your bottle. Gases have no fixed shape or volume and fill all available space, like the air you breathe. You will also discover how matter changes from one state to another through processes like melting, freezing, evaporation, and condensation, using examples like ice turning to water or steam forming from boiling water.

States and characteristics; melting-boiling points; latent heat idea; evaporation causes cooling; plasma-BE condensates mention.

## Is matter around us pure
In this topic, students learn to identify pure substances like elements (e.g., gold, oxygen) and compounds (e.g., water, salt), and mixtures like solutions (e.g., lemonade, air). They understand that pure substances have fixed properties, while mixtures can be separated using physical methods like filtration, evaporation, and chromatography. Students also classify mixtures as heterogeneous (e.g., soil, sand) or homogeneous (e.g., sugar dissolved in water) based on uniformity. They study concentration terms like mass percentage and learn about colloids, whose particles are larger than those in solutions but cannot be seen with the naked eye.

Mixtures-homogeneous-heterogeneous; solutions, colloids, suspensions; concentration by mass percent; separation: centrifugation, sublimation, chromatography; physical vs chemical change.

## Atoms and molecules
Atoms are tiny building blocks of all matter. We learn that every substance is made up of atoms, which join together to form molecules. Atoms are made of smaller particles like protons, neutrons, and electrons. Compounds are formed when different atoms combine in fixed ratios, such as water (H₂O) which has two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. We also study the laws of chemical combination, like the Law of Conservation of Mass, which states that mass is never created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.

Laws: conservation, constant proportions, multiple proportions; Dalton's postulates; atomic-molecular masses; mole concept preview; formula unit mass.

## Structure of the atom
Atoms are tiny building blocks of all matter, made up of three smaller particles called electrons, protons, and neutrons. Protons, which are positively charged, and neutrons, which have no charge, are present in the small, dense centre of an atom called the nucleus. Negatively charged electrons move around the nucleus in fixed paths or shells, like planets orbiting the sun. Each shell can hold a specific number of electrons, for example, the first shell can hold up to two electrons, while the second can hold up to eight. The number of protons in an atom's nucleus is called the atomic number, and it tells us what element the atom is.

Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr models; subatomic particles; atomic number, mass number; isotopes and isobars and uses.

## The fundamental unit of life
The basic building block of all living things is a cell, which is like a tiny room that holds all the parts needed for life. Cells can be very small, like the ones in your skin, or much larger, like the egg of a bird. Inside a cell, you can see important parts such as the nucleus, which acts like the brain and controls the cell's activities, and the cytoplasm, which is a jelly-like material filling most of the cell. Some organisms, like bacteria, have only one cell and are called unicellular, while you and a tree are multicellular because we are made of millions of cells working together. The cell wall, found in plant cells, is a strong outer layer that gives the cell its shape and protects it.

Cell theory; plasma membrane (selective), osmosis-diffusion; cell wall, nucleus, cytoplasm; organelles: ER, Golgi, lysosomes, mitochondria, plastids, vacuoles; plant vs animal cell.

## Tissues
In Class 9, we learn that tissues are groups of similar cells that work together to perform a specific function. There are two main types of plant tissues: meristematic tissues, which help plants grow by actively dividing, like at the tips of roots and stems; and permanent tissues, which have specific jobs, like xylem that carries water from the roots. In animals, we study simple tissues like epithelial tissue that covers body surfaces, and complex tissues like muscle tissue which helps in movement. We also learn about connective tissue, such as bone and blood, which supports and connects different parts of the body.

Plant: meristematic, permanent (parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma), xylem-phloem; animal: epithelial, connective, muscular, nervous; structure fits function.

## Diversity in living organisms
Living organisms are grouped into five kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia, based on their cell structure, mode of nutrition, and other features. Plants are further classified as Thallophytes, Bryophytes, Pteridophytes, Gymnosperms, and Angiosperms, with examples like rose and mango being flowering plants. Animals are divided into phyla such as Porifera, Coelenterata, Annelida, Arthropoda, and Chordata, with earthworms and insects representing different groups. This classification helps us understand the huge variety of life forms around us and their unique characteristics.

Classification basis; hierarchy; major groups: Monera, Protista, Fungi; Plantae divisions; Animalia phyla (porifera to chordata) with examples; nomenclature.

## Motion
In the topic of Motion, we learn about how things move and the ways we can describe their movement. We study speed, which tells us how fast something is going, and velocity, which includes the direction of movement. We also understand that acceleration happens when an object speeds up, slows down, or changes its direction. For example, a car moving on a straight road has a certain speed, while a car taking a turn has both speed and velocity. We use formulas like speed = distance/time to calculate how fast objects are moving in everyday situations.

Distance-displacement; speed-velocity, acceleration; graphical motion (distance-time, velocity-time); three equations of motion (derivation-free use).

## Force and laws of motion
In Class 9, we learn about force and how it makes objects move or stop, like pushing a chair or a ball rolling down a hill. We study Newton’s three laws of motion: objects stay still or move unless pushed; force equals mass times acceleration (F = m × a); and every action has an equal opposite reaction, like rowing a boat. We understand that friction slows moving things, and balanced forces keep objects steady, while unbalanced forces make them move. Real-life examples help us see these rules in action, like a football stopping or a rocket taking off.

Balanced-unbalanced forces; Newton's laws; inertia and mass; F = ma; action-reaction; conservation of momentum in collisions.

## Gravitation
In science class, you will learn about gravitation, which is the force that pulls everything towards the Earth. This force, called gravity, is why you stay on the ground and why objects fall down when you drop them. You will also learn about the universal law of gravitation, which says that every object in the universe pulls every other object with a force that depends on their masses and the distance between them. For example, the Earth pulls the Moon, and the Moon also pulls the Earth, which is why the Moon orbits around us. We use this force to calculate our weight, which is different from our mass and is measured in kilograms.

Universal law; free fall, g; mass vs weight; thrust and pressure; Archimedes' principle; buoyancy and density.

## Work and energy
In Class 9, we learn that work is done only when a force moves an object, like pushing a box across the floor. We study the formula for work done: Work = Force × Distance, and understand that the unit of work is the joule. Energy is the capacity to do work, and it comes in different forms, like kinetic energy (energy of moving things, such as a rolling ball) and potential energy (stored energy, like a stretched rubber band). The law of conservation of energy teaches us that energy can neither be created nor destroyed, only changed from one form to another.

Work = force × displacement (with angle idea); kinetic energy (½mv^2), potential energy (mgh); work-energy theorem; law of conservation; power and commercial unit kWh.

## Sound
Sound is a form of energy that travels in the form of waves through a medium like air, water, or solids. We hear sounds when these waves reach our ears and make the ear vibrate. Sound needs a medium to travel and cannot travel through a vacuum. Sounds can be loud or soft, and high-pitched or low-pitched depending on how fast the sound waves vibrate. The speed of sound is different in different materials; for example, it travels faster through solids than through air.

Production-propagation; longitudinal waves; characteristics: amplitude, frequency, wavelength, speed; reflection: echo, reverberation; ultrasound uses; human ear outline.

## Why do we fall ill
Falling ill happens when our body gets attacked by tiny germs like bacteria, viruses, or fungi, which can cause infections such as a cold or stomach flu. Our body has its own defence system called immunity, which fights these germs to keep us healthy, but sometimes the germs are too strong. Poor nutrition, dirty surroundings, and lack of rest can weaken our body and make it easier for us to fall sick. Maintaining good hygiene, like washing hands, eating clean food, and exercising regularly, helps prevent illnesses and keeps us strong. Vaccines also protect us by teaching our immunity to fight specific germs before they can make us sick.

Health vs disease-free; acute-chronic; causes: infectious (pathogens, vectors) and non-infectious; disease basics: TB, malaria, AIDS prevention-society; immune idea, vaccination.

## Natural resources
In this topic, students learn about natural resources like air, water, and soil, which are essential for life on Earth. They study the importance of conserving these resources because they are limited and getting polluted due to human activities. Children learn about the water cycle, showing how water moves from the ground to the air and back again. They also understand that natural resources are of two types—renewable, like forests, and non-renewable, like coal, which cannot be replaced once used. The topic teaches us to use resources wisely to protect our environment for the future.

Air: composition, pollution, role (wind, rain); water: cycles, pollution; mineral riches in soil; biogeochemical cycles: water, nitrogen, carbon, oxygen; ozone layer.

## Improvement in food resources
In this topic, students learn about different farming practices that help grow more food and protect crops from pests. They study the importance of manure and fertilisers to provide nutrients to the soil, like cow dung improving soil fertility for growing crops such as wheat and rice. Children also understand how crops can be improved by selecting plants with better qualities, such as high yield or disease resistance, through methods like cross-breeding. They explore the role of insects and animals in pollination, like bees helping in the production of fruits and flowers. Finally, students learn about modern farming techniques such as using improved seeds and irrigation to grow more food efficiently.

Crop variety improvement; crop production management; animal husbandry: cattle, poultry, fish (capture-culture), bee-keeping.
