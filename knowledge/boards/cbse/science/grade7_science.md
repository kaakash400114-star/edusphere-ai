# CBSE Class 7 Science - NCERT

## Nutrition in plants
Plants make their own food using a process called photosynthesis, which needs sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide. A green-coloured pigment called chlorophyll in the leaves helps capture sunlight to prepare the food. Water is absorbed by the roots from the soil, and carbon dioxide is taken in from the air through tiny pores on the leaves called stomata. The food prepared by the plant is a type of sugar called glucose, which is used for growth and to provide energy. For example, a banyan tree makes its own food in its green leaves to grow big and strong.

Photosynthesis: inputs (CO2, water, sunlight, chlorophyll), outputs (glucose, oxygen); starch test; other modes: insectivorous plants (pitcher), parasites (cuscuta), saprotrophs (fungi), symbiosis (lichen, rhizobium in pulses).

## Nutrition in animals
Animals get their food by eating plants or other animals. Different animals eat different kinds of food; some animals eat only plants, some eat only other animals, and some eat both plants and animals. Animals break down the food they eat into smaller parts to get energy, a process called digestion. For example, a cow eats grass and a tiger eats deer. The parts of food that the body cannot use are thrown out of the body as waste.

Steps: ingestion, digestion, absorption, assimilation, egestion; human digestive system organs and juices (saliva, gastric, intestinal); amoeba's food vacuole; ruminants' four-chambered stomach, cud chewing.

## Heat
Heat is a form of energy that makes things warm or hot. We measure temperature in degrees Celsius (°C) using a thermometer, and temperature tells us how hot or cold something is. Heat always flows from a hotter object to a colder object, like when you hold an ice cream in your hand and your hand feels cold. Materials like metals allow heat to pass through them easily, and they are called conductors, while materials like plastic and wood do not allow heat to pass easily, and they are called insulators. When we heat water, it turns into steam, and when we cool water, it turns into ice, showing how heat changes the state of matter.

Hot vs cold by temperature, not touch; thermometer (clinical, laboratory) use; conduction (metals good), convection (liquids, gases, sea breeze), radiation (sun's heat); why woollens keep warm.

## Acids, bases and salts
Acids are sour substances like lemon juice and vinegar that turn blue litmus paper red. Bases are bitter substances like baking soda and soap solution that turn red litmus paper blue. Neutral substances like water do not change the colour of litmus paper. When an acid and a base react, they neutralise each other to form salt and water, for example, hydrochloric acid and sodium hydroxide make sodium chloride and water. Some salts are used in our daily life, like sodium chloride in food and sodium bicarbonate in baking.

Natural indicators: litmus, turmeric, china rose; acids sour, bases bitter-slippery; neutralisation: acid + base → salt + water; daily life: antacid, soil treatment, ant bite.

## Physical and chemical changes
Students learn that changes around us can be either physical or chemical. Physical changes affect only the form of a substance, like changing water into ice or tearing paper, but its chemical makeup stays the same. Chemical changes happen when new substances are formed, such as when milk turns into curd or when iron rusts. These changes are often permanent and produce heat, light, or gas. We can identify a chemical change by observing signs like colour change, formation of bubbles, or a change in temperature. Understanding these differences helps us explain the world around us.

Physical: reversible, no new substance (ice-melt, shape-change); chemical: new substance, usually not reversible (rusting, burning, curdling); rust prevention; galvanisation.

## Respiration in organisms
Respiration is the process where living things take in oxygen and give out carbon dioxide. In humans, air enters through the nose and mouth, passes through the windpipe, and goes to the lungs where the exchange of gases happens. In plants, respiration occurs in all parts like roots, stems, and leaves, but they take in oxygen through tiny holes on their leaves called stomata. Fish breathe through their gills which help them take in oxygen dissolved in the water. This process gives energy for all activities like moving, growing, and staying healthy.

Aerobic: glucose + oxygen → CO2 + water + energy; anaerobic in yeast → alcohol; muscle cramps from lactic acid; breathing rate and exercise; fish gills, earthworm moist skin.

## Transportation in animals and plants
Animals and plants have special systems to move things inside their bodies. In animals, the heart acts like a pump that pushes blood through tubes called blood vessels to carry food and oxygen to all parts of the body. Plants have thin tubes called xylem and phloem; the xylem carries water and minerals from the roots to the leaves, while the phloem carries food made by the leaves to other parts of the plant. Blood is a red liquid that flows through the body, and water is carried up the stem in a continuous stream just like water moves through a straw. These transport systems help living things stay healthy and grow.

Human heart four chambers, arteries-veins-capillaries; blood: RBC, WBC, platelets; pulse; xylem carries water up, phloem carries food both ways; transpiration pull.

## Reproduction in plants
Plants make new plants through different methods like seeds, stems, and leaves. Seeds are made after a flower is pollinated, and they grow into new plants when they get water, air, and sunlight. Some plants, like potatoes, grow from stems under the ground, while others, like bryophyllum, grow tiny plants on their leaves. Asexual reproduction happens when one plant makes a copy of itself, like a spider plant making small plants on its runners. Sexual reproduction needs both male and female parts, like the stamen and pistil in a flower, to make seeds.

Asexual: budding (yeast), fragmentation (algae), spores (ferns), vegetative (potato, rose stem); sexual: flower parts, pollination (self/cross by wind, insects, water), fertilisation, fruit and seed.

## Motion and time
In Grade 7, students learn that motion is when an object changes its position over time. They study about slow and fast movements, like a tortoise walking slowly and a rabbit running quickly. We measure time using clocks and watches, and the basic unit of time is a second. A simple pendulum is used to show periodic motion, like the swinging of a clock's pendulum to keep time. We also learn that speed tells us how fast something is moving, which can be calculated by dividing the distance travelled by the time taken.

Speed = distance/time; uniform vs non-uniform; distance-time graph reading; simple pendulum as timekeeper; units.

## Electric current and its effects
Electric current flows through a circuit when a battery or cell is connected, and it moves from the positive terminal to the negative terminal. We use symbols to draw circuit diagrams, such as a straight line for a wire and a circle for a cell. When current flows through a wire, it gets hot because the wire resists the flow of electricity, like how rubbing your hands together makes them warm. A simple electromagnet can be made by winding insulated copper wire around a nail and attaching it to a cell; it can pick up small iron filings, showing how electricity can create magnetism. Electric current can flow easily through some materials called conductors, like metals, but not through others called insulators, like plastic or rubber.

Circuit symbols; heating effect (fuse, heater filament); magnetic effect (compass deflects, electromagnet); bell working.

## Light
Light travels in a straight line, which we call rectilinear propagation. When light hits a shiny surface like a mirror, it bounces back at the same angle, following the law of reflection. We use this to see our reflections in mirrors and in still water. A shadow is formed when an opaque object blocks the path of light, and the shape of the shadow depends on the object and the position of the light source. Coloured objects appear that colour because they reflect only that colour of light and absorb all the others, like a red ball reflecting only red light.

Laws of reflection (equal angles); plane mirror image (virtual, laterally inverted, same size); concave/convex mirrors and lenses basics; dispersion: prism makes rainbow; the eye's care.

## Forests: our lifeline
Forests are large areas covered with many trees and different plants that provide us with oxygen to breathe. They are home to animals like tigers, elephants, and birds, and also serve as shelter for many insects and smaller creatures. The roots of trees hold the soil together, which prevents soil erosion and helps in maintaining the water cycle, bringing us rain. Forests give us useful things like wood for making furniture, fruits and medicines for our health, and clean air to keep us healthy. When forests are cut down, it disturbs nature and harms the animals that live there, so we must always plant more trees to protect them.

Canopy-understorey layers; forests as habitat, water bank, air purifier; food web interdependence; deforestation effects.

## Wastewater story
Wastewater is the used water from homes, schools, and factories that needs cleaning before returning to rivers. We learn about the wastewater treatment plant where water passes through screens to remove large objects like sticks and plastic. Next, it goes into settling tanks where mud and dirt settle at the bottom as sludge. Water then gets filtered through layers of sand and gravel to clean it further. Clean water is then treated with chlorine to kill germs before being released safely into the environment.

Sewage and its treatment steps (screening, sedimentation, aeration, chlorination); septic tanks; maintain drains; reuse grey water; our role in clean water.
