# CBSE Class 9 Mathematics - NCERT

## Number systems
In Grade 9, you learn about the different types of numbers, starting from natural numbers, whole numbers, integers, rational numbers, and irrational numbers. You study how to locate these numbers on the number line, especially understanding that rational numbers can be written as fractions like 1/2, while irrational numbers cannot be written this way, such as √2. You also learn the rules for operations like addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division with both rational and irrational numbers, and how to simplify expressions using these rules. For example, you learn that the sum of a rational number and an irrational number is always irrational. This helps you build a strong foundation for understanding more complex number systems later.

Rational-irrational; real numbers and the number line; surds and laws of exponents with real powers; decimal expansions and rationality tests.

## Polynomials
In Class 9, you learn about polynomials, which are expressions made of variables and numbers using operations like addition, subtraction, and multiplication. A polynomial has terms, like 3x² or -5x + 2, and its degree is the highest power of the variable, such as 2 in x² + 3x + 1. You discover how to add, subtract, and multiply polynomials, including special products like (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b². For example, multiplying (x + 3) by (x - 2) gives x² + x - 6. You also learn to factorise polynomials, breaking them into simpler factors, like turning x² - 9 into (x + 3)(x - 3).

Degree, types; zeroes of a polynomial; remainder theorem; factor theorem; factorising quadratics and cubics using theorems; identities: (a+b)^3, (a−b)^3, a^3+b^3, a^3−b^3.

## Coordinate geometry
In Coordinate Geometry, you learn to find the exact position of points on a graph using numbers called coordinates. You plot points on a plane where the horizontal line is the x-axis and the vertical line is the y-axis. You also find the distance between two points using a special formula: √[(x₂ - x₁)² + (y₂ - y₁)²]. For example, the distance between points (2, 3) and (5, 6) can be found by this formula. Finally, you divide the plane into four sections called quadrants and learn the sign rules for coordinates in each quadrant.

Cartesian plane; quadrants; plot points; abscissa-ordinate; graph of a linear equation preview.

## Linear equations in two variables
In this topic, you learn to write and solve linear equations in two variables, like 2x + 3y = 12. You understand that an equation has two unknown values, x and y, and find pairs of numbers that make the equation true, such as (3, 2) or (0, 4). You also learn to plot these pairs on a graph to draw a straight line, which shows all possible solutions. The lesson teaches you how to check if a given pair of numbers is a solution by putting them into the equation. This helps you see the relationship between two quantities that change together.

Solution as ordered pair; graph of ax + by + c = 0; equations of axes; word problems with two unknowns (graphical sense).

## Introduction to Euclid's geometry
In this topic, we learn about the great mathematician Euclid and his book, "Elements," which explains geometry with simple rules called axioms and postulates. An axiom is a statement that everyone accepts as true without proof, like "things which are equal to the same thing are also equal to one another." A postulate is a special rule for geometry, like "a straight line can be drawn from any one point to any other point." We use these rules to understand basic terms such as point, line, and plane, and to see how they connect in simple shapes. For example, we learn that a line is a collection of points that extends endlessly in both directions.

Axioms, postulates, theorems; definitions; equivalent versions of the fifth postulate (idea).

## Lines and angles
In this topic, you learn about different types of angles like acute, obtuse, and right angles, and how to measure them using a protractor. You discover the special relationships between angles when two lines meet or when a line crosses two other lines; these are called complementary angles, supplementary angles, and vertically opposite angles. For example, if two angles add up to 90 degrees, they are complementary, and if they add up to 180 degrees, they are supplementary. You also learn about parallel lines and a transversal, which creates corresponding angles and alternate angles that are always equal. These rules help you solve problems by finding unknown angle measures in different geometric figures.

Angle pairs; parallel lines and a transversal; angle-sum property of a triangle; exterior angle theorem; proofs introduced formally.

## Triangles
In this topic, you learn about triangles, their parts, and the rules that make them special. You will study different types of triangles like equilateral, isosceles, and scalene, based on their sides and angles. A key rule you learn is that the sum of the three angles in any triangle is always 180 degrees. You also discover the criteria for when two triangles are congruent, meaning they are exactly the same shape and size, such as the SSS rule where all three corresponding sides are equal. For example, if one triangle has sides of 3 cm, 4 cm, and 5 cm, another triangle with the exact same side lengths will be congruent to it.

Congruence criteria: SAS, ASA, SSS, RHS; properties of isosceles triangles; inequalities (side-angle links).

## Quadrilaterals
In Class 9 Mathematics, you learn about quadrilaterals, which are four-sided shapes like squares, rectangles, and parallelograms. You study special properties, such as opposite sides of a parallelogram being equal and parallel, and the diagonals of a rectangle being equal. You also learn to calculate the area of these shapes using formulas, like length times breadth for rectangles and base times height for parallelograms. For example, a square with side 4 cm has an area of 16 cm². The topic helps you understand how these shapes are different and how to solve problems related to their sides and angles.

Parallelogram properties with proofs; midpoint theorem and its converse; diagonal-based properties of special quadrilaterals.

## Circles
In Grade 9, you learn about circles and their important parts like the centre, radius, diameter, and circumference. You discover that the circumference of a circle is the distance around it, and the radius is the distance from the centre to the edge. The formula to find the circumference is \( C = 2\pi r \), where \( r \) is the radius. For example, if a circle has a radius of 7 cm, its circumference is \( 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 7 \) cm, which equals 44 cm. You also study about the area of a circle, which is the space inside it, using the formula \( A = \pi r^2 \). If the radius is 7 cm, the area is \( \frac{22}{7} \times 7 \times 7 \) cm², which is 154 cm².

Chords and their distance from centre (equal chords theorem); angle subtended; cyclic quadrilateral opposite angles 180°.

## Heron's formula
In Heron's formula, we learn to find the area of a triangle when all three sides are known, even without the height. First, we calculate the semi-perimeter (half the sum of all sides), denoted as 's', where s = (a + b + c)/2 for sides a, b, and c. Then, we use the formula Area = √[s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)] to find the area. For example, if a triangle has sides 6 cm, 10 cm, and 8 cm, we find s = (6 + 10 + 8)/2 = 12 cm, and then compute Area = √[12(12 - 6)(12 - 10)(12 - 8)] = √[12 × 6 × 2 × 4] = 24 cm². This formula is very useful for triangles where the height is not given.

Area of a triangle from sides; applications to quadrilaterals split into triangles.

## Surface areas and volumes
In Class 9, you learn to find the surface areas and volumes of different shapes like cubes, cuboids, cylinders, cones, and spheres. You use formulas to calculate the total area of all outer surfaces, such as adding the areas of all six faces of a cuboid. For volumes, you find the space inside a shape, like multiplying the length, breadth, and height of a cuboid. You also solve problems involving real-life objects, like finding the amount of paint needed to paint a box or the water a cylindrical tank can hold. These formulas help you understand how much material is needed to make something and how much space it takes up.

Right circular cone: CSA, TSA, volume; sphere and hemisphere; cube-cuboid-cylinder recap; combined solids (basic).

## Statistics
In this topic, you will learn how to collect, organise, and present data using different methods like tally marks and frequency distribution tables. You will understand how to find the mean, median, and mode of a set of numbers to understand the central value of the data. For example, to find the mean of marks scored by students, you add all the marks and divide by the number of students. You will also learn to draw bar graphs to represent data visually, making it easy to understand. These simple tools help you make sense of information in everyday life.

Raw to grouped data; bar graphs, histograms, frequency polygons; mean (direct), median (ungrouped), mode (observation-based).

## Probability (empirical)
In Grade 9, we learn about empirical probability, which is finding the chance of something happening by actually doing it or observing it many times. We use the formula: Probability of an event = (Number of times the event happens) ÷ (Total number of trials). For example, if you toss a coin 20 times and get heads 12 times, the empirical probability of getting heads is 12/20. We understand that the more we repeat an experiment, the closer the result gets to the true probability. This helps us make simple predictions based on real-life data, like guessing the chance of getting a red ball from a bag after picking many times.

Trial, outcome, event; empirical probability P(E) = favourable/total trials; coin-dice experiments with data.
