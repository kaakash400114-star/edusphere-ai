# CBSE Class 10 Mathematics - NCERT (Standard)

## Real numbers
In Class 10, you will learn about real numbers, which include both rational numbers (like fractions and integers) and irrational numbers (like √2 and π). You will study the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, which states that every number greater than 1 can be written as a unique product of prime numbers. Another important concept is Euclid’s division lemma, which helps in finding the greatest common divisor (HCF) of two numbers by using the rule: dividend = divisor × quotient + remainder. For example, to find the HCF of 56 and 72, you can use the lemma repeatedly until you get zero as the remainder. Finally, you will learn how to rationalise the denominator of a fraction to remove irrational numbers from the bottom, such as writing 1/√3 as √3/3.

Euclid's division lemma (algorithm idea); Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic; revisiting irrational numbers (proof that √2 is irrational); decimal expansions.

## Polynomials
In Class 10, you learn about polynomials which are expressions like \(2x^2 - 3x + 5\) made of variables and numbers. You study the degree of a polynomial, which is the highest power of the variable, like 2 in \(x^3 + 4x^2\). You also learn to divide polynomials using the Remainder Theorem, which says when you divide \(p(x)\) by \(x - a\), the remainder is \(p(a)\), like dividing \(x^2 - 5x + 6\) by \(x - 2\) gives a remainder of 0. Finally, you explore the relationship between a polynomial's zeroes and its coefficients, such as in \(x^2 - 5x + 6\) where the zeroes are 2 and 3, and the sum is 5.

Geometric meaning of zeroes; relationship between zeroes and coefficients (quadratic); division algorithm for polynomials; cubic zeroes (statements).

## Pair of linear equations in two variables
In Class 10, students learn to solve pairs of linear equations in two variables, like 2x + 3y = 7 and x - y = 1. They study three methods: substitution (replacing one variable with an expression), elimination (adding or subtracting equations to remove a variable), and graphical (finding where lines intersect). The topic also covers consistency, checking if equations have one solution, no solution, or infinite solutions. For example, solving x + y = 5 and 2x + 2y = 10 shows infinite solutions since both equations represent the same line. Students practice real-life problems, like finding costs of pens and pencils, using these methods.

Graphical method; substitution, elimination, cross-multiplication; consistent-inconsistent-dependent systems; word problems: ages, boats-streams, money.

## Quadratic equations
In this topic, students learn to solve quadratic equations, which are equations of the form ax² + bx + c = 0, where a, b, and c are numbers and a is not zero. They practice finding the values of x that make the equation true using methods like factorisation, completing the square, and the quadratic formula, which is x = [–b ± √(b² – 4ac)] / 2a. Students also understand that the number inside the square root, called the discriminant (b² – 4ac), tells them how many solutions the equation has. For example, to solve x² – 5x + 6 = 0, they can factor it into (x – 2)(x – 3) = 0 to get the solutions x = 2 and x = 3.

Standard form; solution by factorisation and quadratic formula; nature of roots via discriminant; word problems (speed, area, projectile-like).

## Arithmetic progressions
In this topic, you will learn about arithmetic progressions, which are sequences of numbers where each term increases by a fixed number called the common difference. For example, in 2, 5, 8, 11,... the common difference is 3. You will study the formula to find any term in the sequence, which is aₙ = a + (n-1)d, where 'a' is the first term, 'd' is the common difference, and 'n' is the term number. You will also learn to find the sum of the first 'n' terms of an arithmetic progression using the formula Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n-1)d]. These help in solving real-life problems like calculating savings or patterns in numbers.

nth term; sum of first n terms; applications: seats in rows, savings, ladder steps.

## Triangles
In the Triangles chapter, you learn about the different types of triangles based on sides and angles, like equilateral and right-angled triangles. You study the important rule that the sum of angles in any triangle is always 180 degrees. A key concept is the similarity of triangles, where two triangles have the same shape but not necessarily the same size, and you learn the criteria to check if they are similar, such as the AA (Angle-Angle) rule. You also discover the relationship between the sides and angles of a triangle, for example, that the side opposite the larger angle is always longer. Finally, you apply these ideas to solve problems involving heights and distances using the properties of triangles.

Similar figures; criteria AA, SSS, SAS; Basic Proportionality (Thales) theorem and converse; areas theorem; Pythagoras theorem proof and converse.

## Coordinate geometry
In Coordinate Geometry, students learn to plot points on a plane using numbers called coordinates, written as (x, y). They find the distance between two points using the formula √[(x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²]. They also calculate the midpoint of a line segment with the formula [(x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2]. For example, the distance between (2, 3) and (5, 7) is found by putting these values in the distance formula. Students further study how to find the area of a triangle using the formula ½[x₁(y₂−y₃) + x₂(y₃−y₁) + x₃(y₁−y₂)]. These concepts help in solving real-life problems like locating places or measuring distances accurately.

Distance formula; section formula (internal division); midpoint; area of a triangle from coordinates.

## Introduction to trigonometry
In Class 10, you learn about trigonometric ratios like sine, cosine, and tangent for right-angled triangles. These ratios are found by dividing the length of one side by another, such as the opposite side or the hypotenuse. You will use these ratios to find missing angles and sides in problems, like calculating the height of a building. The chapter also introduces important trigonometric identities, such as sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, which help in solving equations. You will practice using these rules to solve real-life problems involving heights and distances, making maths more practical and useful.

Trigonometric ratios of acute angles; ratios of 0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90°; identities: sin^2+cos^2=1 and family; simple height-distance word problems preview.

## Some applications of trigonometry
In this topic, students learn how to use trigonometric ratios to solve real-life problems involving heights and distances. They find the height of tall objects like trees or buildings using angles measured with clinometers or theodolites. Important rules include the sine, cosine, and tangent functions to relate the sides of a right-angled triangle to its angles. For example, to calculate the height of a flagpole, they measure its angle of elevation and the distance from it. This helps in understanding practical applications of maths in fields like architecture and navigation.

Heights and distances: angle of elevation-depression; two-object problems; ladder-tower-shadow setups with diagrams.

## Circles
In the Circles chapter, you learn about the properties of a circle, including terms like radius, diameter, chord, and tangent. A tangent is a line that touches the circle at exactly one point, and the radius drawn to the point of contact is always perpendicular to the tangent. You also study theorems about the angles formed by a chord and a tangent, where the angle between them is equal to the angle in the alternate segment. For example, if a tangent and a chord meet at a point on the circle, the angle they form is the same as the angle subtended by the chord in the opposite part of the circle. These rules help you solve problems related to circles and their properties.

Tangent theorems: perpendicular from centre; equal tangents from external point; number of tangents from a point; simple proofs and applications.

## Constructions
In this topic, you learn to construct geometric figures using only a compass and straightedge, following specific steps and rules. You will practise drawing the perpendicular bisector of a line segment, which divides it into two equal parts at a right angle, and the bisector of an angle, splitting it into two equal angles. You will also construct triangles given their three sides or two sides and the included angle, ensuring all measurements match the given conditions. For example, you can build a triangle with sides of 5 cm, 7 cm, and 3 cm by marking these lengths and connecting the points correctly. These constructions help you understand and apply the properties of shapes accurately.

Divide a segment in a ratio; similar triangles; tangents to a circle from an external point (ruler-compass precision).

## Areas related to circles
In this topic, students learn to find the area of circles and parts of circles, like sectors and segments. They use the formula πr² for the full area and (θ/360) × πr² for the area of a sector with angle θ. They also calculate the area of a segment by subtracting the triangle's area from the sector's area. For example, a semicircle with radius 7 cm has an area of (180/360) × π × 7². These ideas help in solving real-life problems, like finding the area of a pizza slice or a playground shaped like a circle.

Sector and segment area-perimeter; combinations of plane figures around circles.

## Surface areas and volumes
In this topic, students learn to calculate the surface areas and volumes of different solid shapes like cubes, cuboids, cylinders, cones, spheres, and hemispheres. They use formulas such as TSA of a cube is 6a² and volume of a cylinder is πr²h, where 'a' is the side length and 'r' and 'h' are the radius and height. For example, to find the volume of a cuboid with length 10 cm, breadth 5 cm, and height 3 cm, the formula is l × b × h = 150 cm³. They also study how to combine solids, like finding the total volume of a toy made by attaching a cone to a hemisphere. The key is to remember which formula to use for each shape and apply the correct measurements.

Combination solids (toy = cone + hemisphere); conversion of solids (melting-recasting); frustum of a cone (formula use); mixed problems.

## Statistics
In Class 10 Statistics, students learn to group raw data into class intervals and draw frequency distribution tables. They calculate measures of central tendency, namely mean, median, and mode, using both direct and step-deviation methods to understand data trends. The mean of grouped data is found by the formula \(\bar{x} = a + \frac{\sum f_i d_i}{\sum f_i}\), where \(a\) is the assumed mean and \(d_i\) is the deviation. Students also represent data visually using bar graphs, histograms, and frequency polygons for better interpretation. For example, they might analyse the heights of students in a class using these methods.

Mean by all three methods (direct, assumed mean, step-deviation); mode of grouped data; median of grouped data; ogive graphs; cumulative frequency.

## Probability
In Class 10, you learn that probability is the chance of something happening, written as a number between 0 and 1. You find the probability of an event by dividing the number of favourable outcomes by the total number of possible outcomes. For example, if a bag has 3 red marbles and 2 blue marbles, the probability of picking a red marble is 3/5. You also learn that the probability of an event that cannot happen, like rolling a 7 on a standard die, is 0. The sum of the probabilities of all possible outcomes is always 1.

Classical (theoretical) probability; simple card-dice-coin problems; complementary events; "equally likely" reasoning.
