# CBSE Class 2 Mathematics - NCERT (Math-Magic 2)

## Counting in groups and tens-ones to 99
Children learn to count objects by grouping them in tens and ones to understand numbers up to 99. They break down a two-digit number into tens and ones, like 25 being 2 tens and 5 ones. This helps them read and write numbers correctly, such as 34 as thirty-four. They practice counting forward and backward in tens, like 10, 20, 30, or 50, 40, 30. Using objects like beads or sticks, they group ten together to count bigger numbers easily. This method builds a strong base for understanding place value and larger numbers later.

Bundles of ten; 47 = 4 tens + 7 ones. Order and compare two-digit numbers (greater/smaller, before/after/between).

## Addition of two-digit numbers (with carry)
In Grade 2, children learn to add two-digit numbers where the sum of the ones place is 10 or more, requiring them to carry over the tens digit to the next column. They first add the numbers in the ones place, write down the ones digit of the sum, and carry over the tens digit to the tens place. Then, they add the carried number to the digits in the tens place to get the final sum. For example, when adding 47 and 36, they first add 7 and 6 to get 13, write 3 in the ones place, and carry over 1. Next, they add the carried 1 to 4 and 3 to get 8, resulting in the final answer of 83.

Add ones first, carry to tens: 47 + 38 = 85. NCERT method: story sums with shop money and objects.

## Subtraction of two-digit numbers (with borrow)
In Class 2, we learn to subtract two-digit numbers when the top number is smaller in the ones place, called borrowing. We take one ten from the tens place, change it to ten ones, and add it to the ones place to subtract. For example, in 45 – 17, we cannot subtract 7 from 5, so we borrow 1 ten from 4, making it 3 tens and 15 ones. Then, we subtract 7 from 15 to get 8, and 3 from 1 to get 2, so the answer is 28. This method helps us solve subtraction problems even when the ones digit is smaller.

47 - 29: borrow a ten. Word problems: "Ravi had 52 stamps, gave 27 away."

## Introduction to multiplication as repeated addition
Children learn that multiplication is a quick way to add the same number again and again. For example, 3 × 2 means adding 2 three times: 2 + 2 + 2. The first number tells how many times to add, and the second number tells what to add. We use the multiplication symbol (×) to show repeated addition. This helps in solving problems like 4 × 3, which is the same as 3 + 3 + 3 + 3.

3 + 3 + 3 = 9 = 3 twos... no: 3 groups of 3. Tables of 2, 5 and 10 by skip counting on the number line.

## Introduction to division as sharing
Children learn division as the action of sharing things equally among a group. They understand that when we share items, we divide them into smaller, equal parts so that everyone gets the same amount. For example, if we have 8 toffees and share them between 2 friends, each friend gets 4 toffees. We write this as 8 ÷ 2 = 4. Children practice sharing objects like fruits, pencils, or blocks to understand that division is about making fair groups. They learn that division helps us find out how many things each person will get when we share things equally.

Share 12 laddoos among 4 friends → 3 each. Division as equal sharing and equal grouping (no remainder here).

## Money: rupees and paise
Children learn about money using coins and notes of India, like ₹1, ₹2, ₹5, and ₹10. They count money by adding rupees and paise together, such as ₹5 and ₹50 paise making ₹5.50. They also learn to compare amounts to see which is more or less, like ₹10 is more than ₹5. Children practice using money to buy simple things, such as a pencil for ₹10 or an eraser for ₹3. They understand that money is used to buy things we need and want.

Coins and notes to ₹100; add and subtract money in simple shopping; paise make rupees (100 paise = ₹1).

## Length, weight and capacity (non-standard to standard)
Children learn to measure length, weight, and capacity using standard units like centimetres, metres, grams, kilograms, litres, and millilitres. They compare objects using these units, for example, a pencil may be 15 centimetres long and a book may weigh 200 grams. They also understand that a bottle of water can hold 1 litre of liquid and a small spoon can hold 10 millilitres. Children practice measuring everyday objects using rulers, weighing scales, and measuring containers to understand how these units work. This helps them learn how much or how long something is in a simple and clear way.

Hand-span, cubit, foot → introduce metre and centimetre; heavier/lighter with kg idea; a cup, a litre.

## Time: days, months, the clock (half hours)
Children learn about days and months, understanding that there are 7 days in a week and 12 months in a year. They also learn to read the time to the half hour, where the minute hand points at 6, and the hour hand points between two numbers, for example, half past 2 means the hour hand is between 2 and 3. They practice sequencing days of the week and months of the year in the correct order, from Sunday to Saturday and from January to December. This helps them understand the sequence of time and how time is measured in daily life.

Calendar months in order; reading half past; o'clock; how long activities take (minutes/hours).

## Shapes: 2D and solids around us
In Grade 2, children learn to identify and name simple 2D shapes like circles, squares, triangles, and rectangles from objects around them, such as a clock, a book, or a slice of pizza. They also recognise 3D solids like cubes, cuboids, spheres, and cylinders using real-world examples like a dice, a matchbox, a ball, or a glass. The children observe and describe these shapes and solids by counting their corners (vertices), edges, and faces. They understand that flat shapes are 2D, while solid shapes are 3D and take up space. This helps them see how maths is connected to the world they live in.

Circle, triangle, square, rectangle; cube (dice), cylinder (can), cone (birthday cap), sphere (ball). Edges and corners of solids.

## Patterns and data handling
In Grade 2 Mathematics, children observe and create patterns using shapes, numbers, and objects, like continuing a sequence of red-blue-red-blue cubes or skip counting by 2s (2, 4, 6, 8). They learn to identify the rule of a pattern, such as adding 1 each time in a number sequence (3, 4, 5, 6) or alternating between two shapes. For data handling, children collect information through simple observations, like counting the number of fruits in their lunchbox, and organise it using tally marks or pictographs. They represent data using pictures, where one picture stands for one object, to show which fruit is most or least common. This helps them understand how to sort and compare information visually.

Growing and repeating patterns; picture graphs: "How many children like mango best?"
