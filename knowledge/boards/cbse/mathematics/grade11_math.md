# CBSE Class 11 Mathematics - NCERT

## Sets
In the topic of Sets, students learn about collections of well-defined objects called elements, and how to describe sets using either roster form (listing all elements like {1, 2, 3}) or set-builder notation (describing properties like {x: x is an even number}). They study important concepts such as subsets, universal sets, the empty set, and operations like union (combining elements from two sets, A ∪ B), intersection (finding common elements, A ∩ B), and difference (elements in one set but not the other). Students also understand Venn diagrams, which are circular drawings used to visualise these relationships between two or three sets, like showing that the set of vowels {a, e, i, o, u} is a subset of the set of all English alphabet letters.

Representation, types (empty, finite-infinite, equal); subsets-power set; Venn diagrams; operations and laws; practical problems on union-intersection.

## Relations and functions
Students learn about relations which connect elements from one set to another, like the relation ‘is the brother of’ between names and ages. Functions are a special type of relation where each input has exactly one output, such as a machine that takes a number and returns its square. We study different types of functions, including one-one (injective), onto (surjective), and many-one functions, using examples like f(x) = 2x or f(x) = x². Students also practice identifying if a given relation is a function by checking for multiple outputs for a single input. These concepts help in understanding real-world situations like mapping students to their roll numbers or finding prices based on item quantities.

Ordered pairs, Cartesian product; domain-codomain-range; kinds of relations (reflexive, symmetric, transitive); functions: identity, constant, modulus, signum, greatest integer; graphs.

## Trigonometric functions
In Class 11, students learn the advanced study of trigonometric functions, extending from the basics to understand the signs of trigonometric ratios in all four quadrants. They are taught the fundamental trigonometric identities, such as sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, and how to use them to simplify expressions. The concept of trigonometric functions of sum and difference of two angles is introduced, including formulas like sin(A + B) = sinA cosB + cosA sinB. Students also learn to solve equations using these functions, for example, finding the value of θ in sinθ = 1/2. Additionally, they explore the trigonometric functions of multiple angles and their applications in solving problems.

Angles in radians-degrees; functions with domains-ranges; identities (fundamental, sum-difference, multiple-half angles); general solutions of trig equations; sine-cosine laws.

## Principle of mathematical induction
The principle of mathematical induction is a method to prove statements for all natural numbers. We first prove the statement is true for the initial value, usually n = 1, called the base case. Then, we assume the statement holds for some arbitrary natural number k, the inductive hypothesis. Finally, we prove that if the statement is true for n = k, it must also be true for n = k + 1, the inductive step. For example, we can use it to prove the sum of the first n natural numbers, 1 + 2 + 3 + ... + n, is equal to n(n + 1)/2.

Statement, inductive hypothesis, inductive step; standard divisibility and inequality problems.

## Complex numbers and quadratic equations
In Class 11, you learn about complex numbers, which are numbers of the form a + bi, where 'a' and 'b' are real numbers and 'i' is the imaginary unit with the property i² = -1. You study how to add, subtract, multiply, and divide these numbers, converting them into the polar form r(cosθ + i sinθ) to make multiplication and division easier. The topic also covers quadratic equations and how their solutions can be complex numbers when the discriminant (b² - 4ac) is negative. For example, the equation x² + 4 = 0 has solutions x = ±2i. Finally, you learn about the conjugate of a complex number and how to use it to find the modulus.

i and powers; algebra of complex numbers; modulus-conjugate; argand plane; quadratic with negative discriminant; square root of a complex number (statements).

## Linear inequalities
Students learn to solve linear inequalities in one or two variables, understanding that the solution is a range of values rather than a single number. They apply rules such as multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality sign and plot these solutions on a number line for one variable or in a coordinate plane for two variables. For example, the inequality 3x - 2 > 4 is solved as x > 2, while x + y < 5 is represented by the region below the line x + y = 5. Real-world problems involving cost, speed, or quantities are used to model and interpret these inequalities.

Algebraic solutions in one variable; graphical in two variables; system of inequalities; feasible-region introduction.

## Permutations and combinations
In Class 11, you learn to count arrangements and selections using permutations and combinations. A permutation is an arrangement where order matters, like arranging 3 books on a shelf; the formula is nPr = n! / (n-r)!. A combination is a selection where order does not matter, like choosing 2 fruits from a basket; the formula is nCr = n! / (r! (n-r)!). You also learn about factorial notation (n! = n × (n-1) × ... × 1) and solve problems using these rules. For example, finding the number of ways to form a 2-member committee from 5 people is a combination problem.

Factorial; fundamental counting principle; nPr vs nCr; arrangements with repetitions; mixed selection problems (committees, words, digits).

## Binomial theorem
In Class 11, you learn the binomial theorem to expand expressions like (a + b)ⁿ easily, where 'n' is a positive integer. The theorem states that (a + b)ⁿ equals the sum of terms from k=0 to n of C(n, k) × aⁿ⁻ᵏ × bᵏ, where C(n, k) is the combination formula, also written as ⁿCₖ. For example, (x + y)³ expands to x³ + 3x²y + 3xy² + y³ using the rule. You also discover Pascal’s Triangle, which helps find the coefficients of the expansion quickly, and practice applying the general term Tᵣ₊₁ = C(n, r) × aⁿ⁻ʳ × bʳ to find specific terms in a binomial expansion.

Expansion for positive integer index; general and middle terms; simple applications (approximations preview).

## Sequences and series
Students learn about sequences, which are ordered lists of numbers following a specific rule, like 2, 4, 6, 8, which increases by 2. They also study series, which is the sum of the terms in a sequence, such as 2 + 4 + 6 + 8. The arithmetic progression (AP) has a constant difference between terms, with formulas for the nth term (aₙ = a + (n-1)d) and the sum (Sₙ = n/2 [2a + (n-1)d]). Geometric progression (GP) has a constant ratio between terms, using formulas aₙ = arⁿ⁻¹ and Sₙ = a(rⁿ - 1)/(r - 1). These concepts help in finding patterns and solving real-life problems involving growth or decay.

AP-GP recap at depth; nth term and sums; AM-GM relation; sum to n terms of special series (Σn, Σn^2, Σn^3); GP infinite sum condition.

## Straight lines
In this topic, students learn about straight lines using the Cartesian coordinate system to understand their equations, which can be written in different forms like slope-intercept and two-point forms. They discover how to find the slope of a line, which shows its steepness, and use it to write equations when given points or angles. For example, a line passing through (2, 3) with a slope of 2 has the equation y = 2x − 1. Students also calculate the distance between two points and the midpoint of a line segment, and learn about conditions for two lines to be parallel or perpendicular. These concepts help in solving real-life problems, like finding the shortest path or checking if lines meet at a right angle.

Slope; forms of line equations; angle between lines; distance point-line; family of lines idea.

## Conic sections
In the topic of Conic sections, you will learn about the shapes formed when a cone is cut by a plane, namely circles, ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas. You will study the standard equations of these conics, such as (x-h)² + (y-k)² = r² for a circle with centre (h, k) and radius r, and y² = 4ax for a parabola that opens to the right with its vertex at the origin. You will also explore the focus and directrix of these curves, for example, understanding that a parabola is the set of all points equidistant from a fixed point (focus) and a fixed line (directrix). Additionally, you will solve problems involving these geometric shapes, like finding the equation of a circle passing through given points or determining the focal length of a parabolic mirror.

Circle (centre-radius forms); parabola, ellipse, hyperbola standard forms with focus-directrix-eccentricity definitions.

## Introduction to three-dimensional geometry
In this topic, students learn about the three-dimensional coordinate system, where any point in space is located using three coordinates (x, y, z). They study the distance formula between two points in 3D space, such as finding the distance between a shop at (1, 2, 3) and a school at (4, 5, 6). Additionally, they explore the section formula, which divides the line joining two points in a given ratio. An important concept is the direction ratios and direction cosines of a line, describing its orientation in space. Finally, students apply these formulas to solve practical problems involving the position and distance of objects.

Coordinate axes-octants; distance and section formulae in 3D.

## Limits and derivatives
In this topic, students learn to find the limit of a function as the variable approaches a certain value, using rules like the sum, difference, and product rules. They are introduced to the concept of left-hand and right-hand limits to determine if a limit exists. Derivatives are explained as the rate of change of a function, calculated using the first principle of derivatives and formulas like the power rule, for example, the derivative of x² is 2x. Students apply these concepts to find the derivative of polynomial, trigonometric, and exponential functions, understanding that a derivative represents the slope of the tangent to a curve at a given point.

Intuitive limit idea; algebra of limits; standard limits; derivative as rate/ slope; derivatives of polynomials and sin-cos by first principles plus rules.

## Mathematical reasoning
In the chapter on Mathematical Reasoning, you will learn to distinguish between a mathematical statement and a sentence that is not a statement. A statement is a declarative sentence that is either true or false, such as "The sum of the angles of a triangle is 180 degrees." You will explore the different types of logical connectives like 'and', 'or', and 'implies' and understand how to form compound statements from simpler ones. The topic also introduces the concepts of negation, converse, inverse, and contrapositive of a given statement, which are fundamental for logical deduction and proving mathematical propositions.

Statements, negation, compound statements, quantifiers, implications, contrapositive-converse, validating arguments.

## Statistics and probability
In Class 11, students learn to calculate measures of central tendency like mean, median, and mode for grouped and ungrouped data. They also study measures of dispersion, including range, mean deviation, and standard deviation, to understand how spread out the data is. For probability, they explore the classical approach to find the probability of events, using formulas like P(E) = Number of favourable outcomes / Total number of outcomes. Students learn about different types of events such as mutually exclusive, exhaustive, and independent events. For example, they might find the probability of drawing a red card from a deck of playing cards or the chance of getting an even number on a die.

Dispersion: range, mean deviation, variance, standard deviation; probability: sample space, events, addition rule (mutually exclusive), axiomatic view.
