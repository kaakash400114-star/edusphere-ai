# CBSE Class 5 Mathematics - NCERT (Math-Magic 5)

## The fish tale: large numbers and estimation
In this topic, children learn to read and write very big numbers like lakhs and crores, such as 1,00,000 for one lakh. They practise breaking down large numbers into place values, such as understanding 2,45,312 as 2 lakhs, 4 ten thousands, 5 thousands, 3 hundreds, 1 ten, and 2 ones. Students also learn to round off numbers to the nearest ten, hundred, or thousand, like rounding 674 to 700 for easier addition. They use estimation to guess the answer before calculating, such as guessing about 800 for 321 + 429. The topic helps them solve word problems involving big numbers, like counting total fish in a pond or comparing the number of fish caught by different boats.

Numbers to lakhs and crores; round and estimate to check answers; multiplication with big numbers in real fishing/market stories.

## Shapes and angles
In this topic, children learn about different shapes like squares, rectangles, triangles, and circles, and understand their sides and corners. They are introduced to angles as the space between two lines meeting at a corner, using examples like the sharp point of a cone or the wider opening of a book. The lesson covers basic angle types, such as right angles (like a corner of a book) and acute or obtuse angles. Children also practise identifying shapes in everyday objects, like a wheel (circle) or a box (rectangle), to connect classroom learning with their surroundings. The focus is on recognising and naming these shapes and angles in simple, everyday contexts.

Angle as rotation; right, acute, obtuse with a tester; measuring with a protractor; angles in daily objects and clock hands.

## How many squares: area and perimeter
Children learn to find the area and perimeter of squares using simple rules. The area is the space inside a square, calculated by multiplying the side length by itself, like 3 cm × 3 cm = 9 sq. cm. The perimeter is the distance around the square, found by adding all four side lengths, such as 4 cm + 4 cm + 4 cm + 4 cm = 16 cm. They use these methods to solve real-life problems, like finding how much carpet is needed for a room (area) or how much fence is required for a garden (perimeter). These concepts help them understand shapes and measurements in their daily lives.

Area of rectangles and irregular shapes on squared paper; perimeter comparisons; split shapes to find area.

## Parts and wholes: fractions deep dive
In Class 5 Mathematics, children learn about fractions as parts of a whole, understanding that a fraction has a numerator (top number) and a denominator (bottom number). They learn to identify fractions like ½, ¼, and ¾ using real-life examples, such as dividing a chocolate bar or a pizza into equal parts. The topic also covers comparing fractions with the same denominator and finding equivalent fractions by multiplying both the numerator and denominator by the same number. For example, ½ is the same as 2/4 or 3/6 because they represent equal parts of a whole. Children practice writing fractions from given shapes and simple word problems to strengthen their understanding of parts and wholes.

Equivalent fractions; compare unlike fractions; add/subtract with different denominators (LCM idea); fraction of a quantity; fraction stories from Indian markets.

## Does it look the same: symmetry and views
In Class 5, students learn about symmetry by finding shapes that look the same when folded along a line called the line of symmetry, like a square or a butterfly. They identify objects that have one or more lines of symmetry, such as an alphabet 'A' or a 'T'. The topic also teaches how to see different views of the same object, like front, side, and top views, using real-life examples such as a table or a cup. Children practise drawing these views to understand how objects look from different angles. They learn to recognise and match shapes with their mirror images, helping them understand reflection symmetry in everyday objects.

Line symmetry; mirror halves; rotation; top/side/front views of objects and maps.

## Be my multiple, I'll be your factor
Children learn that a multiple is a number we get when we skip-count by another number, like 2, 4, 6, 8 are multiples of 2. A factor is a number that divides into another number exactly, like 1, 2, 3, 6 are factors of 6. They understand that if a number is a multiple of another, the second number is its factor, such as 12 is a multiple of 3 because 3 × 4 = 12, so 3 is a factor of 12. They practice finding multiples by counting in groups and factors by dividing numbers without any remainders, like 1, 3, 5, 15 are all factors of 15 because they divide 15 perfectly. This helps them see how numbers are related and builds their number sense.

Multiples and factors together; common multiples, LCM; common factors, HCF; prime factorisation; HCF-LCM use in problems.

## Can you see the pattern: number patterns
In Class 5, children learn to identify and describe number patterns, such as sequences that increase or decrease by a fixed number, like 2, 4, 6, 8 or 50, 45, 40, 35. They explore patterns using addition and subtraction, such as the doubling pattern in 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, where each number is twice the previous one. Students also recognise patterns in numbers formed by skip counting, like 5, 10, 15, 20 or 10, 20, 30, 40. They extend these patterns by finding the next number in the sequence, such as continuing 3, 6, 9, 12 to 15. Through simple examples, children understand how rules help predict and complete number patterns.

Number and letter patterns; rule-finding; magic squares; growing patterns expressed as rules.

## Mapping your way
In this topic, children learn to read and draw simple maps using symbols and a key to understand different places like schools, parks, and markets. They understand directions like north, south, east, and west to find places on a map, just like following a path. They also learn to measure distances between two places on a map using a scale, which tells how much distance one small picture or box stands for. For example, if one small box on the map shows one kilometre, then five boxes show five kilometres. This helps them picture real-world places in a smaller, easier way.

Scale and map distance; directions; use of degrees in turning; reading simple road maps.

## Boxes and sketches: 3D shapes
In this topic, children learn to recognise and name common 3D shapes like cubes, cuboids, cylinders, cones, and spheres. They understand that 3D shapes have faces, edges, and corners, which they can count and describe using simple words. For example, a cube has 6 square faces, 12 edges, and 8 corners, while a cylinder has 2 circular faces and 1 curved edge. They also practise making sketches of these shapes by drawing their outlines and identifying them in everyday objects like boxes, balls, and cans. Children explore how 3D shapes look different from various angles and learn to sort them based on their features.

Nets of cube, cuboid, cylinder; count faces, edges, vertices; drawing 3D on 2D isometric grids.

## Tenths and hundredths: decimals
In Class 5, we learn about tenths and hundredths, which are parts of a whole written as decimals. A tenth is one part of ten equal parts, like 0.1, and a hundredth is one part of one hundred equal parts, like 0.01. We learn to write numbers with decimal points, such as 3.4 for 3 whole parts and 4 tenths. We also compare decimals by looking at the digits after the decimal point, like 0.25 is less than 0.5. For example, 50 paise is written as ₹0.50 because it is 50 hundredths of a rupee.

Decimal place value; compare, order; add/subtract decimals; decimals as money and length; a small taste of multiplying by 10.

## Area and its boundary
Children learn to find the area of shapes by counting the number of small square units that fit inside them, like how many tiles cover the floor. The area is the total space taken up by a shape, and we measure it using square centimetres (cm²) or square metres (m²). For example, if a rectangle has 4 rows of 3 squares, its area is 4 × 3 = 12 square centimetres. They also compare the areas of different shapes and understand that bigger shapes have more area. The boundary of a shape is its outer edge, and its length is called the perimeter, which we find by adding all the sides together.

Standard area units; area of rectangles; relate area and perimeter; break compound shapes into rectangles.

## Ways to multiply and divide
In this topic, children learn to multiply and divide numbers using simple methods. They use the multiplication table to find answers, like 4 x 5 = 20. They also learn to break big numbers into smaller parts to make multiplication easier, for example, 12 x 3 can be solved as 10 x 3 = 30 and 2 x 3 = 6, then 30 + 6 = 36. For division, they learn to share or group items equally, such as dividing 24 chocolates among 6 friends to get 4 each. They practice these methods with everyday problems to build confidence.

Array/area model then standard algorithm for 2-digit multipliers; divide by 2-digit divisors; estimate first, check after; multi-step problems.

## How big, how heavy
In Class 5, we learn to compare how big or heavy things are using non-standard and standard units. We measure length using hand spans and then centimetres, and weight using stones and then grams and kilograms. We understand that heavy things need strong force to lift, like a school bag, while light things need little force, like a pencil. We also learn to estimate the weight of common objects around us, such as a mango or a book. This helps us understand why we need standard units to measure correctly.

Volume by filling cubes (unit cubes); weight comparisons; choose sensible units; simple data: bar graphs with scales.
