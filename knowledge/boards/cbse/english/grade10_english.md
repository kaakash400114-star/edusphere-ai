# CBSE Class 10 English - Language & Literature approach

## Reading: discursive and case-based passages
Students learn to read discursive and case-based passages carefully to understand the author’s main idea and supporting arguments. They identify different types of evidence used in such passages, such as facts, examples, and expert opinions, to support the central theme. Students also analyse the structure of the passage, distinguishing between the introduction, body paragraphs, and conclusion. For example, in a case study passage about a community project, they can find the problem stated in the beginning, steps taken in the middle, and the final result at the end. This helps them think critically and answer questions that ask for specific information or inference.

Boards-format MCQs and short answers; inference, tone, writer's purpose; headline matching; vocabulary clusters.

## Writing: formal letter (complaint/enquiry/application)
In this topic, students learn to write formal letters for complaints, enquiries, or applications following a specific format. They include the sender's address, date, receiver's address, subject line, salutation, body paragraphs, and a polite closing with signature. For complaints, they clearly state the issue and suggest solutions, like writing to a shop manager about a defective product. In enquiries, they ask for specific information such as course details from a school, while applications request something such as a leave of absence. The language must be respectful, formal, and free of slang or contractions.

CBSE layout: sender, date, receiver, subject, salutation, body, closing; 120-150 word discipline; register control.

## Writing: analytical paragraph (data/chart based)
In this topic, you learn to write an analytical paragraph by observing a chart or graph, identifying its main features, and explaining them in your own words. You must begin with a clear topic sentence that states the most important information shown in the data. You then use specific figures and numbers from the chart as evidence to support your topic sentence. Finally, you write one or two concluding sentences to sum up your observations. For example, if a bar chart shows the number of books read by students in a month, you would write about which grade read the most books and how many.

Interpret graphs/tables: trend verbs (increase, decline, stabilise), comparatives, summary sentence, no opinions.

## Grammar: determiners, tenses, modals gap-filling
Students learn to use determiners like 'the', 'a', 'an', 'this', and 'these' to specify nouns. They study tenses to show when actions happen, such as the simple present tense for regular actions ("I play football") and the past continuous tense for ongoing actions in the past ("They were studying"). Modals like 'can', 'must', 'should', and 'may' are used to express ability, necessity, or permission, for example, "You must complete your homework." In gap-filling exercises, students choose the correct determiners, tenses, or modals to complete sentences accurately, ensuring proper grammar usage.

Board cloze format; error-correction across a passage; tense-modality precision.

## Grammar: reported speech and voice transformations
In Class 10, you will learn how to change sentences from direct speech to reported speech by shifting the tense of the verb, changing pronouns, and adjusting words like 'this' and 'now'. For example, 'She said, "I am happy"' becomes 'She said that she was happy'. You will also study active and passive voice, where in the active voice the subject performs the action, like 'The boy writes a letter', and in the passive voice the subject receives the action, like 'A letter is written by the boy'. Mastering these helps in expressing ideas clearly and accurately in writing.

Exam patterns: dialogue-to-reported; passive with modals; keep meaning intact.

## Literature: First Flight prose
Students in Class 10 study selected prose pieces from the First Flight textbook to understand character development and moral values. They learn to analyse the motivations and actions of characters like the bangle makers in "The Sermon at Benares" or the boy in "The Thief's Story" to draw life lessons. The curriculum teaches students to identify the central theme, such as justice in "The Proposal," and the author's purpose for writing. They also practice summarising a story's main events and explaining how the setting influences the plot. Through these stories, children enhance their comprehension and critical thinking skills.

Theme, character, extract-based questions; value-based answers with textual evidence; title significance.

## Literature: First Flight poetry
In the First Flight poetry unit, students learn to identify and appreciate the central theme and main idea of a poem, such as patriotism in 'The Frog and the Nightingale'. They understand different poetic devices like simile, metaphor, and personification with examples from 'Dust of Snow' where the hemlock tree is personified. Students also analyse the rhyme scheme and rhythm of poems, identifying patterns like 'AABB' in 'Animals'. They explore the emotions and messages conveyed through imagery and symbolic language, as seen in 'Fire and Ice' where fire represents desire. The lesson builds comprehension and critical thinking by encouraging students to interpret deeper meanings.

Central idea; devices: metaphor, simile, personification, alliteration, imagery; rhyme-scheme identification; message in 40-50 words.

## Literature: supplementary reader
In the supplementary reader, students learn to read and understand different types of stories and poems from various authors and cultures. They identify the main characters, plot, and setting of a story, and find the theme or moral lesson it teaches. For poems, they notice the rhythm, rhyme, and imagery that the poet uses to create meaning. Students also learn new words and phrases from the text and use context clues to understand their meaning. They practice answering questions about the text by recalling details and expressing their own ideas clearly.

Plot-based questions; character motivation; short value questions.

## Vocabulary and exam register
In Class 10, students learn to identify and use the formal language required for examinations, known as the exam register. They practice replacing common words with more precise and advanced vocabulary, like using “commence” instead of “begin” or “sufficient” instead of “enough”. Grammar rules focus on constructing clear and concise sentences to avoid errors. Students also learn to expand simple ideas into detailed explanations, as seen when describing a picture by adding specific adjectives and adverbs. This helps them write answers that are both accurate and well-structured for exams.

Formal vs informal; phrasal verbs; idioms in context; avoid Indianism errors in exam English.

## Full-length practice structure
In this topic, students learn the structure of full-length practice exams for English Language Arts, which include sections like reading comprehension, writing, and grammar. They are taught to follow a specific format for each section, such as answering reading questions in complete sentences and organising their writing into clear paragraphs with a beginning, middle, and end. For example, in writing tasks, they practice using proper structure for essays or letters, ensuring they include all necessary details. The practice also helps them understand how to manage their time effectively during the exam, allocating minutes for each section. By learning this structure, students become more confident and prepared for the final examination.

Section-wise time plan; presentation: underline keywords, leave margins; attempt strategy for MCQ + subjective mix.
