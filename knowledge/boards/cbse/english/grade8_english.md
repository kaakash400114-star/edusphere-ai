# CBSE Class 8 English - NCERT (Honeydew) approach

## Determiners, articles and quantifiers revisited through texts
In Class 8, we learn about determiners, articles, and quantifiers by reading and analysing different texts. We understand how determiners like 'this', 'that', 'these', and 'those' point out specific nouns, while articles 'a', 'an', and 'the' tell us if a noun is general or specific. Quantifiers such as 'many', 'few', 'some', and 'all' help us describe the quantity of nouns. For example, we use 'a' for a single countable noun like 'a book' and 'the' for a particular book that we both know about. We also see how to correctly use quantifiers in sentences to show if there are a lot or only a few of something.

Precise use with uncountables; zero article cases (school, hospital as purpose).

## Tense system review: narration harmony
In this topic, students learn to maintain tense harmony when changing the form of a sentence, like converting direct speech to indirect speech. They understand that the tense of the reported verb changes according to the tense shift rules, for example, present simple tense changes to past simple tense. Students also practice adjusting time and place expressions to match the new narrative context, such as changing "today" to "that day". Mastering these rules helps children write stories and reports correctly without any mistakes in tenses.

Keep time consistency in long narrations; perfect forms in reporting.

## Active-passive and reported speech mastery
In Grade 8, you learn to change the voice of a sentence from active to passive and back. An active sentence has a subject doing the action, like "Rahul reads a book," while a passive sentence shows the subject receiving the action, like "A book is read by Rahul." For reported speech, you change a direct speech sentence, like "He said, 'I am happy'," into indirect speech, like "He said that he was happy." You also change pronouns and verb tenses to make the sentence correct, such as changing 'here' to 'there'. These rules help you write and speak in many different ways.

Multi-tense transformations; instructions and requests in passive (imperatives: Let the work be done).

## Clauses and complex sentences
In Class 8, you learn to find and identify two main types of clauses: the main clause and the subordinate clause. A main clause makes a complete sense by itself and can stand as a simple sentence, for example, "The dog barked loudly." A subordinate clause, however, does not make sense on its own and begins with words like because, since, if, when, although, or which. When we join a main clause and a subordinate clause, we form a complex sentence, such as "The dog barked loudly because it saw a stranger." This helps you write more interesting and detailed sentences.

Identify noun-adjective-adverb clauses; combine simple sentences; punctuate complex sentences.

## Non-finites: infinitives, gerunds, participles
In Grade 8, you learn to identify and use non-finite verbs, which do not show tense and are not limited by a subject. An infinitive is "to" + a verb, like "to play" or "to read". A gerund is a verb ending in "-ing" that works as a noun, for example, "Swimming is fun". A participle is a verb form that works like an adjective, such as "the crying baby" (present participle) or "the broken chair" (past participle). You will also learn to use these forms correctly in sentences, like "I like to draw" or "Running fast is good for health".

To-swim is fun; enjoy swimming; the crying baby, a broken chair.

## Modals for deduction: may-might-could-must have
Students learn to use modals like may, might, could, and must have to make guesses about past events based on evidence. For example, seeing muddy shoes, we can say, "Rahul must have played in the garden," because it is a strong possibility. We use might have or may have when we are less certain, like if we see wet towels and say, "She may have gone for a swim." The modal "could have" is used for both possible and past abilities, such as, "He could have finished his homework, but he did not." This helps us talk about what probably happened in the past even when we did not see it ourselves.

"must have eaten" certainty about past; possibility ladders.

## Vocabulary: prefixes-suffixes, idioms, phrasal verbs
Students in Class 8 learn to build new words by adding prefixes to the beginning and suffixes to the end of root words. A prefix, like 'un-' or 'dis-', changes the meaning of a word, for example, 'happy' becomes 'unhappy'. A suffix, such as '-ful' or '-less', changes a word’s part of speech, for example, 'care' (a noun) becomes 'careful' (an adjective). They also study common phrasal verbs, which are a verb and a particle together that have a new meaning, like 'give up' meaning 'to stop trying'. Additionally, they learn idioms, which are phrases that have a figurative meaning different from their literal words, such as 'break the ice' meaning to start a conversation in a tense situation.

un-, dis-, -able, -tion; idiom meanings in context; separable-inseparable phrasals.

## Comprehension: literary devices
In this topic, students learn to identify and understand common literary devices used in stories and poems. They study similes, which compare two things using "like" or "as" for example, "The moon is like a silver coin." They also learn about metaphors, which directly compare two things without using "like" or "as," for instance, "Time is a thief." Additionally, students are introduced to personification, where human qualities are given to non-human things, like "The wind whispered secrets." Recognising these devices helps students better appreciate the beauty and creativity in the texts they read.

Simile, metaphor, personification, alliteration; tone and mood; author's purpose.

## Writing: formal letters, emails, article, speech
Students learn to write formal letters, emails, articles, and speeches using specific structures and language. For formal letters, they include sender’s address, date, recipient’s address, subject, salutation, body, closing, and signature in a correct format. Emails follow a similar structure but use a subject line and greetings like "Dear Sir/Ma'am". Articles have a catchy title, an introduction, body paragraphs with main ideas, and a conclusion, while speeches begin with a greeting and clear topic, have main points in order, and end with a thank you. All writing must be polite, clear, and use correct grammar, avoiding short forms like "can't" or "won't".

Formal letter of complaint/enquiry; email etiquette; article with heading-byline-ideas; speech with opening-hook-close.

## Listening-speaking integration
In Grade 8, students learn to listen carefully to understand main ideas and important details from speeches, discussions, and announcements. They practice speaking clearly and confidently in small groups and class discussions, following rules like taking turns and respecting others' opinions. They also learn to express their own ideas using complete sentences and appropriate words, for example, explaining a book’s story or sharing a personal experience. Students are taught to listen for the speaker’s purpose and feelings, and to ask relevant questions to show their understanding. Through these activities, they develop good communication skills for both listening and speaking in everyday situations.

Take dictation of a paragraph; debate basics: for-against with reasons; pronounce multisyllabic academic words clearly.
