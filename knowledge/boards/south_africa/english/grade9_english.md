# South African (CAPS) Grade 9 English

## Transactional Writing: Formal Letters
Learners are able to write formal letters for specific contexts. They master the structure: sender’s address, date, recipient’s address, subject line, formal salutation (e.g., “Dear Mr. Dlamini”), body with logical paragraphs, formal closing (e.g., “Yours faithfully”), and signature. They learn to use a formal register, avoid contractions, and state the purpose clearly. For example, a formal letter to a local councillor requesting repairs to a community park requires factual details, a polite tone, and a clear call to action.

## Transactional Writing: Business Reports
Learners are able to structure and write concise business reports. They learn to include a title, clear introduction stating the purpose, main body with headings and bullet points, and a conclusion with recommendations. They must use objective language, present information logically, and avoid personal opinions. For example, a report analysing the attendance and ticket sales at a Proteas cricket match requires data, findings, and a summary of the event’s success or challenges.

## Poetry: Appreciation and Analysis
Learners are able to analyse poetry by identifying imagery, figurative language, and sound devices. They learn to interpret themes, mood, and the poet’s message. For example, analysing a poem about Table Mountain involves identifying the use of personification to describe the mountain’s majesty and discussing how the imagery reflects South African identity. They learn to support their interpretations with evidence from the text and explain the effect of poetic devices.

## Novel Study: Theme and Characterisation
Learners will engage in systematic thematic analysis by examining how central concepts like prejudice or justice are developed through plot and character. They will learn to trace a character's psychological and social evolution, identifying how traits are revealed through direct and indirect methods, such as the character's thoughts, words, and interactions with others. A key concept is the role of conflict in driving character change, where a protagonist’s response to adversity reveals their core values and directly illustrates the novel’s central themes. For example, a learner might analyse how a character’s refusal to conform to societal pressures in the face of bullying demonstrates both the theme of individualism and the protagonist's development of personal integrity.

## Language Structures: Conjunctions and Discourse Markers
Learners are able to use a range of conjunctions and discourse markers to create complex and coherent sentences. They learn to use coordinating conjunctions (e.g., ‘and’, ‘but’, ‘so’), subordinating conjunctions (e.g., ‘because’, ‘although’), and discourse markers (e.g., ‘furthermore’, ‘however’, ‘in conclusion’) to show relationships between ideas. For example, writing an argument about the benefits of hosting a braai requires using ‘however’ to introduce a counter-argument and ‘furthermore’ to add supporting points.

## Language Structures: Active and Passive Voice
Learners are able to distinguish between active and passive voice and use them appropriately. They learn that the active voice (e.g., ‘The learners donated rand to the charity’) is direct and preferred for clarity, while the passive voice (e.g., ‘Rand was donated to the charity by the learners’) is used to shift focus to the object. They practise converting between voices and identifying the correct construction in different contexts, such as reporting on a school fundraising event.
