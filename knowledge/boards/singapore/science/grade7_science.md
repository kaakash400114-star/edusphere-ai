# Singapore Curriculum (MOE) Grade 7 Science  

## Diversity of Living Things
Students classify organisms using a hierarchical taxonomic system from Kingdom to Species to understand evolutionary relationships. They explore Singapore's biodiversity within specific habitats, analysing adaptations like the mangrove tree’s pneumatophores for respiration in waterlogged soil and the proboscis monkey's specialised nose to amplify warning calls. This framework is applied to distinct ecosystems, comparing the structural diversity of Sungei Buloh’s mangroves with the species richness of Pulau Semakau’s coral reefs. Specific examples, such as the parasitic Rafflesia with its large, foul-smelling flower to attract pollinators, illustrate unique survival strategies in the local environment.

## Cells as the Basic Unit of Life
All living organisms are composed of cells, the basic units of life, which can be unicellular or multicellular. Plant cells possess a rigid cell wall, chloroplasts for photosynthesis, and a large central vacuole, while animal cells lack a cell wall and chloroplasts. The nucleus controls cellular activities and contains genetic material, whereas mitochondria generate energy for the cell through respiration. Students use microscopes to observe onion epidermis to identify plant cells and cheek cells to view the nucleus and cell membrane. This practical investigation in the Singapore curriculum reinforces understanding of cellular organisation through direct observation of local biological specimens.

## Forces and their Effects
Forces, measured in Newtons (N), are pushes or pulls that can change an object's state of motion or cause deformation. Newton’s First Law states that an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion stays in motion at a constant velocity unless acted upon by an unbalanced force, exemplified by a stationary hawker cart remaining in place until pushed. Newton’s Second Law describes how acceleration is produced when a force acts on a mass, expressed by the formula F = m × a, and is demonstrated when an MRT train decelerates. The gravitational force, Earth's pull on an object, is calculated as weight (W) using W = m × g, where 'g' is the acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s²). Friction, a force opposing motion, is investigated through experiments comparing the grip of different shoe soles on wet surfaces to understand its practical implications.

## Energy Forms and Conversion
The law of conservation of energy states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only converted between different forms. Students learn to identify and trace these energy transformations in common contexts, such as the conversion of chemical energy in food to thermal energy during cooking. Similarly, they analyse how gravitational potential energy in the Singapore Flyer is converted to kinetic energy as it moves. The model method is used to calculate energy efficiency, a key concept for understanding how useful energy output from an appliance compares to the total energy input. By analysing household devices, students evaluate how different forms of energy are managed and the importance of minimising energy loss for sustainability.

## The Air Around Us
Air is a mixture of gases, primarily nitrogen (78%) and oxygen (21%), with other gases in trace amounts. Students utilise the Pollutant Standards Index (PSI) to assess Singapore's air quality, correlating index levels with health advisories for the public. Through experiments like the candle-in-jar investigation, they demonstrate that combustion is a chemical reaction requiring oxygen, causing the flame to extinguish when it is depleted. Pictorial models illustrate this invisible process, showing how a burning candle consumes oxygen from the trapped air within a confined space. This understanding is directly applied to real-world events, such as transboundary haze from regional burning, and its impact on daily life in Singapore.

## Heat and Temperature
Temperature is a measure of an object's average kinetic energy, while heat is the total thermal energy transferred due to a temperature difference. Students learn to distinguish between thermal conductors, like the metal prongs of a satay stick that transfer heat quickly, and insulators, such as its wooden handle that prevents heat transfer. They analyse convection currents in HDB flats, where warm air rises near windows and cooler air sinks, creating natural ventilation. The concept of specific heat capacity is introduced, with water's high value requiring significant energy to change its temperature, demonstrated through problems solved using the formula Energy = mass × specific heat capacity × change in temperature.
