# Canadian Curriculum Grade 3 Mathematics
## Understanding Numbers
Students learn to read, represent, and order whole numbers up to 1000. They practice writing numerals (e.g., 257) and words (e.g., "two hundred fifty-seven") and understand place value: hundreds, tens, and ones. Using base-ten blocks, students can build numbers, showing that 3 hundreds, 4 tens, and 5 ones make 345. They compare numbers using greater than (>), less than (<), and equal to (=) signs, for example, 567 is less than 575. Students also use number lines to estimate and order numbers, counting forward and backward in 1s, 2s, 5s, 10s, and 25s.
## Addition and Subtraction
Students develop fluency in adding and subtracting three-digit numbers. They learn the standard algorithms, starting with regrouping, also known as borrowing and carrying. For example, to add 248 + 136, they add the ones (8+6=14, write down 4, carry over 1), then the tens (4+3+1=8), and then the hundreds (2+1=3), for a sum of 384. For subtraction, like 512 - 247, they may need to regroup, turning one ten into ten ones to subtract. Students solve simple, real-life problems, such as finding the total cost of a hockey stick priced at $45 and a puck at $5.
## Multiplication and Division
Grade 3 students are introduced to multiplication and division facts to 7x7. They understand that multiplication means adding equal groups (e.g., 3 groups of 4 is 3x4=12) and division means sharing or grouping equally. Using arrays, like a 4x3 grid of maple leaves, students visualize these concepts. They practice with concrete materials, counters, and drawings. For division, a problem could be: "There are 28 loonies to share equally among 4 children." Students use repeated subtraction (e.g., 28 - 7 - 7 - 7 - 7) to find that each child gets 7 loonies.
## Measurement and Time
Students measure and compare lengths, masses, and capacities using metric units. They measure the length of a hockey stick in centimetres (cm) and the mass of a textbook in grams (g). They estimate and measure the capacity of a juice box in millilitres (mL). For time, students tell time to the nearest minute on both digital and analog clocks. They practice calculating elapsed time, such as finding out how many minutes are from 3:15 PM to 3:45 PM. Students also read calendar dates and understand durations, like how many days are in a school week or a month.
## Geometry and Spatial Sense
Students learn to identify, describe, and compare 2D shapes and 3D figures. They explore polygons like triangles, quadrilaterals, pentagons, and hexagons. They identify 3D figures such as cubes, spheres, cones, pyramids, and cylinders. Students learn the characteristics of these shapes, noting that a cube has 6 square faces. They create patterns and designs, and they understand concepts like symmetry by folding paper shapes to find a line of symmetry. They also learn to describe the location of an object using positional language (e.g., "the hockey puck is to the left of the net").
## Data Management and Probability
Students learn to collect, organize, and interpret data. They conduct surveys, like asking their classmates, "What is your favourite Canadian province?" They record data using tally marks and create bar graphs and pictographs to show the results. For example, a pictograph of the CN Tower's height could use one symbol to represent 10 metres. Students interpret the graphs to answer questions like, "Which province was the most popular?" They are also introduced to probability, discussing the likelihood of events using terms like "certain," "possible," and "impossible," such as "it is certain the sun will rise in the east."
