# Canadian Curriculum Grade 11 Mathematics  

## Rational Expressions and Equations
Students simplify, add, subtract, multiply, and divide rational expressions by applying the same rules as for numerical fractions. Essential to this process is factoring polynomials to identify non-permissible values, which are the values that make any denominator zero and must be excluded from the solution set. For example, to simplify (x²-9)/(x+3), students factor the numerator to (x-3)(x+3) and then cancel the common factor (x+3), resulting in the simplified expression x-3, with the non-permissible value x ≠ -3. To solve rational equations like 2/(x-1) = 3/x, students cross-multiply to eliminate the denominators, resulting in the linear equation 2x = 3(x-1), which is then solved to find the valid solution.

## Trigonometric Functions
Students analyze the unit circle and special angles to determine exact trigonometric ratios, developing the sine and cosine laws as tools for solving non-right triangles. They manipulate the functions y = a sin(b(x - c)) + d and y = a cos(b(x - c)) + d, establishing how parameters 'a', 'b', 'c', and 'd' affect amplitude, period, phase shift, and vertical displacement. For example, the function y = -3 cos(2(x + 45°)) + 1 has an amplitude of 3, a period of 180°, a phase shift of 45° left, and a vertical shift of 1 unit up. These concepts are applied to model periodic phenomena, such as calculating the height of Niagara Falls using angle of elevation or analyzing tidal patterns in the Bay of Fundy.

## Exponential and Logarithmic Functions
Students analyze exponential functions using the form y = ab^x to model growth or decay, with applications like compound interest calculated through A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) for Canadian investments. They explore logarithmic functions, understanding that log_b(x) = y is equivalent to b^y = x, and convert between these forms to solve problems. Key methods include solving exponential equations using logarithms, such as applying the common logarithm to find x in 2^x = 8 by taking log₂(8) = x. Students also apply logarithmic rules, including the product, quotient, and power rules, to simplify expressions. These concepts are reinforced with examples like modeling the growth of Canada’s loonie or calculating half-lives in radioactive decay.

## Quadratic Relations
Quadratic relations are conic sections defined by second-degree equations in two variables; parabolas, represented by y = ax² + bx + c, model projectile motion like a hockey puck's path, while circles and ellipses use standard forms like (x-h)² + (y-k)² = r² and (x-h)²/a² + (y-k)²/b² = 1, respectively, to define their geometric properties. Key methods include graphing by transforming the standard form to identify the vertex, focus, and directrix for parabolas, or the centre and radii for circles and ellipses. Solving these relations requires algebraic tools like completing the square to convert equations into standard forms and applying the quadratic formula, x = [-b ± √(b²-4ac)]/2a, to find roots and points of intersection.

## Financial Mathematics
Students apply mathematical formulas to solve real-world financial problems, such as calculating future value using FV = PV(1 + i)^n for an initial $10,000 investment in a Guaranteed Investment Certificate (GIC). They determine periodic loan and mortgage payments through annuity formulas, for instance, calculating monthly payments on a $450,000 mortgage with a 5-year fixed rate from a Canadian bank. Learners also analyze amortization schedules to understand how each payment divides into interest and principal. By incorporating real Canadian interest rates and financial products, students evaluate long-term debt scenarios like car loans. This builds practical skills for budgeting, saving, and understanding the time value of money for informed personal financial decisions.

## Geometry and Measurement
Students solve problems involving perimeter, area, and volume of 2D and 3D shapes, including prisms, pyramids, and spheres. For example, calculating the volume of a hockey puck (a cylinder) or the surface area of the Rogers Centre dome. The metric system is used exclusively, with conversions between units like metres and centimetres. Trigonometry is integrated to solve for unknown sides or angles in right triangles, such as determining the height of a maple tree using shadow measurements.
