# Canadian Curriculum Grade 8 English


## Literary Devices and Analysis
Students analyze how authors use literary devices to create meaning. They will study simile ("as brave as a hockey player"), metaphor ("the CN Tower was a silver needle"), and personification ("the old maple tree sighed"). Lessons focus on identifying devices, explaining their purpose, and analyzing their effect on the reader. Students will explore how the use of symbolism, such as the loonie representing national unity, contributes to a text's deeper themes. This analytical skill is applied to classic and contemporary Canadian literature.

## Grammar and Punctuation
Students analyze sentence structures to apply punctuation rules correctly, using commas to set off introductory clauses and non-restrictive elements as in "Although it was raining, the game continued." They learn to join closely related independent clauses with a semicolon, as demonstrated in "The test was difficult; however, most students passed." The rules for subject-verb agreement are applied to compound subjects, requiring plural verbs as in "Neither the coach nor the players were discouraged." Apostrophes are used for singular possession ("the team's captain"), plural possession ("the players' equipment"), and contractions ("they've won"). Additionally, students consistently apply Canadian spelling conventions, using words like "colour" and "theatre" instead of their American alternatives.

## Structured Writing
Students learn to write in a variety of formal, structured formats. They develop skills in creating well-organized five-paragraph expository essays, with a clear thesis statement, supporting body paragraphs with evidence, and a concluding summary. Instruction includes crafting persuasive arguments using the five Ws and H (Who, What, Where, When, Why, How) for clarity. Students also practice writing formal business letters, ensuring proper salutations, body structure, and closings appropriate for Canadian communication standards.

## Media Literacy
Students critically deconstruct various forms of media. They analyze the techniques used in advertising, including persuasive language and imagery, to target specific audiences. Lessons involve examining the portrayal of Canadian identity in films, news reports, and social media, identifying potential bias and stereotypes. Students learn to differentiate between facts, opinions, and propaganda, and to evaluate the credibility and purpose of online sources, particularly concerning issues relevant to Canadian provinces and territories.

## Vocabulary Development
Students develop vocabulary by analyzing words from Latin and Greek roots, using prefixes and suffixes to deduce meanings of unfamiliar terms, such as deducing "benevolent" from "bene" (good). The instruction focuses on precise word choice through the study of synonyms (e.g., "large," "enormous," "vast"), antonyms (e.g., "ancient," "modern"), and homophones (e.g., "there," "their," "they're"). Students apply these skills by learning and using vocabulary specific to Canadian culture and geography, such as "outport" (small coastal community in Newfoundland), "prairie" (vast grassland region), and "Shield" (the Canadian Shield rock formation). This contextual understanding enables students to communicate with precision and deepen their comprehension of diverse texts.

## Oral Presentation Skills
Students develop confidence and clarity in spoken communication. They learn to structure and deliver oral reports, including an introduction, main points with supporting details, and a conclusion. Emphasis is placed on pacing, volume, and using appropriate body language. Instruction on active listening is integral, with students practicing providing constructive feedback to peers. Presentations often incorporate topics related to Canadian studies, such as a report on a province's history or the significance of a national symbol like the maple leaf.
