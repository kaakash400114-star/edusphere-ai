# Canadian Curriculum Grade 11 English

## Literary Analysis and Critical Reading
Students learn to deconstruct complex literary works from diverse Canadian authors, including novelists, playwrights, and poets. They employ formalist, feminist, and post-colonial lenses to analyze themes, symbolism, and narrative structure. A key method involves identifying literary devices such as allegory and juxtaposition and explaining their effect on meaning. For example, students would analyze the symbolic use of the vast prairie in a prairie novel to represent isolation or the resilience of the human spirit, moving beyond simple plot summary to articulate thematic depth.

## Academic and Persuasive Writing
Students master the structure and style of academic essays, focusing on thesis development, logical argumentation, and evidence integration. They learn to craft multi-paragraph arguments using primary and secondary sources to support claims, properly citing them in a recognized format like MLA. They practice writing persuasively on topics relevant to Canada, such as the national impact of the hockey industry or the economic significance of maple syrup production, ensuring their arguments are coherent, well-organized, and supported by credible research.

## Media Studies and Digital Literacy
Students examine the influence of various media forms on Canadian society and culture. They learn to deconstruct messages in advertising, news broadcasts, and social media, analyzing techniques like framing, propaganda, and audience targeting. A core skill is identifying bias and distinguishing between credible journalism and misinformation. Students critically analyze how media represents Canadian identity, exploring the portrayal of provinces or national symbols like the loonie or the CN Tower, and assess the social and political implications of these representations.

## Canadian Literature and Identity
This unit focuses on the study of works that reflect the diversity of Canadian experiences. Students analyze texts that explore themes of landscape, identity, multiculturalism, and regionalism within Canada. They will examine how authors from different provinces use setting to influence character and plot, contrasting the urban setting of a Toronto-based novel with a rural setting in Atlantic Canada. By studying works by authors like Margaret Atwood or Richard Wagamese, students understand the complexities of forging a collective national identity from varied individual and cultural perspectives.

## Functional and Transactional Communication
Students develop essential communication skills for real-world Canadian contexts. This includes writing formal business letters for job applications, composing professional emails, and creating clear, concise reports. They also practice oral skills through formal presentations and group discussions. A key learning outcome is tailoring communication for different audiences and purposes. For instance, a student might draft a formal proposal to a municipal council about improving a local community rink, demonstrating an understanding of professional tone and persuasive language.

## Creative Writing and Expression
Students experiment with various creative writing forms, including poetry, short stories, and personal narratives, honing their own distinct voice. They are taught specific techniques for imagery, dialogue, and point of view to create vivid and compelling narratives. Students are encouraged to draw on Canadian cultural elements for inspiration, such as writing a short story set during a long winter in a prairie town or crafting a poem that evokes the feeling of standing at the foot of the CN Tower, applying literary conventions to express original ideas.
