# Canadian Curriculum Grade 7 Science  
## Interactions in the Environment  
Students explore the structure and relationships within ecosystems. They learn about producers, consumers, and decomposers, and how energy flows through a food chain, using examples like algae (producer), a loon (consumer), and fungi (decomposer). Trophic levels are explained, and the concept of a food web is introduced. The roles of biotic and abiotic factors are examined, such as how a change in beaver populations impacts a pond's water levels and fish habitat. Students investigate the water and carbon cycles, understanding how water moves from lakes to vapour and back, and how carbon is exchanged between living things and the atmosphere.  

## Pure Substances and Mixtures  
Students classify matter as pure substances (elements or compounds) or mixtures (heterogeneous or homogeneous). They learn that elements, like gold (Au) or oxygen (O₂), cannot be broken down, while compounds, such as water (H₂O), are made of two or more elements. Mixtures, like trail mix, combine substances without chemical bonding. Techniques for separating mixtures are studied, including filtration (to separate sand from water) and distillation (to purify maple syrup by boiling off water). Canadian examples are used, such as separating the components of air or extracting minerals from ore.  

## Heat and Temperature  
Students differentiate between heat (energy transfer) and temperature (a measure of thermal energy). They explore how heat moves through conduction (a metal spoon in hot chocolate), convection (warm air rising in a hockey arena), and radiation (the sun warming the Earth). The Celsius (°C) scale is used, with examples like the freezing point of water (0°C) and body temperature (37°C). Students learn about thermal expansion and contraction, explaining why railway tracks have small gaps. They also investigate insulators, like a winter coat trapping body heat, and conductors, such as a metal CN Tower dissipating heat from a lightning strike.  

## Structures and Forces  
Students analyze the forces acting on structures, including tension, compression, and torsion. They learn that engineers in Canada design buildings to withstand heavy snow loads, like the roofs in provinces such as Quebec. The concept of centre of gravity is introduced, using examples like balancing on a hockey skate. Simple machines are explored, including levers (a seesaw), pulleys (raising a flag), and inclined planes (a ramp). Students apply their knowledge to evaluate structures, assessing how the CN Tower’s tapered shape helps it resist strong winds, ensuring stability in Toronto’s varied climate.  

## Earth’s Crust  
Students examine the structure of Earth’s layers, from the crust to the core. They study the theory of plate tectonics, learning how plates move and create earthquakes or mountains, like the Rocky Mountains. Canadian examples include the Pacific Plate causing quakes in British Columbia. Students identify rocks as igneous (formed from magma, like granite), sedimentary (compressed layers, like sandstone), or metamorphic (altered by heat, like marble). They analyze the rock cycle, explaining how volcanic activity in the creation of new landforms in places like Newfoundland contributes to Canada’s geology.  

## Electrical Applications  
Students explore the principles of electricity, including current, voltage, and resistance. They learn about series and parallel circuits, using examples like holiday lights in a Canadian winter. Ohm’s Law (V = IR) is introduced, with calculations using simple values, such as a 1.5-volt battery powering a 3-ohm light bulb. Students study energy consumption, comparing the power use of a hair dryer to a hockey arena’s scoreboard. They analyze electrical safety, including fuses and breakers, and discuss renewable sources, like hydroelectric power from dams in Manitoba, and how wind turbines in Alberta generate clean electricity for communities.
