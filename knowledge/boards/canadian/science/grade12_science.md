# Canadian Curriculum Grade 12 Science

## Quantum Mechanics and Wave-Particle Duality
Students explore the dual nature of light and matter, learning that particles exhibit wave-like properties and waves can behave as particles. They analyze the photoelectric effect to demonstrate that light is quantized into photons, governed by Einstein's equation $E = hf$. De Broglie's hypothesis extends this to matter, where $\lambda = h/p$ defines the wavelength of a particle. Students apply these concepts using Canadian examples, such as calculating the wavelength of an electron in a scanning electron microscope used at a Toronto research lab, linking quantum theory to real-world Canadian technology.

## Thermodynamics and Energy Transformations
This unit focuses on the laws of thermodynamics, particularly the first and second laws. Students learn that energy cannot be created or destroyed (first law) and that entropy of an isolated system never decreases (second law). They apply these principles to calculate the efficiency of heat engines using the formula $\eta = 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}$. A practical Canadian example involves analyzing the energy efficiency of a hockey arena's refrigeration system, calculating heat loss and work required to maintain the ice, demonstrating how thermodynamics governs everyday Canadian infrastructure.

## Genetics and Biotechnology
Students study advanced genetic concepts, including Mendelian inheritance, pedigrees, and modern biotechniques. They learn to use Punnett squares to predict monohybrid and dihybrid crosses, calculating probabilities of genotypic and phenotypic ratios. The CRISPR-Cas9 system is examined as a tool for gene editing, with applications in Canadian agricultural research, such as developing disease-resistant maple trees. Students analyze real genetic data, such as the probability of inheriting a trait like the ability to roll one's tongue, a common trait studied in Canadian high school labs.

## Kinematics and Dynamics in Two Dimensions
This unit extends kinematics to two dimensions, emphasizing vector components and projectile motion. Students learn to resolve vectors into x and y components and apply kinematic equations to solve problems. They calculate the trajectory of a projectile using $x = v_{0x}t$ and $y = v_{0y}t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2$, incorporating gravity ($g = 9.8 \, \text{m/s}^2$). A Canadian example involves modeling the path of a hockey puck sliding across the ice, accounting for friction and initial velocity, linking physics to Canada's national sport.

## Electrochemistry and Redox Reactions
Students explore oxidation-reduction (redox) reactions and their application in electrochemical cells. They learn to identify oxidation and half-reactions, calculate cell potential using the Nernst equation ($E = E^\circ - \frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q$), and distinguish between galvanic and electrolytic cells. A practical Canadian example involves analyzing the redox reactions in a car battery used in a Canadian winter, calculating how temperature affects cell voltage and performance, demonstrating the real-world relevance of electrochemistry in Canadian life.

## Astronomy and Cosmology
This unit covers the life cycle of stars, Hertzsprung-Russell diagrams, and the Big Bang theory. Students calculate the luminosity and distance to stars using the inverse-square law ($L = 4\pi d^2 F$) and analyze stellar classifications. They explore Canada's contribution to astronomy, such as the Dominion Radio Astrophysical Observatory in British Columbia, studying cosmic microwave background radiation. Students also examine the role of gravity in shaping the universe, linking concepts to the vast scale of the Canadian night sky visible in places like Algonquin Provincial Park.
