# Canadian Curriculum Grade 9 Science

## Fundamentals of Scientific Inquiry
Students learn the scientific method as a structured process for investigation. This includes forming a hypothesis based on observation, designing controlled experiments with variables, collecting quantitative and qualitative data using tools like metre sticks and thermometers, and analyzing results to draw evidence-based conclusions. For example, a student might test how salt concentration affects the freezing point of water, a practical application relevant to Canadian winters. Lab safety protocols are emphasized, including proper handling of chemicals and equipment.

## Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table
Grade 9 science explores the nuclear model of the atom, which consists of protons, neutrons, and electrons. Students learn to use atomic number (protons) and mass number (protons + neutrons) to identify elements. The periodic table is studied as an organized chart of all known elements. Students examine how atomic structure determines an element's chemical properties and position in the table, including the groups (e.g., alkali metals) and periods. Canadian examples include the use of nickel in the five-cent coin and its position on the table.

## Chemical Reactions and Bonding
Students analyze how atoms interact through ionic and covalent bonding. In ionic bonding, metals like sodium transfer electrons to non-metals like chlorine, forming compounds such as table salt (NaCl). Covalent bonding involves the sharing of electrons between non-metals, like in water (H₂O). Students learn to balance chemical equations and identify signs of reactions, such as colour change or temperature shift. A common example involves the reaction of baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) with vinegar (acetic acid) to produce carbon dioxide gas.

## Motion, Forces, and Energy
This unit covers Newton’s three laws of motion. The first law (inertia) explains why a hockey puck sliding on ice will eventually stop due to friction. The second law (F=ma) demonstrates that a greater force is needed to accelerate a Zamboni machine than a hockey puck. The third law states that every action has an equal and opposite reaction, like a rocket launching. Students analyze gravitational, frictional, and normal forces, and explore concepts of potential energy (e.g., a roller coaster at the CN Tower) and kinetic energy.

## Characteristics of Electricity
Students explore the fundamental principles of static and current electricity. Coulomb’s law describes the force between charged objects. Ohm’s law (V=IR) is used to calculate voltage, current, and resistance in circuits. A key application is understanding how electrical energy is converted into light and heat in a household circuit, a crucial skill in a country with diverse climates requiring reliable heating and lighting. Students also learn about the Canadian electrical grid and the role of hydroelectric power from sources like Niagara Falls.

## Environmental Science and Sustainability
This unit examines the impact of human activity on Canadian ecosystems. Students study biotic and abiotic factors, food webs, and the cycling of matter, including the carbon and nitrogen cycles. They analyze environmental issues like acid rain affecting lakes in the Canadian Shield and the impact of the pine beetle on British Columbia’s forests. Solutions are explored, such as sustainable forestry practices and government policies to protect national parks, emphasizing Canada's commitment to preserving its natural heritage, including the iconic maple forests.
