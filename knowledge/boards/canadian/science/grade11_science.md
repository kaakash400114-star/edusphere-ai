# Canadian Curriculum Grade 11 Science

## Kinematics and Dynamics
Students analyze motion using displacement, velocity, and acceleration. They apply kinematic equations for constant acceleration, such as d = v₁t + ½at², and solve problems involving objects in free fall near the Earth's surface (9.8 m/s²). Newton's laws are used to explain forces, including the tension in a hockey puck's rope during a slalom drill or the normal force on a loonie at rest. Vector analysis is essential for resolving forces, like calculating the components of a CN Tower elevator's acceleration.

## Energy and Momentum
The principle of conservation of mechanical energy is central to solving problems involving kinetic and potential energy. Students calculate gravitational potential energy (Eₚ = mgh) using Canadian examples, such as a snowboarder's height at Whistler Blackcomb. They apply the work-energy theorem (W = ΔE) and analyze collisions. The conservation of momentum (p = mv) is used to describe interactions, from a body check in hockey to two railway boxcars coupling in Saskatchewan, distinguishing between elastic and inelastic collisions.

## Waves and Sound
Students explore the properties of mechanical and electromagnetic waves. They calculate wave speed (v = fλ) using the example of sound waves travelling through air at room temperature (343 m/s). Topics include wave superposition, interference, and the Doppler effect, explaining why a police siren's pitch changes as it passes on a Montreal street. The characteristics of sound, including loudness, pitch, and timbre, are analyzed in the context of acoustic properties of a concert hall like the iconic Massey Hall in Toronto.

## Electric, Magnetic, and Electromagnetic Fields
Electrostatic forces are calculated using Coulomb's Law (Fₑ = k|q₁q₂|/r²), where 'k' is Coulomb's constant. Circuit analysis applies Ohm's Law (V = IR) to determine voltage, current, and resistance; students calculate total resistance in series (Rₜ = R₁ + R₂) and parallel (1/Rₜ = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂) circuits, such as one with a 10 Ω and 20 Ω resistor. Magnetic fields are produced by moving charges, as seen in current-carrying wires, with direction determined by the right-hand rule. Faraday's Law of electromagnetic induction states that a changing magnetic field induces a voltage, a principle used in hydroelectric generators at Niagara Falls, where mechanical energy rotates coils within a magnetic field to produce electrical current for the national grid.

## Atomic and Nuclear Physics
This topic covers the structure of the atom, including Bohr's model for the hydrogen atom. Students differentiate between alpha, beta, and gamma radiation and apply the concepts of half-life to calculate the decay of radioactive isotopes. They examine nuclear fission and fusion, discussing the energy released in reactions like those in the CANDU nuclear reactors used to power homes in Ontario and New Brunswick, connecting atomic theory to national energy infrastructure.

## Organic Chemistry
Organic chemistry focuses on carbon-based compounds, beginning with the classification and nomenclature of alkanes, alkenes, and alkynes using IUPAC rules to name their structures and isomers. Students analyze chemical reactions, including combustion, substitution, and addition, applying principles of stoichiometry and reaction mechanisms. Functional groups, such as hydroxyl and carboxyl, are studied to understand the properties and reactivity of alcohols, carboxylic acids, and esters, which are vital to Canada's biofuel and food industries. Polymers are examined through their synthesis, including addition and condensation polymerization, to comprehend their applications in materials like the polymers used in hockey equipment and food packaging for maple syrup.
