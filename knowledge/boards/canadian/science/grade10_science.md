# Canadian Curriculum Grade 10 Science
## Introduction to Scientific Inquiry
Students learn the scientific method is a structured process for investigating phenomena. It begins with making an observation, such as the changing colour of fall maple leaves in Ontario. This leads to forming a testable hypothesis and designing a controlled experiment. For example, one could test how soil pH affects the intensity of red pigment in sugar maples. Data is collected, often using metric units, and analyzed to determine if it supports or refutes the hypothesis. A conclusion is drawn, and the results are communicated, forming the basis for further scientific understanding.

## The Diversity of Living Things
Grade 10 students classify life into five fundamental kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia. They examine how organisms share common ancestry and how this diversity is shaped by natural selection. A key Canadian example is the adaptation of the common loon, with its specialized feet for swimming and distinct call echoing across northern lakes. Students explore how environmental factors, like climate change affecting caribou migration, can act as selective pressures, altering the genetic makeup of populations over successive generations.

## Chemical Reactions and Radioactivity
Students explore the law of conservation of mass, where in a chemical reaction like the combustion of propane (C₃H₈) for a BBQ, mass is neither created nor destroyed. They learn to balance equations, for instance, showing that one methane molecule (CH₄) and two oxygen molecules (O₂) produce one carbon dioxide molecule and two water molecules. Nuclear reactions, such as radioactive decay in uranium used in CANDU reactors, are contrasted with chemical reactions, highlighting the release of far greater energy and the changes to atomic nuclei themselves.

## Motion and Forces
Newton’s three laws of motion are foundational. An example is a hockey player applying force to a puck to accelerate it (First Law). The Second Law (F=ma) explains that a greater force from a player’s stick creates a greater acceleration. The Third Law is demonstrated when a player pushes against the boards to propel themselves forward. Students analyze these forces using metric units (Newtons) and metres per second squared (m/s²), applying them to real-world scenarios like the trajectory of a slap shot from the blue line at Scotiabank Arena in Toronto.

## Electricity and Magnetism
Students build on their understanding of static and current electricity. They analyze circuits using Ohm’s Law (V=IR), calculating that a 12-volt battery connected to a 3-ohm lightbulb creates a 4-ampere current (I = V/R). They learn about series and parallel circuits and the safety features like Ground Fault Circuit Interrupters (GFCIs). The relationship between electricity and magnetism is explored, explaining how electromagnets power the motors in the Canadian-designed and built Canadarm robotic arm used on the International Space Station.

## Earth’s Dynamic Systems
This unit covers geological processes like the rock cycle. Igneous rock, such as granite formed from cooled magma, can become metamorphic rock like gneiss under immense heat and pressure. Students examine the structure of the Earth, learning about the tectonic plate movements responsible for forming the Rocky Mountains. They also study atmospheric science, understanding how global wind patterns and ocean currents, like those influencing the climate of the Maritimes, are driven by the unequal heating of the Earth.
