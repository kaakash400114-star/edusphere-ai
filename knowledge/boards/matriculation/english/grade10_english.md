# Matriculation Class 10 English

## Discursive passage comprehension

In discursive passage comprehension, you learn to read and understand texts that present arguments on different sides of a topic, like advantages and disadvantages of mobile phones. You identify the main idea, supporting facts, and the author’s opinion or tone, such as whether the text is persuasive or neutral. You also learn to summarise the key points by separating the arguments for and against a subject, like whether homework is useful. This helps you build critical thinking skills to evaluate information and form your own views based on the text provided.

## Formal letter writing

Students learn to write formal letters following a specific structure, including the sender's address, date, receiver's address, subject line, salutation, body, closing, and signature. They must use polite language and correct abbreviations like 'Mr.' or 'Dr.', and include all necessary details like addresses and dates clearly. For example, a letter to the principal requesting a new library book should begin with a formal greeting and end with 'Yours faithfully' if the receiver's name is unknown. The body of the letter should state the purpose clearly and provide all relevant information in short, simple paragraphs. Proper formatting and respectful tone are essential to ensure the letter is understood and taken seriously.

## Analytical paragraph writing

In Class 10, you learn to write analytical paragraphs by clearly stating a central idea in the topic sentence, supporting it with specific evidence from the text, and explaining how that evidence proves the point. You must ensure the paragraph has a logical flow with connecting words like 'however', 'furthermore', or 'as a result'. For example, when analysing a character, you could write, "Karna’s loyalty to Duryodhana shows his strong devotion, as he chose to fight against his own brothers despite knowing the truth." Finally, you need to conclude the paragraph by summarising your analysis without introducing new information.

## Grammar: gap filling and error correction

In Grammar, you learn to fill in the blanks in sentences by choosing the correct words, like using 'a', 'an', or 'the' properly. You also correct mistakes in sentences, such as fixing subject-verb agreement, like changing "The dog are barking" to "The dog is barking". Another rule is using tenses correctly, for example, changing "She goes to school yesterday" to "She went to school yesterday". You practice identifying and fixing errors in word order and prepositions, like saying "She is fond on chocolates" instead of "She is fond of chocolates". These exercises help you write and speak accurately.

## Reported speech and voice transformations

Students learn to change direct speech into reported speech by adjusting pronouns, tenses, and time expressions. When converting from direct to reported speech, the verb tense usually shifts one step backward, for example, 'is' becomes 'was' and 'will' becomes 'would'. Questions become statements with words like 'if' or 'whether', and exclamations turn into reported statements. For instance, He said, "I am happy" changes to He said that he was happy. Similarly, She asked, "Are you coming?" becomes She asked if I was coming. Mastering these transformations helps in accurately conveying conversations and narratives.

## Prose: extract-based questions

In this topic, you learn how to answer questions about a prose extract by reading the text carefully and finding answers directly from it. You must use your own words to write short answers and support them with small examples from the passage, like quoting a phrase or sentence. Your answers should be clear and to the point, focusing on what the text says about a character, place, or event. For example, if the question asks about a character’s feelings, you will find sentences that describe their mood and write them in your answer. Practising this helps you understand stories better and write good answers in exams.

## Poetry: devices and central idea

In Class 10, you learn to identify and use poetic devices like simile, metaphor, and personification to understand how poets create meaning. You explore the structure of poems, such as stanzas and rhyme schemes, to see how they shape the poem’s flow and sound. The central idea is the main message or theme that the poem communicates, and you practice finding it by connecting the devices and images used by the poet. For example, in the poem "The Frog and the Nightingale," you see how the poet uses personification to tell a story and share a lesson about greed. By analysing these elements, you learn how to appreciate the deeper meaning behind the words.

## Supplementary reader questions

Students learn to read and understand supplementary readers by answering questions that check their comprehension of the story. They practise identifying the main characters, setting, and important events in the text. Questions also ask them to find specific details and understand the meanings of new words from the story. For example, they might be asked to describe the main character's feelings or explain what happened in a particular chapter. Students also learn to answer questions in complete sentences using their own words.

## Register and vocabulary

Students learn to identify and use different registers, such as formal, informal, and technical, for various situations and audiences. They understand that vocabulary choices depend on the context, like using ‘arrive’ instead of ‘come’ in a formal letter or ‘mobile’ instead of ‘cell phone’ in casual speech. The topic explains how to select precise and appropriate words to express ideas clearly, such as using ‘delighted’ instead of ‘happy’ to show stronger feeling. Students also practice analysing texts to recognise the register used, like identifying formal language in a news report versus informal words in a conversation. This helps them communicate effectively in different settings, from writing essays to talking to friends.

## Examination strategy

Students learn to carefully read and understand all the instructions before answering any examination questions. They are taught to manage their time wisely by spending more time on questions that carry more marks and leaving difficult ones for later. A key strategy is to plan answers before writing, especially for long-form questions, by making brief notes or a mind map to include all important points. For example, in a comprehension passage, they should underline the main ideas to answer questions accurately. Finally, students must always check their answers for spelling mistakes and ensure all parts of the question have been addressed before submitting.
