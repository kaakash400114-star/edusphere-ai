# Matriculation Class 5 English

## Abstract and material nouns

In Class 5, children learn to identify and differentiate between abstract nouns and material nouns. Abstract nouns are names of ideas, feelings, qualities, or states that cannot be seen or touched, like happiness, courage, or kindness. Material nouns, on the other hand, are names of things that can be seen or touched, such as gold, water, or cotton. The lesson teaches children to spot these nouns in sentences and understand that abstract nouns represent non-physical things, while material nouns represent physical ones. For example, "love" is an abstract noun, and "milk" is a material noun. This helps them build a strong foundation for using and understanding different types of words in English.

## Relative pronouns

Children learn to use relative pronouns like who, which, and that to join two simple sentences and form one complex sentence. For example, they change "The girl is my friend" and "She lives next door" into "The girl who lives next door is my friend". Relative pronouns help add more details about the person or thing already mentioned in the sentence. They understand that 'who' is used for people, 'which' for animals or things, and 'that' can be used for both. This makes their sentences longer and more interesting when they describe things or people clearly.

## Perfect tenses (has/have done)

Children learn to use the present perfect tense, formed with has or have, to talk about actions that are completed but have importance now. We add has or have before the past participle of the verb, like "has eaten" or "have played." They understand that this tense connects past actions to the present time, for example, "She has finished her homework." We also use it to ask questions such as "Have you seen my book?" This helps children describe experiences and results that happened before now but matter today.

## Modals: can-may-must

Children learn that modals are special helping words like ‘can’, ‘may’, and ‘must’ that come before main verbs to express ability, permission, or necessity. We use ‘can’ to show what someone is able to do, for example, “Rani can swim well.” We use ‘may’ to ask for or give permission, such as “May I go to the library?” We use ‘must’ to show something is necessary, like “We must finish our homework.” These modals do not change with the number or person of the subject and are always followed by the main verb in its base form.

## Active-passive intro

In Class 5, we learn about changing sentences from active to passive voice. In the active voice, the subject does the action, like "The cat chases the mouse." In the passive voice, the subject receives the action, like "The mouse is chased by the cat." We remember to use 'is' or 'are' before the main verb and 'by' before the subject in passive sentences. We practice by asking, "Who is doing the action?" to find the subject. For example, "Riya reads a book" becomes "A book is read by Riya." This helps us understand how sentences can be written differently.

## Reported statements

In Grade 5, we learn about turning someone's words into reported statements. We change the words from the first person to the third person and change the tense of the verb. For example, if Riya says, "I am happy," we report it as Riya said that she was happy. We also change the time words like 'today' to 'that day'. The word 'that' is often used to join the reporting verb and the reported statement. We practice changing sentences like, "They are playing football," to "He said that they were playing football".

## Punctuation and paragraphs

In Class 5, children learn to use full stops, question marks, and exclamation marks correctly to end sentences. They also study commas to separate words in a list, like in “I bought apples, bananas, and oranges.” Paragraphs are taught as groups of related sentences, each starting with a small indent or space. Students practice writing short paragraphs about familiar topics, such as their school or a hobby, to show how ideas are organised together. They understand that every new paragraph introduces a new part of the story or idea.

## Fact vs opinion reading

Children learn to identify the difference between facts and opinions by checking if information is true or false. A fact is something that can be proven true, like "The sky is blue," while an opinion is what someone thinks or feels, such as "Chocolate ice cream is the best." They practice by reading simple sentences and sorting them into two groups: facts and opinions. For example, "There are seven days in a week" is a fact, but "Monday is the best day" is an opinion. This helps them understand what is real and what is someone's idea.

## Formal letter

Children learn to write formal letters using a proper structure with the sender’s address, date, receiver’s address, subject, salutation like ‘Respected Sir/Madam’, the main message in clear sentences, and a closing such as ‘Yours faithfully’ followed by their name. They practice writing letters to different people, like the principal of their school or the head of a library, to request information or ask for permission. The main rules include writing in a polite and respectful tone, using correct spelling and punctuation, and organising information in a neat, orderly manner. Children also learn that the subject line should be short and tell the main purpose of the letter clearly. For example, they might write a letter to request new books for the school library.

## Story completion

Children learn to write the ending of a story by using details from the beginning and middle. They must make a new ending that connects with what happened before. For example, if a story is about a lost dog finding its way home, the ending should show the dog reaching home happily. They also learn to add a small lesson or moral at the end, like "always listen to your elders". The ending should be short, clear, and make sense so the story feels complete.
