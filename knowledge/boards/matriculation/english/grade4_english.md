# Matriculation Class 4 English

## Countable-uncountable nouns

In Class 4, we learn about countable and uncountable nouns. Countable nouns are things we can count, like one mango, two bananas, or three pencils. Uncountable nouns are things we cannot count, like sugar, water, or rice because they come in amounts, not separate pieces. We use ‘a’ or ‘an’ with countable nouns, such as ‘a book’, but we do not use them with uncountable nouns. For example, we say ‘a glass of water’ because water is uncountable, but we count the glass. We also learn to use words like ‘many’ for countable nouns (many sweets) and ‘much’ for uncountable nouns (much milk).

## Reflexive pronouns

Reflexive pronouns show that the action of the verb comes back to the subject of the sentence. We use them when the subject and the object of the verb are the same person or thing. The common reflexive pronouns are myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, and themselves. For example, in the sentence "Ravi made a card himself," the action of making the card returns to Ravi. We also use reflexive pronouns to add emphasis, as in "The children solved the puzzle themselves.

## Adjective degrees

Children learn to compare things using three forms of adjectives: positive, comparative, and superlative. The positive form describes one thing, like "This book is big." The comparative form compares two things by adding "-er" or using "more," such as "This book is bigger than that one." The superlative form shows the highest degree among many things by adding "-est" or using "most," like "This is the biggest book in the class." We use these forms to talk about size, age, and other qualities. For example, "An elephant is bigger than a dog, but the blue whale is the biggest animal.

## Present and past continuous

In class 4, we learn about present continuous tense to describe actions happening now. We form it with am/is/are + verb-ing, like "I am playing" or "She is reading". Past continuous tense tells about actions that were happening in the past. We make it with was/were + verb-ing, like "They were swimming" or "He was eating". We use these tenses to show that actions are ongoing, and we remember to add "-ing" to the main verb. This helps us talk about things happening at this moment or before.

## Will and going to

In Class 4, children learn to use 'will' and 'going to' to talk about the future. They use 'will' for spontaneous decisions, like "I will help you" when seeing a friend fall. They use 'going to' for plans, like "We are going to play cricket" after deciding with friends. They also use 'going to' for predictions based on present evidence, such as "Look at the dark clouds; it is going to rain." The simple structure is subject + will + base verb for 'will', and subject + am/is/are + going to + base verb for 'going to'. These rules help them share their future ideas clearly.

## Adverb kinds

In Class 4, children learn about adverbs and how they describe actions by telling us how, when, or where something happens. They learn to find words like "quickly" (how), "yesterday" (when), and "here" (where) in sentences and use them to make their writing more interesting. The teacher shows that adverbs often end in "-ly", such as "slowly" or "happily", and helps them practice using these words to describe verbs. For example, in the sentence "The bird sang sweetly", the word "sweetly" is an adverb that tells how the bird sang. By the end of the lesson, children can identify and use adverbs to improve their sentences.

## Conjunctions: and-but-because

In this topic, children learn to use simple conjunctions like 'and', 'but', and 'because' to connect words and short sentences. They understand that 'and' joins similar things, for example, "Ramesh has a ball and bat." The word 'but' is used to show difference, as in "She is small but strong." Lastly, they learn to use 'because' to give a reason, like "The plants are green because they have water." These words help make sentences longer and more meaningful in everyday talking and writing.

## Question-exclamation punctuation

In Class 4, we learn to use question marks (?) and exclamation marks (!) correctly at the end of sentences. A question mark comes after a sentence that asks something, like "Where are you going?" An exclamation mark shows strong feeling or a sudden idea, such as "Look at the big elephant!" We also see that exclamations can start with "What" or "How", like "What a beautiful day!" or "How fast she runs!" We practice spotting these sentences and putting the right mark to show if we are asking or feeling something strong. This helps us read and write better.

## Comprehension skills

In Class 4, children learn to find the main idea and supporting details in short passages, like identifying that a story is about a helping friend from key sentences. They practice answering questions using information directly from the text, such as "What did the cat do?" by locating the answer in the given paragraph. Students also develop skills to sequence events in the correct order, like arranging steps shown in a story about planting a seed. They begin to make simple inferences, for example, guessing a character’s feelings from their actions or words in the story. Additionally, children learn to find meanings of unfamiliar words using clues from the surrounding text.

## Letter and notice writing

In Class 4, students learn to write clear and simple letters and notices using proper formats. A letter has a date, address of the sender and receiver, subject line, salutation like "Dear Sir", body with main points, and closing like "Yours sincerely". Notices must have a bold heading, date, short message with important details, and a signature. For example, a notice about a sports day would say "Sports Day: 15 November 2023, Wear your house t-shirt". They practice writing friendly letters to family members and formal notices for school events.
