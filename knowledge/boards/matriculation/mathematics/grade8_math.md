# Matriculation Class 8 Mathematics

## Rational numbers

Rational numbers are numbers that can be written as a fraction where the numerator and denominator are both integers, and the denominator is not zero. You learn to find equivalent fractions by multiplying or dividing both the numerator and denominator by the same number. Operations like addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division follow specific rules: for addition, you find a common denominator, and for multiplication, you multiply the numerators and denominators directly. For example, you can add 1/2 and 1/4 by converting them to 2/4 and 1/4 to get the answer 3/4. These numbers are used to represent quantities that are not whole numbers, like 1.5, which is the same as 3/2.

## Linear equations

In Class 8, students learn to solve linear equations in one variable, which are equations like 2x + 5 = 15. They follow rules to find the value of the unknown letter, such as adding, subtracting, multiplying, or dividing both sides by the same number to keep the equation balanced. For example, in 3x - 4 = 8, we add 4 to both sides to get 3x = 12, then divide by 3 to find x = 4. They also learn to translate word problems into simple equations, like "a number plus 7 is 20," and write it as x + 7 = 20. This helps them understand how equations represent real-life situations.

## Quadrilaterals

In Class 8, you learn about quadrilaterals, which are shapes with four sides. You will study their different types like squares, rectangles, rhombuses, and parallelograms, and learn their special properties, such as opposite sides being equal and parallel. You also learn to find the area of these shapes, using formulas like length × breadth for rectangles and side × side for squares. For example, a square with a side of 4 cm has an area of 16 square centimetres. Understanding these rules helps you recognise and solve problems with these common shapes.

## Data handling-probability intro

In this topic, students learn about chance and probability using simple words like 'sure', 'impossible', 'likely', and 'unlikely'. They understand that probability tells us how likely something is to happen, using examples like tossing a coin or rolling a die. For example, getting a head when tossing a coin is equally likely as getting a tail, each having a probability of 1 out of 2. They also learn to describe events using fractions and simple percentages, such as a 50% chance for a coin toss outcome. By comparing events like picking a red marble from a bag, they practice ordering outcomes from most likely to least likely.

## Squares-roots

In Class 8, you learn about square roots as the number that, when multiplied by itself, gives the original number. For example, the square root of 16 is 4, because 4 × 4 = 16. You also find square roots of perfect squares like 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 and so on. The symbol for square root is √, so √25 = 5 because 5 × 5 = 25. You practice finding square roots of small numbers and see how they relate to squares, helping you understand the relationship between multiplication and reverse operations.

## Cubes-roots

Cubes and cube roots are numbers that multiply by themselves three times. A cube root is the opposite of a cube; it helps us find the original number that was multiplied. For example, the cube of 2 is 8 because 2 × 2 × 2 = 8, so the cube root of 8 is 2. We use the symbol ∛ to show cube roots, like writing ∛27 = 3 because 3 × 3 × 3 = 27. Learning this helps us solve problems where we need to find equal sides of a cube or split things into equal groups.

## Comparing quantities-interest

In this topic, children learn to find simple interest by using the formula I = (P × R × T)/100, where P is the principal amount, R is the rate of interest per year, and T is the time in years. They solve problems to calculate how much extra money one earns or pays as interest, such as finding the interest on ₹5000 at 5% for 3 years. Pupils also understand that the principal amount is the starting money, the rate is how fast the money grows, and the time is for how long the money is borrowed or saved. They practice comparing different interest amounts to find the better deal, like choosing between two banks with different interest rates.

## Algebraic expressions-identities

In this topic, children learn to expand algebraic expressions using identities like (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² and (a – b)² = a² – 2ab + b². They practice multiplying two binomials, such as (x + 3)(x + 4), by applying these formulas step by step. For example, (2y + 5)² is expanded to 4y² + 20y + 25 using the first identity. They also simplify expressions by combining like terms, like turning 3x² + 2x + 5x² – x into 8x² + x. These rules help them solve problems faster without multiplying long numbers directly.

## Mensuration

In Mensuration, you learn to find the area and perimeter of simple 2D shapes like squares, rectangles, and circles. The perimeter is the total length around a shape, found by adding all its sides, while the area is the space inside it. For a square, you multiply its side by itself, and for a rectangle, you multiply length by breadth. Circles use the formula for area (π × radius × radius) and circumference (2 × π × radius). You also calculate the volume of 3D shapes like cubes and cuboids by multiplying length, breadth, and height. For example, a cube with sides 3 cm each has a volume of 27 cubic centimetres. These formulas help solve real-life problems, like finding the fence needed for a garden or paint required for a wall.

## Exponents-power

In Class 8, we learn about exponents, which are small numbers written above and to the right of another number to show how many times it is multiplied by itself, like 2³ which means 2 × 2 × 2. We study important rules such as multiplying powers with the same base, like 3² × 3⁴ = 3⁽²⁺⁴⁾ = 3⁶, and dividing them, like 5⁵ ÷ 5² = 5⁽⁵⁻²⁾ = 5³. We also learn how to find the value of a number raised to the power of zero, which is always one, for example, 10⁰ = 1. These rules help us solve big calculations easily by making numbers simpler and easier to work with.

## Direct-inverse proportion

In Class 8, we learn about direct and inverse proportion, which describe how two things change together. In direct proportion, when one quantity increases, the other increases at the same rate, like the number of bananas and their total cost. In inverse proportion, when one quantity increases, the other decreases, such as the number of workers and the time taken to finish a job. We use simple formulas to solve these problems: for direct proportion, \( \frac{x_1}{y_1} = \frac{x_2}{y_2} \), and for inverse proportion, \( x_1 \times y_1 = x_2 \times y_2 \). These concepts help us understand real-life situations, like sharing sweets or completing tasks.

## Factorisation-graphs

Children learn to factorise algebraic expressions by taking out common factors, like writing 3x + 6 as 3(x + 2). They also study factorisation using identities, such as a² - b² = (a + b)(a - b), for example, x² - 9 = (x + 3)(x - 3). The topic connects factorisation to graphs, where they learn that if f(a) = 0, then (x - a) is a factor of the polynomial f(x). They plot simple quadratic graphs like y = x² and see how they are U-shaped curves. By solving equations like x² - 4 = 0, they find roots and see where the graph crosses the x-axis.
