# Matriculation Class 3 Mathematics

## Numbers to 10,000

Children learn to read, write, and compare numbers up to 10,000 using place value. They understand that a number like 5,432 is made up of 5 thousands, 4 hundreds, 3 tens, and 2 ones. They use the symbols > (greater than), < (less than), and = (equal to) to compare numbers, such as 4,500 > 4,058. Children also learn to form numbers using expanded notation, like 7,000 + 300 + 20 + 5 for 7,325. They practice arranging numbers in order from smallest to largest or largest to smallest.

## Four operations

In Class 3, children learn the four operations: addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. They solve simple problems using facts and properties, like 2 + 3 = 5 or 10 – 4 = 6. They understand multiplication as repeated addition, such as 3 × 4 is 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12. For division, they use grouping or sharing, like dividing 12 sweets into 4 groups gives 3 sweets each. They solve word problems using these operations, applying concepts of carry-over in addition and borrowing in subtraction.

## Fractions: half-quarter-third

In Class 3, children learn to identify and write fractions like half (1/2), quarter (1/4), and third (1/3). They understand that fractions show parts of a whole, such as cutting a chocolate bar into equal pieces and taking one part. For example, half means dividing into two equal parts, quarter into four equal parts, and third into three equal parts. They practise shading these fractions in shapes like circles and rectangles to see the parts clearly. The children also learn to write the fraction using numbers, where the top number (numerator) shows the part taken and the bottom number (denominator) shows the total equal parts.

## Money: bills and change

In Grade 3, children learn to identify and count Indian currency notes and coins, understanding their values and how they are used for buying things. They practice counting money by grouping coins of the same value, such as 10-rupee coins, to find the total amount. Students are taught to make amounts using different combinations of notes and coins, like using one 50-rupee note and two 20-rupee coins to make 90 rupees. They also learn to calculate simple change, for example, if you pay 100 rupees for a 75-rupee book, you get back 25 rupees. The focus is on understanding money as a tool for exchange and solving basic real-life problems involving transactions.

## Measurement with conversions

In this unit, children learn to measure length, weight, and capacity using standard units like centimetres, metres, grams, kilograms, litres, and millilitres. They compare and order objects based on these measurements, such as finding the longest ribbon or the heaviest book. They are introduced to simple conversions, like knowing that 100 centimetres make 1 metre and 1000 millilitres make 1 litre, using practical examples. For instance, they measure a pencil in centimetres and a bottle of water in millilitres or litres. The focus is on understanding the relationship between different units through hands-on activities and real-life objects.

## Time and calendar

Children learn to read time on clocks with only the hour and minute hands. They understand that the short hand points to the hour and the long hand points to the minutes. For example, when the short hand is on 8 and the long hand is on 12, it is 8 o'clock. They also learn about the days of the week and the months of the year in their proper order. Children are taught that there are 7 days in a week and 12 months in a year. They learn to name and sequence these days and months correctly.

## Geometry: lines and shapes

In Grade 3, we learn about different kinds of lines like straight, curved, and slanting lines. We also recognise and name basic two-dimensional shapes such as circle, square, rectangle, and triangle, counting their sides and corners. We understand that a square has four equal sides and four corners, while a rectangle has two long sides and two short sides with four corners. We learn to identify these shapes in objects around us, like a wheel is a circle and a book is a rectangle. This helps us see how geometry is part of our daily life.

## Patterns and pictographs

Children learn to identify and extend simple repeating patterns using shapes, colours, objects, and numbers. They observe patterns like red-blue-red-blue or triangle-circle-triangle-circle and predict the next element in the sequence. Students also learn to read and interpret simple pictographs, where pictures represent data, such as counting different types of fruits. They learn to draw one-to-one correspondences between pictures and actual objects to count and compare quantities. For example, if three apples and two bananas are shown in a pictograph, children understand it means there are more apples than bananas.
