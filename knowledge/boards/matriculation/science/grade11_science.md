# Matriculation Class 11 Science

## Physics: units-vectors-motion-laws-work-gravitation

Students learn about the International System of Units (SI) and how to measure quantities like length and mass using standard units like metre and kilogram. They study vectors as quantities having both magnitude and direction, and calculate their components to understand motion in two dimensions. The laws of motion explain how force causes acceleration, with examples like a car speeding up or a ball rolling down a hill. The concept of work is introduced as force multiplied by displacement, and students apply the formula W = F × d, such as calculating work done when lifting a heavy box. Finally, they explore gravitation, learning Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation and how it explains why objects fall towards Earth and the motion of planets.

## Physics: matter-heat-waves

In Class 11 Physics, students learn that heat is a form of energy that flows from a hotter body to a colder one. They study three modes of heat transfer: conduction, like a metal spoon getting hot in a pot of soup; convection, like warm air rising near a heater; and radiation, like heat from the Sun reaching Earth. The concept of thermal expansion explains why metal bridges have gaps and why a glass lid can get stuck on a hot jar. They also learn about the relationship between heat and work, and how different substances require different amounts of heat to change their temperature, measured by specific heat capacity.

## Chemistry: mole-atom-periodicity

In this topic, students learn about the mole concept as a counting unit for atoms and molecules, understanding that one mole contains Avogadro’s number (6.022 × 10²³) of particles. They study how to calculate the number of moles using the formula *n = m/M*, where *m* is mass in grams and *M* is molar mass, and convert between mass, moles, and number of particles. The periodic table is examined to understand periodic trends like atomic radius, ionization energy, and electronegativity, which change across a period and down a group due to electron configuration and nuclear charge. For example, sodium (Na) has a lower ionization energy than chlorine (Cl) in the third period because chlorine’s nucleus attracts electrons more strongly. Students also link these trends to chemical behaviour, like why fluorine is the most reactive non-metal.

## Chemistry: bonding-states-equilibrium-redox

Students learn about chemical bonding through ionic and covalent bonds, understanding how atoms transfer or share electrons to form stable compounds like sodium chloride and water. They examine states of matter, including the arrangement of particles in solids, liquids, and gases, and study phase changes like melting and evaporation. Chemical equilibrium is introduced as a dynamic state where the rate of forward and reverse reactions is equal, as seen in the reaction between nitrogen and hydrogen to form ammonia. Redox reactions, involving transfer of electrons, are explained through examples such as rusting of iron and the reaction between zinc and copper sulphate solution.

## Chemistry: organic basics-hydrocarbons

In Class 11 Chemistry, students learn about hydrocarbons, which are compounds made only of carbon and hydrogen atoms. They study different types like alkanes (e.g., methane, CH₄, with single bonds), alkenes (e.g., ethene, C₂H₄, with a double bond), and alkynes (e.g., ethyne, C₂H₂, with a triple bond). Students also understand structural formulas and isomerism, where compounds have the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms, like butane and isobutane. These concepts help in understanding how carbon forms millions of compounds by bonding in various ways.

## Biology: diversity

In Biology, students learn about the diversity of living organisms, including plants, animals, and microorganisms. They study the classification system, which groups organisms based on shared characteristics like body structure and modes of nutrition. For example, plants are divided into flowering and non-flowering types, while animals are classified as vertebrates (with backbone) and invertebrates (without backbone). Students also explore the five kingdoms of life—Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, and Animalia—and learn about the unique features of each kingdom, such as bacteria in Monera being unicellular and fungi like mushrooms being decomposers. This understanding helps in appreciating the wide variety of life on Earth.

## Biology: plant structure-physiology

Students learn about the anatomy and functions of flowering plants, focusing on root systems for water and mineral absorption and shoot systems for photosynthesis. They study the structure of the leaf, including the epidermis, palisade parenchyma, and spongy mesophyll, and its role in gas exchange and transpiration. The transport of water and food in plants is explained through xylem and phloem tissues, using processes like transpiration pull and translocation. Key life processes such as photosynthesis and respiration are examined with their chemical equations, emphasising the role of chlorophyll and sunlight. They also learn about plant hormones like auxin and gibberellin and their functions in growth and development.

## Biology: human physiology

Students learn about the structure and function of human organs and organ systems, focusing on how they work together to maintain life. They study the digestive system, understanding how food is broken down and absorbed for energy, with the small intestine being a key site. The respiratory system is explored to see how oxygen is taken in and carbon dioxide is expelled through the process of breathing. The circulatory system explains how the heart pumps blood through blood vessels to carry oxygen and nutrients to all body cells. For example, students learn that the kidneys filter waste products from the blood to form urine.
