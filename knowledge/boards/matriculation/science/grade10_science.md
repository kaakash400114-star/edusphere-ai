# Matriculation Class 10 Science

## Chemical reactions and equations

Students learn that a chemical reaction happens when substances change to form new materials, like when rust forms on iron or baking soda fizzes with vinegar. They study how to write word equations and balance chemical equations by making sure the number of atoms for each element is the same on both sides, such as in the reaction between hydrogen and oxygen to form water. They classify reactions as combination, decomposition, displacement, and double displacement based on how reactants join or split, like quicklime reacting with water to form slaked lime. Students also identify physical changes, like ice melting, where no new substance is created.

## Acids, bases and salts

Students learn about acids, bases, and salts, and their properties. Acids like lemon juice and vinegar taste sour and turn blue litmus paper red. Bases, such as soap and baking soda, feel slippery and turn red litmus paper blue. Neutralisation happens when an acid and a base react to form salt and water, for example, hydrochloric acid reacts with sodium hydroxide to form sodium chloride (table salt) and water. Students also learn about the pH scale, which measures how acidic or basic a substance is. Salts are formed when acids react with metals, carbonates, or bases, like common salt (NaCl) from sodium and chlorine.

## Metals and non-metals

Metals are shiny, malleable, and good conductors of heat and electricity, like iron and copper. Non-metals are dull, brittle, and poor conductors, such as sulphur and phosphorus. We learn about the formation of ionic compounds when metals lose electrons to non-metals, creating stable structures like sodium chloride (table salt). Metals react with oxygen to form metal oxides, which are basic, while non-metals form acidic oxides. The reactivity series helps us predict how vigorously metals will displace hydrogen from acids, with zinc being more reactive than copper.

## Carbon and its compounds

Students learn that carbon is a special element because it forms many compounds, including fuels like coal and food like sugar. Carbon has four valence electrons, so it forms four covalent bonds, allowing it to make long chains and rings, creating molecules like methane (CH₄) and ethanol (C₂H₅OH). They study hydrocarbons, which are compounds of carbon and hydrogen, grouped into alkanes (like propane, C₃H₈) and alkenes (like ethene, C₂H₄). The topic also covers functional groups, such as the –OH group in alcohols, which give compounds their unique properties. Students identify these structures and understand how carbon’s bonding ability makes it the basis of life and countless useful substances.

## Periodic classification

In this topic, students learn about the periodic classification of elements, which organises them based on their properties. They study how Mendeleev arranged elements in order of increasing atomic mass and left gaps for undiscovered elements, like the prediction for eka-aluminium which later became gallium. The modern periodic law states that the properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic numbers. Students also identify groups and periods in the periodic table, and learn that elements in the same group have similar chemical properties, such as all alkali metals in group 1 reacting vigorously with water.

## Life processes

Life processes are the basic activities that keep living things alive. Living organisms perform functions like nutrition, respiration, transportation, and excretion to survive. For example, plants make their own food through photosynthesis using sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide, while animals eat food for nutrition. Respiration happens in all cells to release energy by breaking down food, and excretion removes waste products like carbon dioxide and urea from the body. These processes help maintain life and growth in all living beings.

## Control and coordination

Students learn how our body controls and coordinates actions with the help of the nervous system and hormones. The brain is the main control centre that sends messages through nerves to all parts of the body like muscles and glands. Reflex actions are quick, automatic responses, like pulling your hand away from a hot object, which happen without thinking. The spinal cord also helps in reflex actions by sending signals back and forth from the body to the brain. Hormones, which are chemical messengers from glands like the pancreas, help in slow and long-term control, such as regulating growth and blood sugar levels.

## How do organisms reproduce

Organisms reproduce to produce young ones like themselves. Plants reproduce by seeds, like mango seeds growing into mango trees, and also by parts like stems in rose plants. Animals reproduce by giving birth to live young ones, such as dogs and cats, or by laying eggs, like hens and frogs. Some simple organisms like yeast and hydra reproduce by splitting into two, a process called budding or fission. This helps in the continuation of life for different species.

## Heredity and evolution

In the topic of Heredity and Evolution, students learn that heredity is the process by which parents pass on traits to their children through genes found on chromosomes. They study Mendel’s laws of inheritance, such as the Law of Dominance, which explains how a dominant allele masks a recessive one in characteristics like pea plant flower colour. Evolution is explored through the idea of variation in populations, where environmental factors like camouflage help some organisms survive and reproduce better, leading to changes over generations. Examples include how bacteria can develop resistance to antibiotics and the formation of new species through adaptation. Students also learn about fossils as evidence of how living things have changed over time.

## Light: reflection and refraction

Light travels in straight lines and bounces back when it hits a shiny surface, like a mirror. This is called reflection, where the angle of incidence equals the angle of refraction. When light passes from one medium, like air, to another, like water, it bends; this is refraction, which makes a straw look bent in a glass of water. We use a formula, n = sin i / sin r, to find how much light bends, where n is the refractive index. Lenses use refraction to form images; a convex lens brings light together to make a real image, like in a camera. Mirrors form images based on reflection; a plane mirror gives an erect virtual image.

## The human eye and the colourful world

The human eye is a special organ that helps us see by allowing light to enter and forming an image on the retina. It has important parts like the cornea, lens, and iris that work together to control how much light comes in and focuses the picture clearly. We learn about a condition called myopia, or short-sightedness, where a person can see nearby objects but distant ones look blurry, and how spectacles with concave lenses can help. Similarly, hypermetropia, or long-sightedness, is when distant objects are seen clearly but close ones are blurry, and convex lenses in spectacles are used to correct this. We also study the colourful world, understanding how a prism splits white light into seven colours, like a rainbow, and that different colours have different wavelengths, which is why they appear differently to our eyes.

## Electricity

Students learn about electric circuits and the flow of current, which is measured in amperes using an ammeter. They discover that voltage, measured in volts, pushes the current through a circuit, while resistance, measured in ohms, opposes it. Ohm’s Law, V = I × R, helps us calculate these values, like finding the voltage needed for a 2 A current through a 5 Ω resistor. They also understand series and parallel circuits, where the total resistance changes, and learn to calculate electrical power in watts using P = V × I, such as a 100 W bulb using more energy than a 40 W bulb.

## Magnetic effects of electric current

Students learn that an electric current creates a magnetic field around a wire, forming concentric circles. The direction of this field can be found using the right-hand thumb rule: if the thumb points in the current direction, the curled fingers show the field direction. When a wire is coiled into a solenoid, it behaves like a bar magnet with north and south poles. An electromagnet is made by wrapping insulated wire around a soft iron core, which becomes a strong temporary magnet. For example, an electric bell uses an electromagnet to attract a striker, making it ring.

## Our environment

Students learn that the environment is made up of living things called biotic factors, like plants and animals, and non-living things called abiotic factors, like soil, water, and air. They study the interdependence of these components through food chains and food webs, which show how energy from the sun flows from producers like grass to consumers like insects and birds. The chapter explains how our environment provides essential resources such as clean air, fresh water, and fertile soil, and how these can be affected by human activities like pollution and deforestation. They also learn about different ecosystems, such as forests and grasslands, and understand the importance of conserving them to maintain balance and protect biodiversity.

## Sustainable management of natural resources

Students learn that natural resources like water, soil, and minerals must be used wisely to meet our needs without harming the environment. They study the importance of sustainable practices, such as rainwater harvesting and drip irrigation, to conserve water for future generations. The concept of a balanced ecosystem is introduced, where living organisms and their environment depend on each other for survival. Children also learn about reducing waste by reusing materials like paper and plastic, and recycling them into new products. For example, they understand how planting trees helps prevent soil erosion and provides clean air.
