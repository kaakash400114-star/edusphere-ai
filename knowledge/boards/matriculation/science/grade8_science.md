# Matriculation Class 8 Science

## Crop production

Students learn that crop production involves growing plants for food and fibre, like wheat for roti and cotton for clothes. They discover that healthy crops need the right amount of sunlight, water, and good soil full of nutrients. Farmers prepare the land by ploughing and adding manure or fertiliser to help plants grow strong. Crops are protected from pests by using natural methods like neem oil or by growing plants like marigold that keep insects away. Different seasons are important; for example, rice grows well in the rainy season while wheat grows better in the winter.

## Microorganisms

Microorganisms are tiny living things that we cannot see with our naked eyes. They can be unicellular, like bacteria and protozoa, or multicellular, like some fungi. These organisms grow and multiply very quickly in different places, such as soil, water, and inside the bodies of plants and animals. Some microorganisms are helpful, like the ones that help make curd from milk, while others can cause diseases, like the flu or food spoilage. We can stop harmful microorganisms from spreading by keeping ourselves clean and eating fresh, cooked food.

## Metals-non-metals

Metals are shiny, hard materials that can bend, like iron and copper, while non-metals are dull and break easily, like sulphur and carbon. Metals can be made into wires because they are malleable and ductile, whereas non-metals are brittle and cannot be shaped like metals. We learn that metals lose electrons to form positive ions, but non-metals gain electrons to form negative ions, which helps them bond together. For example, sodium metal gives an electron to chlorine gas, making sodium chloride, which is common table salt. Most metals are good conductors of heat and electricity, but non-metals like wood and plastic are not.

## Coal-petroleum

Coal and petroleum are important natural resources that we get from the Earth. They are called fossil fuels because they were formed from the remains of plants and animals that lived millions of years ago. Coal is a black, solid fuel that is used to produce electricity in thermal power plants. Petroleum, also called crude oil, is a dark, liquid fuel that we use to make petrol, diesel, and for cooking as LPG. We must save these fuels because they are not renewable and will finish one day.

## Combustion-flame

Combustion is a chemical process where a substance burns rapidly with oxygen to produce heat and light, which we call a flame. A flame has three zones: the dark innermost zone of unburnt gases, the bright middle zone of complete combustion, and the outermost blue zone of hot gases. We need three things for combustion to happen: a fuel like wood or paper, air which has oxygen, and heat to start the burning process. Fire can be put out by removing any one of these three things, such as using water to cool the fuel or sand to block oxygen from reaching it.

## Conservation of plants-animals

Plants and animals are conserved to save them from disappearing forever. We protect forests like the Western Ghats which are homes to elephants and tigers. National parks such as Jim Corbett give safe spaces for animals to live freely in their natural habitat. We also grow new trees in a process called afforestation to make more homes for birds and insects. Saving the one-horned rhinoceros in Kaziranga National Park is an important example of animal conservation. These efforts help keep nature balanced and ensure future generations can see these wonderful creatures.

## Cell-structure

Every living thing is made of tiny building blocks called cells. A cell has three main parts: the cell wall is a strong outer cover that gives the cell its shape, the cell membrane is a thin layer inside the wall that controls what goes in and out, and the cytoplasm is a jelly-like substance where small parts called organelles are found. Organelles are like tiny machines that do important jobs, such as the nucleus, which acts as the control centre of the cell. For example, a plant cell has a cell wall, but an animal cell does not.

## Reproduction in animals

Animals reproduce by making their own kind through different methods, like eggs and live birth. Male animals make tiny sperms and female animals make larger eggs, and when a sperm and an egg join together, a baby starts to grow inside the mother’s body. This process is called fertilisation and it happens in some animals like hens, which lay eggs, and in others like dogs, which give birth to young ones directly. Animals that lay eggs are called oviparous, and those which give birth to babies are called viviparous. The young ones grow up to become adults and repeat the same process to keep their species alive.

## Adolescence

Adolescence is the time when a child grows into a young adult, usually between 11 and 18 years of age. During this period, the body changes like height increase, growth of facial hair in boys and development of breasts in girls. The reproductive system also matures, with girls starting menstruation and boys experiencing sperm production. Adolescents experience strong emotions and mood swings because of hormonal changes in their bodies. These changes are completely natural and a normal part of becoming an adult.

## Force-pressure

Force is a push or pull on an object. Pressure is the amount of force acting on a unit area, calculated by dividing the force by the area. The same force creates more pressure on a smaller area and less pressure on a larger area. For example, a sharp knife cuts better because its thin blade applies the same force over a tiny area, creating high pressure. A school bag with wide straps is more comfortable because the weight spreads over a bigger area, reducing pressure on the shoulders.

## Friction

Friction is the force that stops objects from moving easily when they touch surfaces. It happens when two surfaces rub against each other, and it can slow down or stop a moving ball or a sliding book. We can change friction by using things like oil or grease, which makes surfaces smooth and reduces the force, so things move faster. Rough surfaces, like a carpet, create more friction than smooth surfaces, like a tiled floor. This force is useful for us when we walk or write, as it helps our shoes and pens grip the ground or paper.

## Sound

Sound is a type of energy that travels through the air, water or solids in the form of waves, just like ripples move across the surface of water. When an object vibrates or shakes very fast, it makes sound waves that reach our ears and allow us to hear things like a ringing bell or a barking dog. The loudness of a sound depends on how much energy is used to create it, so a whisper is soft but a drum beat is loud, and the pitch or how high or low a sound is, depends on how fast the object is vibrating. Sounds can also be reflected from hard surfaces, like when your voice echoes in a big empty room, or they can be absorbed by soft materials like pillows, which stop the sound from bouncing back.

## Chemical effects of current

When electricity passes through certain liquids like salt water or copper sulphate solution, it causes a chemical change; this is called the chemical effect of current. Substances that allow electricity to pass through them and undergo chemical decomposition are called electrolytes, while those that do not are non-electrolytes. During this process, metals may get deposited on the electrodes (like copper on the negative electrode) and gases may be released at the electrodes. This is used in electroplating, where a thin layer of a metal like silver is deposited on an object to make it look shiny and prevent rusting.

## Light-eyes

Light travels in a straight line until it hits something or changes direction, like a shadow behind a chair. A mirror reflects light; we see our face because light from our eyes bounces off the mirror into our eyes. A mirror is smooth and shiny, which makes it reflect light clearly, unlike a rough wall which scatters light and shows a blurry image. A periscope uses two mirrors to help us see around corners, like looking over a crowd in a school function. Convex mirrors make objects look smaller and help us see a wider area, like in big shops or on the roads.

## Natural phenomena

Students learn about natural phenomena like lightning and thunder, understanding that lightning is a huge spark of electricity caused by clouds rubbing against each other. They study how thunder is the loud sound made by the rapid heating and expansion of air from the lightning’s heat. The class also explores the water cycle, learning about evaporation, condensation, and precipitation, which are the processes that make rain. Another important concept is the formation of rainbows, which are colourful arcs seen in the sky when sunlight is bent by water droplets after it rains. Finally, students get introduced to earthquakes, learning that they are sudden shaking of the ground caused by movements deep inside the Earth.
