# Matriculation Class 12 Science

## Physics: electrostatics-current

In electrostatics, you learn about electric charges, their types (positive and negative), and how they interact through Coulomb's Law, which states that like charges repel and opposite charges attract. You study electric fields, which are the regions around a charge where a force is felt, and calculate their strength using the formula E = F/Q. Current electricity teaches how electric charges flow through conductors like copper wires, forming an electric current, and you learn about Ohm’s Law, which connects voltage (V), current (I), and resistance (R) as V = IR. You also explore how cells and batteries provide the voltage needed to make current flow in a circuit, for example, in a torch bulb lighting up when connected correctly.

## Physics: magnetism-EMI-AC

In Class 12 Physics, you learn about magnetism and how changing magnetic fields produce electricity, a phenomenon called electromagnetic induction (EMI). You study Faraday’s Law, which states that the induced electromotive force (EMF) in a coil is equal to the rate of change of magnetic flux through it, and use the formula ε = -N(dΦ/dt). You also explore Lenz’s Law, which determines the direction of the induced current, such that it opposes the change in flux causing it. This principle is used in devices like electric generators, which convert mechanical energy into electrical energy. Additionally, you study alternating current (AC), how it is generated using a rotating coil in a magnetic field, and its advantages over direct current (DC) for long-distance transmission.

## Physics: optics-modern physics

In modern physics, students learn about the photoelectric effect, where light behaves as particles called photons, and Einstein’s equation 𝐸 = ℎ𝑣 explains this phenomenon. They study atomic models, including Bohr’s model, which depicts electrons in fixed orbits around the nucleus, emitting or absorbing energy when jumping between levels. The nuclear physics section covers radioactivity, the three types of radiation (alpha, beta, gamma), and nuclear reactions like fission and fusion, with examples such as uranium fission in atomic reactors. Additionally, the dual nature of matter and radiation introduces de Broglie’s hypothesis that particles like electrons exhibit wave-like properties. Finally, students explore semiconductor devices, including diodes and transistors, and their applications in modern electronics.

## Chemistry: solutions-electrochemistry

In this topic, students learn about solutions, their types like colloids and suspensions, and how concentration is measured using molarity and molality. They study electrochemistry, understanding redox reactions, how electrolytes conduct electricity, and the difference between galvanic and electrolytic cells with examples like the Daniel cell. The rules for balancing redox reactions and the Nernst equation for electrode potential are also covered. Students identify the structure and function of different electrodes, including the calomel electrode and the salt bridge, and solve problems involving cell reactions and standard electrode potentials.

## Chemistry: kinetics-surface-d-f block

Students learn about the rate of chemical reactions and factors affecting it, like concentration, temperature, and catalysts, with rate laws and orders of reaction. The Arrhenius equation and concept of activation energy are studied to explain reaction speeds. Surface chemistry covers adsorption, catalysis, and colloids, including lyophilic and lyophobic types with examples like catalysts in Haber’s process. For d-block elements, students learn about variable oxidation states, colour in complexes, and catalytic properties, with examples of transition metals like iron and copper forming coloured compounds. Coordination compounds, including their nomenclature and bonding, are also explored with examples such as [Fe(CN)6]4–.

## Chemistry: organic families-biomolecules

Students study four main types of biomolecules: carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. Carbohydrates, like glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) and sucrose, provide energy and have general formula (CH₂O)n. Proteins, such as enzymes and haemoglobin, are made of amino acids linked by peptide bonds and have primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structures. Lipids, like oils and fats, are insoluble in water and store energy, while nucleic acids, DNA and RNA, store and transmit genetic information. They also learn about the functions of these biomolecules in living organisms.

## Biology: reproduction

Students learn about the different modes of reproduction in plants and animals, including sexual and asexual reproduction. In plants, they study processes like budding in yeast and fragmentation in Spirogyra, as well as the parts of a flower involved in sexual reproduction such as the stamen and pistil. In animals, they explore the male and female reproductive systems, learning about the formation of gametes like sperm and egg cells. The process of fertilisation, where male and female gametes fuse to form a zygote, is a key concept covered, followed by the stages of embryonic development.

## Biology: genetics-evolution

Students learn about genetics and evolution, studying how traits are inherited through genes and chromosomes following Mendel’s laws. They explore concepts like dominance, codominance, and sex-linked inheritance with examples such as pea plant traits and haemophilia in humans. The theory of evolution by natural selection is explained, highlighting variations, adaptations, and how species change over generations, as seen in the development of antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Students also examine the role of mutations in creating genetic diversity and driving evolution. The fossil record and comparative anatomy provide evidence for the common ancestry of organisms, reinforcing the process of evolution.

## Biology: biotechnology-ecology-health

Students study how biotechnology techniques like genetic engineering and recombinant DNA technology are used in medicine and agriculture, such as producing insulin for diabetes and developing pest-resistant crops. They learn about ecological concepts like food chains, food webs, and energy pyramids, and how human activities like pollution and deforestation affect ecosystems. The topic also covers human health topics, including the spread of infectious diseases, the importance of vaccines, and how the immune system defends the body against pathogens. Students explore the relationship between biotechnology and ecology, understanding how genetically modified organisms can impact natural environments and biodiversity.
