# Matriculation Class 9 Science

## Matter-pure substances

Pure substances are made of only one kind of particle and cannot be broken down into simpler substances by physical means. They exist as elements, which are basic substances like gold or oxygen, or as compounds, which are formed when two or more elements chemically combine, such as water (H₂O) or common salt (NaCl). Matter can be classified as pure or impure; pure substances have a fixed composition and properties, like the colour and melting point of ice. Impure substances, like saltwater or air, contain two or more different kinds of substances mixed together.

## Atoms-molecules

Atoms are the smallest building blocks of all matter, like tiny bricks that make up everything around us. Molecules are formed when two or more atoms of the same or different elements join together, just like two friends holding hands; for example, two hydrogen atoms bond with one oxygen atom to form water (H₂O). The number of atoms in a molecule is shown by a small number written below its symbol, such as in carbon dioxide (CO₂) where one carbon atom is bonded to two oxygen atoms. We learn that elements are substances made of only one type of atom, like pure gold (Au) or pure oxygen (O₂), and they cannot be broken down into simpler substances.

## Atom structure

Atoms are tiny particles that make up everything around us. Each atom has a centre called a nucleus, which contains positively charged protons and neutral neutrons. Negatively charged electrons move around the nucleus in fixed paths called shells. The first shell can hold only 2 electrons, while the second and third can hold up to 8 electrons, like the atoms of helium and neon which have full outer shells. The number of protons in the nucleus is called the atomic number and tells us what element the atom is, such as hydrogen with 1 proton or oxygen with 8 protons.

## Cell-fundamental unit

The cell is the smallest building block of all living things, like a tiny brick that makes up our body. It has a outer cover called the cell membrane that controls what goes in and out. Inside, a jelly-like substance called cytoplasm holds parts called organelles, such as the nucleus, which is the control centre of the cell. Plants also have a rigid cell wall outside the membrane for support, and many contain green structures called chloroplasts that help in making food. We can see cells under a microscope, and they can be of different shapes, like long nerve cells or round blood cells.

## Tissues

Tissues are groups of similar cells that work together to perform a specific function in the body. For example, muscle tissue helps in movement by contracting and relaxing, while nerve tissue carries messages between the brain and other parts of the body. Plants also have different tissues like parenchyma for storage and collenchyma for providing support. The main types of animal tissues are epithelial, connective, muscular, and nervous tissues, each with unique structures and jobs. These tissues combine to form organs like the heart or stomach, which work together to carry out life processes.

## Diversity of organisms

In this topic, students learn about the classification of living organisms into groups called kingdoms, such as plants, animals, and microorganisms. They study how organisms are divided based on features like their body structure, number of cells, and method of getting food. For example, plants are separated into trees, herbs, and shrubs, while animals are grouped as insects, birds, or mammals. Students also explore simple concepts like habitats, where different organisms live, such as fish in water and camels in deserts. This helps them understand the variety of life around us and how every organism has a special place in nature.

## Motion

Students learn about motion, which is when an object changes its position. We measure how fast something moves using speed, and we calculate speed by dividing the distance travelled by the time taken, like a car covering 60 kilometres in 1 hour. Motion can be fast or slow, and we describe its direction, such as a train moving towards the station. We also understand that forces like pushing or pulling are needed to make an object start or stop moving. For example, we need to push a ball to make it roll on the ground.

## Force-laws

Students learn about force-laws, starting with the idea that forces can push or pull objects. They discover that a force changes the state of motion of an object, like making a still ball roll or stopping a moving bicycle. The rule of forces is that when two forces act in opposite directions, their net force is the difference between them. For example, if a boy pushes a box with 10 units of force to the right and his friend pushes it with 5 units to the left, the box moves to the right with a net force of 5 units. Students also understand that a force needs contact, like a hand pushing a door, or can act at a distance, like the Earth pulling a ball down due to gravity.

## Gravitation

In class 9, you learn about gravitation, which is the force that pulls objects towards the Earth. This force depends on the mass of the objects and the distance between them; more mass means a stronger pull. The formula to find this force is called Newton's law of gravitation, and it helps us understand why things fall down. For example, it explains why a ball dropped from a height always falls towards the ground and not float away. We also discuss the acceleration due to gravity, which is how fast an object's speed increases as it falls, and it is nearly the same for all objects on Earth.

## Work-energy

In this topic, you learn that work is done when a force makes an object move. You also learn that energy is the capacity to do work, and energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another. You will study different types of energy, like kinetic energy (energy of moving objects) and potential energy (energy stored due to position). For example, when you lift a book, your muscles do work, and the book gains potential energy, which can change to kinetic energy if you drop it. The important formula you will use is Work = Force × Distance.

## Sound

Sound is a form of energy that is produced by vibrating objects, like a guitar string or a drum, and travels in the form of waves through a medium such as air, water, or solids. We hear sound when these waves reach our ears and make the eardrum vibrate; for example, a school bell makes sound by ringing, which we hear through the air. Sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids, and slowest in gases, and its loudness depends on how much the source of sound vibrates—a louder sound means more vibration, like a shouting person compared to a whispering one. The pitch of a sound is how high or low it is and depends on how fast the object vibrates: a thin guitar string vibrates fast and makes a high-pitched sound, while a thick one vibrates slowly and makes a low-pitched sound. Sound cannot travel through a vacuum because there are no particles to carry the sound waves, which is why astronauts cannot talk to each other in space without a special device.

## Illness causes

Students learn that illnesses are caused by tiny living things called microorganisms like bacteria and viruses, which enter our bodies and make us sick. Some diseases spread through the air when an infected person coughs or sneezes, while others spread through contaminated water or food, like getting an upset stomach from eating dirty fruits. Our body has natural defences, like skin and blood cells, that fight these germs to keep us healthy. Sometimes, illnesses can also be caused by not eating nutritious food, leading to weakness or deficiency diseases. Washing hands with soap and drinking clean water are simple ways to prevent many infections caused by these germs.

## Natural resources

Natural resources are materials and substances found in nature that are useful to humans. They are divided into two main types: renewable resources, like forests and water, which can be replaced quickly, and non-renewable resources, like coal and petrol, which cannot be replaced once used. We learn that overusing resources can harm the environment, so we must save them. For example, we should not waste water and should plant new trees to replace the ones we cut down. It is also important to reduce, reuse, and recycle things like paper and plastic to conserve our natural resources.

## Food improvement

Food improvement means making our food more healthy and tasty. We learn about the five main food groups: carbohydrates (like rice and chapatti), proteins (like dal and eggs), fats (like ghee), vitamins (from fruits and vegetables), and minerals (from milk and leafy vegetables). We understand that we need a balanced diet with all these groups to stay strong and fight diseases. We also learn simple methods to improve food, such as adding vegetables to dal to increase vitamins or mixing millets with rice to make it more nutritious. This helps us grow well and stay healthy every day.
