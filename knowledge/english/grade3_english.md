# Grade 3 English — Complete Curriculum

## 1. Vocabulary

### Context Clues

**Context clues** are hints that an author gives within a sentence or paragraph to help you figure out the meaning of an unknown word. There are several types:

**1. Definition Clue** — the author directly defines the word.
> The archaeologist, a scientist who studies ancient objects, carefully dug up the bones.
> (The words after the comma define "archaeologist.")

**2. Example Clue** — the author gives examples that help explain the word.
> The cafeteria serves many **savory** foods like fried chicken, grilled cheese, and pizza.
> (Fried chicken, grilled cheese, and pizza are all salty or flavorful, so "savory" means flavorful.)

**3. Synonym Clue** — a nearby word means the same thing.
> The puppy was very **frisky**, or playful, and ran around the yard.
> ("Playful" is a synonym for "frisky.")

**4. Antonym Clue** — a nearby word means the opposite.
> Unlike her **gregarious** sister who loved talking to everyone at the party, Maya sat quietly in the corner.
> (Maya is quiet and does not like socializing, so "gregarious" means the opposite — sociable and outgoing.)

**5. Explanation Clue** — the sentence explains the word.
> The weather was **balmy**; the warm breeze felt wonderful on my skin.
> ("Warm breeze felt wonderful" explains that "balmy" means pleasantly warm.)

### Dictionary Skills

A **dictionary** is a book (or online tool) that tells you what words mean and how to use them. Here is what you find in a dictionary entry:

> **brave** (brāv) *adjective* 1. Ready to face danger or pain; not afraid. 2. Having courage. *synonyms:* courageous, bold, fearless. *antonyms:* afraid, timid, cowardly.

| Part | What It Tells You |
|------|-------------------|
| **Guide words** | The first and last words on the page — help you find your word faster |
| **Entry word** | The word you are looking up (in bold) |
| **Pronunciation** | How to say the word, often with symbols |
| **Part of speech** | noun, verb, adjective, adverb, etc. |
| **Definition(s)** | The meaning(s) of the word — often numbered |
| **Example sentence** | Shows the word used in context |
| **Synonyms/Antonyms** | Words that mean the same or the opposite |

**How to use a dictionary:**
1. Think of the first letter of your word.
2. Open the dictionary to near that letter.
3. Use the **guide words** at the top of the page to narrow down where to look.
4. Alphabetize — if the first letter is the same, check the second, then third letter.
5. Read the definition that fits the way the word is used in your sentence.

An **idiom** is a phrase whose meaning is different from the literal meaning of the individual words. You cannot figure out what an idiom means just by reading each word.

| **Under the weather** | Standing below rain | Feeling sick |
| **Cost an arm and a leg** | Body parts as payment | Very expensive |

### Similes and Metaphors

Both similes and metaphors are **figures of speech** that compare two different things. They make writing more interesting and paint pictures in the reader's mind.

**Similes** compare two things using the words **like** or **as**:

| Simile | What It Compares |
|--------|-----------------|
| She runs **as fast as a cheetah**. | Her speed → cheetah's speed |
| His smile was **as bright as the sun**. | His smile → the sun |
| The room was **as messy as a pigsty**. | The room's condition → a pigsty |
| Her eyes sparkled **like stars**. | Her eyes → stars |
| The water was **as cold as ice**. | Water temperature → ice |
| He fought **like a lion**. | His bravery → a lion |
| The baby cried **as loud as a siren**. | The baby's cry → a siren |
| Her hair was **like silk**. | Her hair → silk |

**Metaphors** say one thing **IS** another (no "like" or "as"):

| Metaphor | Meaning |
|----------|---------|
| The classroom **was a zoo**. | The classroom was wild and chaotic. |
| Her words **were daggers**. | Her words were very hurtful. |
| He **is a shining star**. | He is outstanding and talented. |
| The snow **was a white blanket**. | The snow covered everything like a blanket. |
| My dad **is a rock**. | My dad is strong and dependable. |
| Her heart **is gold**. | She is very kind and generous. |
| Time **is money**. | Time is valuable and should not be wasted. |
| The test **was a monster**. | The test was very difficult and scary. |

## 2. Grammar

### Complete Subjects and Predicates

Every sentence has a **subject** and a **predicate**.

- **Complete subject** — all the words that tell who or what the sentence is about.
- **Complete predicate** — all the words that tell what the subject does or is.
- **Simple subject** — the main word (usually a noun or pronoun) in the subject.
- **Simple predicate** — the main word (the verb) in the predicate.

| Complete Sentence | Complete Subject | Simple Subject | Complete Predicate | Simple Predicate |
|-----------------|-----------------|----------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| The big brown dog barked loudly. | The big brown dog | dog | barked loudly. | barked |
| My sister and I went to the park. | My sister and I | sister, I | went to the park. | went |
| The flowers in the garden look beautiful. | The flowers in the garden | flowers | look beautiful. | look |
| Several students finished the test early. | Several students | students | finished the test early. | finished |
| The old man with the hat smiled. | The old man with the hat | man | smiled. | smiled |

### Verb Tenses

Verbs show **when** an action happens — past, present, or future.

| Tense | When | How to Form | Examples |
|-------|------|------------|----------|
| **Present** | happening now | base form / base + s (he/she/it) | I walk. She walks. We run. He runs. |
| **Past** | already happened | add -ed (regular) / change word (irregular) | I walked. She walked. We ran. He ran. |
| **Future** | will happen | will + base verb | I will walk. She will walk. We will run. He will run. |

**Present Tense Examples:**
- I **jump** rope every day.
- She **reads** a book.
- They **play** soccer after school.
- He **eats** breakfast at seven.

**Past Tense Examples:**
- I **jumped** rope yesterday.
- She **read** a book last night.
- They **played** soccer after school.
- He **ate** breakfast at seven.

**Future Tense Examples:**
- I **will jump** rope tomorrow.
- She **will read** a book tonight.
- They **will play** soccer after school.
- He **will eat** breakfast at seven.

### Possessive Nouns

A **possessive noun** shows ownership. To make a noun possessive, add an **apostrophe (')**.

| Rule | Singular | Singular Possessive | Plural | Plural Possessive |
|------|----------|-------------------|--------|------------------|
| Regular singular | dog | dog's | — | — |
| Regular plural | — | — | dogs | dogs' |
| Singular ending in s | James | James's (or James') | — | — |
| Irregular plural | — | — | children | children's |

**Examples:**

| Sentence | Explanation |
|----------|------------|
| The **dog's** tail wagged. | One dog owns the tail. |
| The **dogs'** water bowl is empty. | Multiple dogs share the bowl. |
| **Maria's** book is on the desk. | Maria owns the book. |
| The **children's** playground is new. | The children share the playground. |
| **Mr. Smith's** car is blue. | Mr. Smith owns the car. |
| The **teachers'** lounge is down the hall. | Multiple teachers share the lounge. |

### Comparative and Superlative Adjectives

Adjectives can be used to **compare** things.

| Type | Rule | Examples |
|------|------|----------|
| **Positive** | Just the adjective | tall, fast, happy, big |
| **Comparative** | Compare 2 things — add -er or use "more" | taller, faster, happier, bigger |
| **Superlative** | Compare 3+ things — add -est or use "most" | tallest, fastest, happiest, biggest |

**Rules for forming comparatives and superlatives:**

| Rule | Adjective | Comparative | Superlative |
|------|-----------|------------|-------------|
| Short words (1 syllable): add -er / -est | tall | taller | tallest |
| Words ending in -y: change y to i, add -er / -est | happy | happier | happiest |
| Short words ending in one consonant after short vowel: double consonant, add -er / -est | big | bigger | biggest |
| Long words (2+ syllables): use more / most | beautiful | more beautiful | most beautiful |
| Irregular adjectives: memorize | good | better | best |
| Irregular adjectives: memorize | bad | worse | worst |
| Irregular adjectives: memorize | far | farther/further | farthest/furthest |
| Irregular adjectives: memorize | little | less | least |
| Irregular adjectives: memorize | many/much | more | most |

**Example sentences:**
- The cheetah is **faster** than the lion, but the peregrine falcon is the **fastest** of all.
- This book is **more interesting** than that one, but the third book is the **most interesting** in the series.
- She is a **better** singer than her brother, but their mother is the **best** singer of all.

### Prepositions

A **preposition** is a word that shows the relationship between a noun (or pronoun) and another word in the sentence. It often tells **where** or **when**.

**Common Prepositions:**

| Position | Time | Other |
|----------|------|-------|
| in, on, at, under, over, above, below, between, behind, in front of, beside, next to, near, far from, around, through, across, along, into, out of, onto, off, up, down, toward, away from | in, on, at, before, after, during, since, until, by, about, around | with, without, about, of, from, like, as, against, for, to |

**Examples:**
- The cat is **under** the table.
- We go to school **in** the morning.
- She walked **through** the forest.
- The book is **on** the shelf.
- He arrived **after** lunch.
- They sat **beside** each other.
- The bird flew **over** the house.
- We will meet **at** the park.

### Conjunctions

**Conjunctions** connect words, phrases, or clauses.

**Coordinating Conjunctions (FANBOYS):**

| Conjunction | Use | Example |
|------------|-----|---------|
| **For** | gives a reason | I stayed inside, **for** it was raining. |
| **And** | adds information | I like apples **and** bananas. |
| **Nor** | adds a negative | She does not like peas, **nor** does she like carrots. |
| **But** | shows contrast | It was cold, **but** we went outside anyway. |
| **Or** | gives a choice | Would you like tea **or** juice? |
| **Yet** | shows contrast (stronger) | He was tired, **yet** he kept working. |
| **So** | shows result | It was raining, **so** we stayed inside. |

**Subordinating Conjunctions** connect a dependent clause to an independent clause:

| Conjunction | Example |
|------------|---------|
| because | We stayed inside **because** it was raining. |
| when | Call me **when** you get home. |
| while | She read **while** he slept. |
| if | **If** it snows, school will be closed. |
| after | We had dessert **after** we ate dinner. |
| before | Wash your hands **before** you eat. |
| since | He has been happy **since** he got the puppy. |
| until | Wait here **until** I come back. |
| although | **Although** she was scared, she went on the ride. |

### Abbreviations

An **abbreviation** is a shortened form of a word or phrase.

| Type | Full Form | Abbreviation |
|------|----------|-------------|
| Months | January, February, March, April, June, July, August, September, October, November, December | Jan., Feb., Mar., Apr., Jun., Jul., Aug., Sep., Oct., Nov., Dec. |
| Days | Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday, Sunday | Mon., Tue. (Tues.), Wed., Thu. (Thurs.), Fri., Sat., Sun. |
| Titles | Mister, Missus, Miss, Doctor, Professor | Mr., Mrs., Ms., Dr., Prof. |
| Addresses | Street, Avenue, Boulevard, Road, Lane, Drive | St., Ave., Blvd., Rd., Ln., Dr. |
| Place names | United States of America, United Kingdom | USA, UK |
| Measurements | tablespoon, teaspoon, ounce, pound, inch, foot, cup, quart | tbsp., tsp., oz., lb., in., ft., c., qt. |
| Other | for example, that is, versus | e.g., i.e., vs. |
| Professional | company, incorporated, limited | Co., Inc., Ltd. |

**Rules for abbreviations:**
- Always put a **period** at the end of most abbreviations.
- Abbreviations for titles (Mr., Mrs., Dr.) are used **before** a person's name: Dr. Smith, Mrs. Johnson.
- Abbreviations for months are used **with dates**: Jan. 15, 2026.
- Do NOT abbreviate in formal writing (essays, reports).

## 3. Reading Skills

### Narrative Elements

A **narrative** is a story. Every narrative has key elements:

| Element | Definition | Example |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| **Plot** | The events of the story in order — what happens | A boy finds a lost puppy, searches for the owner, and finally returns the puppy home. |
| **Characters** | The people, animals, or beings in the story | Jack (the boy), Max (the puppy), the owner (Mrs. Lee) |
| **Setting** | Where and when the story takes place | A small neighborhood on a Saturday afternoon in summer |
| **Theme** | The lesson or message of the story | Helping others is the right thing to do, even when it is hard work. |

**Parts of a Plot:**

| Part | Description |
|------|------------|
| **Exposition (Beginning)** | Introduces characters, setting, and the basic situation. |
| **Rising Action** | Events build up; the main character faces challenges. |
| **Climax** | The most exciting or important moment — the turning point. |
| **Falling Action** | Events that happen after the climax as things calm down. |
| **Resolution (Ending)** | The problem is solved; the story wraps up. |

**Character Traits** — words that describe a character's personality:
- brave, clever, kind, selfish, generous, curious, honest, patient, stubborn, funny, loyal

### Non-Fiction Text Features

Non-fiction (informational) books and articles have special features that help you find and understand information:

| Text Feature | What It Is | Purpose |
|-------------|-----------|---------|
| **Title** | The name of the book or section | Tells you what the text will be about |
| **Headings** | Bold or large text at the top of a section | Breaks the text into sections; helps you find topics |
| **Subheadings** | Smaller headings under main headings | Further divides information |
| **Table of Contents** | List of chapters/sections with page numbers | Helps you find where information is located |
| **Index** | Alphabetical list of topics with page numbers (at the back of the book) | Helps you look up specific topics quickly |
| **Glossary** | List of important words and their definitions (at the back) | Defines key vocabulary from the text |
| **Captions** | Text below or beside a picture or photograph | Explains what the image shows |
| **Labels** | Words pointing to parts of a diagram | Names specific parts |
| **Bold / Italic Text** | Words printed darker or slanted | Highlights important terms |
| **Bullets / Numbers** | Lists of information | Organizes facts clearly |
| **Maps / Charts / Graphs** | Visual displays of data | Shows information visually |
| **Diagrams** | Drawings with labels | Shows parts of something or how it works |
| **Sidebars** | Boxes of text on the side of the page | Provides extra information |
| **Photographs / Illustrations** | Real images or drawings | Show what something looks like |

### Author's Purpose — PIE

Authors write for different reasons. You can remember them with **PIE**:

| Purpose | What It Means | Key Words to Look For | Example |
|---------|--------------|----------------------|---------|
| **P** — Persuade | To convince the reader to think or do something | should, must, believe, argue, opinion, best, worst | "You should recycle every day to help save our planet." |
| **I** — Inform | To teach the reader facts and information | facts, explain, describe, tell, information, data | "The Amazon Rainforest produces about 20% of the world's oxygen." |
| **E** — Entertain | To amuse, interest, or engage the reader | characters, story, fun, exciting, adventure, once upon a time | A funny story about a talking dog who goes to school. |

**Sometimes a text has more than one purpose.** For example, a persuasive essay might also inform you with facts.

### Fact vs. Opinion

| | Fact | Opinion |
|---|------|---------|
| **Definition** | A statement that can be PROVED true or false | A statement that shows someone's belief or feeling — it CANNOT be proved |
| **Can be proven?** | YES | NO |
| **Examples** | The sun rises in the east. Water freezes at 32°F. There are 50 states in the United States. | Pizza is the best food. Dogs are better than cats. Summer is the most fun season. |
| **Key words** | numbers, dates, names, measurements, scientific facts | best, worst, should, think, believe, pretty, beautiful, fun, boring, delicious |

**Practice:**

| Statement | Fact or Opinion? | Why? |
|-----------|-------------------|------|
| The Earth orbits the Sun. | **Fact** | This can be proven by science. |
| Green is the prettiest color. | **Opinion** | Some people may disagree; it is a feeling. |
| A cheetah can run 70 miles per hour. | **Fact** | This can be measured and proven. |
| School should be shorter. | **Opinion** | Not everyone agrees; it is a belief. |
| Abraham Lincoln was the 16th president. | **Fact** | This is a historical fact. |
| Broccoli tastes terrible. | **Opinion** | Some people love broccoli; it is a preference. |

### Making Inferences

An **inference** is a smart guess or conclusion you make by combining what you **read** with what you **already know**. Authors do not always state everything directly — good readers infer meaning.

**Formula:** What I read + What I know = **My Inference**

| What You Read | What You Know | Inference |
|--------------|--------------|-----------|
| "Maria's eyes were red and puffy. She sniffled and wiped her face with a tissue." | People get red, puffy eyes and sniffle when they cry. | Maria has been crying. She is probably sad or upset. |
| "Tom slumped in his chair and stared at the floor. His shoulders drooped." | People slouch and look down when they are unhappy or disappointed. | Tom is sad or disappointed about something. |
| "Sara grabbed her coat, scarf, and gloves and looked out the window at the gray sky." | Coats, scarves, and gloves are for cold weather. | It is cold outside. It might snow. |
| "The classroom was completely silent. Every student had a pencil in hand and was looking at their paper." | Students are quiet and focused when taking a test. | The students are taking a test. |

**Signal that you need to infer:** When the author does not directly tell you how someone feels, what happened, or why something happened — you must infer!

## 4. Writing

### Multi-Paragraph Essays

An essay is longer than a single paragraph. A basic essay has **3 or more paragraphs**:

**Structure:**

| Paragraph | Purpose | What to Include |
|-----------|---------|----------------|
| **Introduction** (1st paragraph) | Hook the reader and state your topic | Hook (attention-grabber), background information, thesis/main idea statement |
| **Body** (1–3 paragraphs) | Provide details, evidence, and examples | Each body paragraph has one main idea + supporting details |
| **Conclusion** (last paragraph) | Wrap up and leave the reader thinking | Restate main idea (in new words), summarize key points, closing thought |

**Example Essay — "Why Reading Is Important":**

> **(Introduction)** Reading is one of the most important skills a person can learn. It opens doors to new worlds, teaches us about different people and places, and helps our brains grow stronger. Every child should make time to read every day.
>
> **(Body 1)** First, reading helps us learn new things. When you read a book about space, you learn about planets and stars. When you read about history, you learn about people who lived long ago. Books are like teachers that you can carry with you everywhere.
>
> **(Body 2)** Second, reading makes our imaginations grow. When you read a story, you picture the characters and places in your mind. You travel to magical lands, meet brave heroes, and go on exciting adventures — all from your chair!
>
> **(Body 3)** Finally, reading improves our vocabulary. The more books you read, the more words you learn. A strong vocabulary helps you become a better writer and speaker. It also helps you understand more of what people say.
>
> **(Conclusion)** In conclusion, reading is important because it teaches us, builds our imagination, and improves our vocabulary. Whether you like fiction, non-fiction, or comics, the most important thing is to pick up a book and start reading. Your future self will thank you!

### Opinion Writing

In opinion writing, you **state your opinion** and support it with **reasons and evidence**.

**Structure:**

| Part | What to Write |
|------|--------------|
| **State your opinion** | Clearly say what you believe (thesis statement) |
| **Give reasons** | 2–3 reasons that support your opinion |
| **Provide evidence** | Facts, examples, or personal experiences for each reason |
| **Address the other side** | Acknowledge the opposing opinion, then explain why yours is stronger |
| **Conclusion** | Restate your opinion in a new way and end with a strong statement |

**Example opinion paragraph:**
> I believe that every student should have at least 30 minutes of recess every day. First, children need physical activity to stay healthy. According to doctors, kids should exercise for at least one hour each day, and recess helps them reach that goal. Second, recess gives students a break from sitting still, which helps them focus better in class. Studies show that students who have recess pay more attention after the break. Finally, recess is when students learn important social skills like sharing, taking turns, and solving disagreements. For these reasons, schools should protect recess time and make sure every student gets to play outside.

### Informational Writing

Informational writing **teaches the reader** about a topic using facts and details. It does NOT include the writer's opinions or feelings.

**Structure:**

| Part | What to Include |
|------|----------------|
| **Introduction** | Hook + what the topic is + brief overview of what will be covered |
| **Body paragraphs** | Each paragraph covers ONE subtopic with facts, details, and examples |
| **Conclusion** | Summarize key points + interesting final fact or thought |

**Example informational paragraph — "The Life Cycle of a Butterfly":**
> A butterfly goes through four stages in its life. This process is called metamorphosis. First, a female butterfly lays an egg on a leaf. The egg is very tiny and round. Next, the egg hatches into a caterpillar, which is also called a larva. The caterpillar eats leaves and grows bigger. It sheds its skin several times as it grows. Then, the caterpillar forms a chrysalis around itself. Inside the chrysalis, the caterpillar changes into a butterfly. This stage is called the pupa. Finally, the chrysalis opens, and an adult butterfly emerges. Its wings are wet at first, but they soon dry, and the butterfly is ready to fly. The entire process takes about three to four weeks.

### Narrative Writing with Dialogue

A **narrative** tells a story. Good narratives include:
- A clear beginning, middle, and end
- Characters with thoughts and feelings
- A setting that is described
- A problem and a solution
- **Dialogue** — the exact words characters say

**Rules for writing dialogue:**
1. Use **quotation marks (" ")** around the exact words a character says.
2. A new speaker gets a **new paragraph**.
3. Use a **comma** before the quotation when a dialogue tag comes before the quote.
4. Use a **comma, period, question mark, or exclamation mark** inside the closing quotation mark.
5. **Capitalize** the first letter of the quote.
6. Use dialogue tags like: said, asked, exclaimed, whispered, replied, shouted.

**Examples:**

| Rule | Example |
|------|---------|
| Dialogue tag before quote | Mom said, "Please clean your room." |
| Dialogue tag after quote | "I'll do it right now," I replied. |
| Dialogue tag in the middle | "I can't believe it," she whispered. "We won!" |
| Question | Jake asked, "Can we go to the park?" |
| Exclamation | "Watch out!" yelled Sam. |
| Two speakers | "What are you doing?" asked Lily. "I'm building a fort," Tom answered. |
| New paragraph for new speaker | "Are you coming to my party?" Maria asked. | "Of course!" said Juan. "I wouldn't miss it." |

**Example narrative with dialogue:**

> One rainy afternoon, I was bored and decided to visit my neighbor, Mr. Chen. I knocked on his door, and he opened it with a big smile.
>
> "Well, hello there!" he said. "Come on in out of the rain."
>
> "Thank you, Mr. Chen," I said as I stepped inside. His house was warm and smelled like fresh cookies.
>
> "I just baked chocolate chip cookies," he told me. "Would you like some?"
>
> "Yes, please!" I exclaimed.
>
> While we ate cookies, Mr. Chen showed me his collection of model airplanes. He had dozens of them, each one carefully painted. "This one is my favorite," he said, pointing to a silver plane. "I built it with my grandfather when I was your age."
>
> I stared at the beautiful airplane and smiled. "Maybe you could teach me to build one someday," I said.
>
> Mr. Chen laughed. "I would love that," he replied. "Come back next Saturday and we will start."

### Advanced Transition Words

Good writers use **transition words** to connect ideas smoothly. Here is a comprehensive list organized by type:

| Type | Transition Words |
|------|-----------------|
| **Showing addition** | also, additionally, in addition, furthermore, moreover, besides, equally important, another, as well |
| **Showing order** | first, second, third, next, then, after that, later, meanwhile, soon, finally, last, lastly |
| **Showing time** | today, yesterday, tomorrow, in the morning, in the afternoon, in the evening, before long, shortly after, at last, eventually |
| **Showing contrast** | however, on the other hand, in contrast, nevertheless, still, yet, although, even though, instead, conversely |
| **Showing comparison** | similarly, likewise, in the same way, just as, equally, compared to |
| **Showing cause and effect** | because, since, therefore, consequently, as a result, thus, for this reason, so, accordingly, due to |
| **Giving examples** | for example, for instance, specifically, to illustrate, such as, including, namely |
| **Emphasizing** | in fact, indeed, certainly, truly, absolutely, without a doubt, above all, most importantly |
| **Summarizing** | in conclusion, to summarize, all in all, in summary, on the whole, to sum up, in short, ultimately |
| **Showing condition** | if, unless, whether, provided that, as long as, on the condition that |

**How to use advanced transitions in writing:**

> I love playing soccer. **Furthermore**, it helps me stay healthy and make friends. **However**, practice can be tiring after a long day at school. **Nevertheless**, I never want to quit. **For example**, last season our team won the championship after months of hard work. **In conclusion**, soccer teaches me about teamwork and dedication, which are important life lessons.
