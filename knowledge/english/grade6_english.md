# Grade 6 English – Complete Study Guide

## 1. Reading Comprehension

Reading comprehension is the ability to understand, interpret, and analyse what you read. Questions fall into three main types.

### 1.1 Literal Questions

Literal questions ask you to find information **directly stated** in the text. You just need to locate the facts.

**Tips:**
- Look for exact words or numbers in the passage.
- Scan the text for keywords from the question.
- Do not add your own ideas — stick to what is written.

> **Example Passage:** "The science fair will be held on Friday, March 15, in the school gymnasium from 9:00 a.m. to 12:00 p.m. Students in grades 5–8 may enter projects."
>
> **Literal Question:** Where will the science fair take place?
> **Answer:** In the school gymnasium.

### 1.2 Inferential Questions

Inferential questions require you to **draw conclusions** using clues from the text plus your own reasoning. The answer is not directly stated.

**Tips:**
- Ask yourself: *What does the author suggest but not say outright?*
- Use context clues, character actions, and descriptions.
- Combine multiple pieces of evidence.

> **Example Passage:** "Maria slammed her locker shut and walked down the hall with her head down. She shoved her books into her bag without looking at anyone."
>
> **Inferential Question:** How is Maria probably feeling?
> **Answer:** Maria is likely feeling upset or angry. The evidence is that she slammed her locker, kept her head down, and avoided looking at anyone — all signs of distress.

### 1.3 Evaluative Questions

Evaluative questions ask you to **judge or form an opinion** about the text — the author's purpose, the quality of an argument, or whether you agree.

**Tips:**
- Think about the author's message and whether it is convincing.
- Consider whether the evidence is strong and logical.
- Support your opinion with examples from the text.

> **Example:** "The author argues that all students should have homework every night. Do you agree? Why or why not?"
> **Answer (sample):** I partially agree. Homework can reinforce learning, but too much can cause stress. The author only presents one side and does not mention the importance of free time, which weakens the argument.

### 1.4 Comprehension Strategies

| Strategy | Description |
|---|---|
| **Predicting** | Guess what will happen next based on clues. |
| **Summarising** | Retell the main ideas in your own words. |
| **Questioning** | Ask who, what, when, where, why, and how. |
| **Visualising** | Create mental images of scenes or events. |
| **Making Connections** | Link the text to your own experience, other texts, or the world. |

## 2. Vocabulary

### 2.1 Prefixes

A **prefix** is a group of letters added to the **beginning** of a word that changes its meaning.

| Prefix | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| un- | not, opposite of | unhappy, unfair |
| re- | again | redo, rewrite |
| dis- | not, opposite of | disagree, disappear |
| mis- | wrong, badly | misunderstand, misplace |
| pre- | before | preview, pretest |
| im-/in- | not | impossible, invisible |
| over- | too much | overcook, overflow |
| sub- | under, below | submarine, subtitle |
| inter- | between | international, interact |
| trans- | across | transport, transfer |

**Practice:**
1. What does "uncomfortable" mean? → Not comfortable.
2. Add a prefix to "trust" to mean the opposite. → **Distrust** or **mistrust**.

### 2.2 Suffixes

A **suffix** is added to the **end** of a word. It can change the meaning or the part of speech.

| Suffix | Meaning / Part of Speech | Example |
|---|---|---|
| -ful | full of (adjective) | helpful, beautiful |
| -less | without (adjective) | careless, hopeless |
| -ness | state of being (noun) | kindness, sadness |
| -ly | in the manner of (adverb) | quickly, happily |
| -er / -or | one who does (noun) | teacher, actor |
| -tion / -sion | act or state of (noun) | education, discussion |
| -able / -ible | able to be (adjective) | readable, visible |
| -ment | act or result (noun) | enjoyment, achievement |
| -ous | full of (adjective) | dangerous, curious |
| -ive | having the quality of (adjective) | creative, active |

**Practice:**
1. The suffix "-less" turns "care" into what? → **Careless** (without care).
2. What part of speech does "-ly" usually create? → **Adverb**.

### 2.3 Context Clues

**Context clues** are hints within a sentence or paragraph that help you figure out the meaning of an unknown word.

**Types of context clues:**

1. **Definition clue:** The author directly defines the word.
   > "The archaeologist, a person who studies ancient remains, carefully brushed dirt off the pottery."

2. **Synonym clue:** A word with a similar meaning appears nearby.
   > "The hallway was **dim**; the lights were very **faint**."

3. **Antonym clue:** A word with the opposite meaning appears nearby.
   > "Unlike her **gregarious** sister who loved parties, Emma preferred to be alone."

4. **Example clue:** Examples are given to illustrate the word.
   > "The store sold **confections** such as chocolates, lollipops, and caramel."

5. **Explanation clue:** The meaning is explained in the next sentence.
   > "The river was **treacherous**. Many swimmers had been caught in its strong, hidden currents."

### 2.4 Word Families

A **word family** is a group of words that share the same root or base word. Understanding word families helps you figure out new words.

**Base: ACT** (to do)

| Word | Part of Speech | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| act | verb / noun | to do something / a deed |
| action | noun | the process of doing something |
| active | adjective | busy, involved |
| actively | adverb | in an active way |
| activity | noun | something you do |
| activate | verb | to make something start working |
| actor | noun | a person who performs |

**Base: STRUCT** (to build)

| Word | Part of Speech | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| structure | noun / verb | the way something is built |
| construct | verb | to build |
| construction | noun | the act of building |
| destructive | adjective | causing damage |

**Practice:** Build a word family from the base **FORM** (shape):
→ form, reform, transform, formation, formal, formally, formless

## 3. Grammar

### 3.1 Parts of Speech Review

| Part of Speech | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Noun** | A person, place, thing, or idea | dog, London, freedom |
| **Pronoun** | Replaces a noun | he, she, it, they, we |
| **Verb** | An action or state of being | run, think, is, seem |
| **Adjective** | Describes a noun | tall, red, amazing |
| **Adverb** | Describes a verb, adjective, or adverb | quickly, very, carefully |
| **Preposition** | Shows relationship (time, place, direction) | in, on, at, under |
| **Conjunction** | Connects words or clauses | and, but, or, because |
| **Interjection** | Expresses emotion | Wow!, Oh no!, Hurray! |

**Tip to remember:** Every word in a sentence has a job. Ask yourself: *What does this word do here?*

### 3.2 Subject-Verb Agreement

The **subject** and **verb** in a sentence must agree in number.

**Rules:**

1. **Singular subject → singular verb**
   - The cat **runs** every morning.
   - She **is** reading a book.

2. **Plural subject → plural verb**
   - The cats **run** every morning.
   - They **are** reading books.

3. **Compound subjects joined by "and" → plural verb**
   - Tom **and** Jerry **play** outside.

4. **Compound subjects joined by "or/nor" → verb agrees with the closer subject**
   - Neither the teacher nor the **students were** ready.
   - Neither the students nor the **teacher was** ready.

5. **Collective nouns** (group, team, family) → usually singular
   - The team **is** winning the game.

6. **Indefinite pronouns** (everyone, anyone, nobody, each) → singular
   - Everyone **wants** to go.

7. **Don't be fooled by phrases between the subject and verb:**
   - The box of chocolates **is** on the table. *(not "are")*

**Practice:**
1. The dogs (bark / barks) loudly. → **bark**
2. Each of the girls (has / have) a notebook. → **has**
3. Neither rain nor snow (stops / stop) the mail. → **stops**

### 3.3 Tenses: Perfect Progressive

The **perfect progressive** tenses show an action that started in the past and is still continuing or has just finished.

| Tense | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Present Perfect Progressive** | has/have been + verb-ing | I **have been studying** for two hours. (started in the past, still studying) |
| **Past Perfect Progressive** | had been + verb-ing | She **had been waiting** for an hour when the bus arrived. (was waiting before another past event) |
| **Future Perfect Progressive** | will have been + verb-ing | By next June, he **will have been living** here for ten years. (focus on duration before a future point) |

**Signal words:**
- Present Perfect Progressive: for, since, all day, recently
- Past Perfect Progressive: by the time, before, for (when referring to a past duration)
- Future Perfect Progressive: by (future date), for (duration)

**Practice:**
1. They ___ (play) football since 3 p.m. → **have been playing**
2. By the time I arrived, she ___ (work) on the project for three hours. → **had been working**

### 3.4 Active and Passive Voice

**Active voice:** The subject **does** the action.
- *The dog chased the ball.*

**Passive voice:** The subject **receives** the action.
- *The ball was chased by the dog.*

**How to form the passive:**
| Tense | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Simple Present | She writes the report. | The report is written by her. |
| Simple Past | He painted the wall. | The wall was painted by him. |
| Present Perfect | They have finished the work. | The work has been finished by them. |
| Future | We will send the email. | The email will be sent by us. |

**Structure:** Object of active sentence → Subject of passive sentence + be verb + past participle (+ by + agent).

**When to use passive:**
- When the doer is unknown or unimportant.
  - *The painting was stolen last night.*
- When you want to emphasise the receiver.
  - *The new bridge was opened by the mayor.*
- In formal or scientific writing.
  - *The experiment was conducted over three weeks.*

**Practice (change to passive):**
1. The teacher graded the papers. → **The papers were graded by the teacher.**
2. Scientists have discovered a new planet. → **A new planet has been discovered by scientists.**

### 3.5 Direct and Indirect Speech

**Direct speech** reports the exact words a person said, using quotation marks.

> She said, **"I am going to the market."**

**Indirect speech (reported speech)** reports what someone said without quoting exactly.

> She said **that she was going to the market**.

**Key changes when converting direct to indirect:**

| Change | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Tense** | Backshift one step: Present → Past; Past → Past Perfect |
| **Pronouns** | Change to match the speaker's perspective |
| **Time/Place words** | now → then, today → that day, here → there, tomorrow → the next day, yesterday → the day before |

**Tense backshift examples:**

| Direct Speech | Indirect Speech |
|---|---|
| "I **am** happy." | He said he **was** happy. |
| "She **likes** pizza." | He said she **liked** pizza. |
| "They **went** home." | She said they **had gone** home. |
| "I **will** come." | He said he **would** come. |
| "I **can** swim." | She said she **could** swim. |
| "I **may** help." | He said he **might** help. |

**Reporting verbs:**
- said, told, asked, explained, promised, warned, suggested

**Practice:**
1. He said, "I forgot my homework." → He said that he had forgotten his homework.
2. "Are you coming?" she asked me. → She asked me if I was coming.

**Questions in reported speech:**
- Yes/No questions → use **if** or **whether**
  - He asked, "Do you like tea?" → He asked if I liked tea.
- Wh- questions → keep the question word
  - She asked, "Where are you going?" → She asked where I was going.

### 3.6 Conditionals

Conditionals describe a condition and its result. There are four main types.

#### Zero Conditional (general truths)
**Structure:** If + present simple, present simple
> **If** you heat water to 100°C, **it boils**.

#### First Conditional (real/possible future)
**Structure:** If + present simple, will + base verb
> **If** it rains tomorrow, **we will cancel** the picnic.
> **I will help** you **if** you ask me.

#### Second Conditional (unreal/hypothetical present or future)
**Structure:** If + past simple, would + base verb
> **If** I had wings, **I would fly** around the world.
> *(I don't have wings — this is imaginary.)*

#### Third Conditional (unreal past — things that didn't happen)
**Structure:** If + past perfect, would have + past participle
> **If** she had studied harder, **she would have passed** the exam.
> *(She didn't study hard and she didn't pass — regret about the past.)*

**Mixed Conditional (past condition → present result)**
> If he had taken the doctor's advice (past), he would be healthy now (present).

**Practice:**
1. If I ___ (be) the president, I would improve education. → **were**
2. If she ___ (study) last night, she would have passed the test. → **had studied**
3. If it rains, we ___ (stay) inside. → **will stay**

### 3.7 Relative Clauses

A **relative clause** gives more information about a noun. It begins with a **relative pronoun**.

**Relative pronouns:** who, whom, whose, which, that

| Pronoun | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **who** | People (subject) | The girl **who** won the race is my friend. |
| **whom** | People (object) | The man **whom** I met yesterday is a doctor. |
| **whose** | Possession | The boy **whose** dog is missing is very sad. |
| **which** | Things/animals | The book **which** is on the table is mine. |
| **that** | People or things (defining only) | The car **that** he bought is very fast. |

**Defining relative clauses** (essential information — no commas):
> The students **who passed** the exam will receive certificates. *(Only the students who passed — the clause defines which students.)*

**Non-defining relative clauses** (extra information — use commas):
> My brother, **who lives in London**, is a teacher. *(We already know which brother — the clause adds extra information.)*

**Note:** In non-defining clauses, do NOT use "that."
- ✅ My car, **which** is red, is very old.
- ❌ My car, **that** is red, is very old.

**Practice:**
1. The man ___ bag was stolen called the police. → **whose**
2. This is the book ___ I bought yesterday. → **that / which**

### 3.8 Transitions (Linking Words)

Transitions connect ideas and help writing flow smoothly.

| Purpose | Transitions | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Addition** | and, also, furthermore, moreover, in addition | She plays guitar. **Furthermore**, she sings. |
| **Contrast** | but, however, although, nevertheless, on the other hand | He was tired; **however**, he kept running. |
| **Cause/Effect** | because, since, therefore, as a result, consequently | It rained heavily, **so** the match was cancelled. |
| **Sequence/Time** | first, then, next, finally, meanwhile, afterwards | **First**, chop the onions. **Then**, fry them. |
| **Emphasis** | indeed, in fact, certainly, without a doubt | The test was, **in fact**, much harder than expected. |
| **Conclusion** | in conclusion, to sum up, overall, all in all | **Overall**, the trip was a great success. |
| **Example** | for example, for instance, such as | I love fruit, **such as** mangoes and bananas. |
| **Condition** | if, unless, provided that, as long as | You can go out **provided that** you finish your homework. |

**Practice:** Complete with appropriate transitions:
1. I woke up late. ___, I missed the bus. → **As a result**
2. She is talented ___ hardworking. → **and / as well as**

## 4. Writing

### 4.1 Formal and Informal Letters

#### Formal Letter (e.g., to a principal, government office, company)

**Format:**

```
[Your Address]
[City, State, ZIP]
[Date]

[Recipient's Name]
[Recipient's Title/Organization]
[Address]

Dear [Title + Last Name],      (e.g., Dear Mr. Johnson,)

[Body Paragraph 1: State your purpose clearly]

[Body Paragraph 2: Provide details and supporting information]

[Body Paragraph 3: State what action you want and express gratitude]

Yours sincerely,

[Your Full Name]
```

**Key features:** Professional tone, no slang, complete sentences, structured paragraphs.

> **Sample:** Write to your principal requesting a library extension.
>
> Dear Mrs. Patterson,
>
> I am writing to request that the school library remain open for an additional hour after school. Many students, including myself, find it difficult to complete research assignments during the current hours due to extracurricular activities. An extended schedule would give us the opportunity to use the library's resources more effectively.
>
> I believe this change would benefit a large number of students. Thank you for considering this request.
>
> Yours sincerely,
> Alex Rivera

#### Informal Letter (e.g., to a friend or relative)

**Format:**

```
[Your Address]
[Date]

Dear [First Name],

[Opening: Ask about their well-being]

[Body: Share news, tell a story, describe an event]

[Closing: Warm ending]

Best wishes / Warmly / Love,

[Your First Name]
```

**Key features:** Conversational tone, contractions allowed, personal language.

> **Sample:** Write to a friend about a recent trip.
>
> Dear Sam,
>
> How have you been? I hope everything is going well. I just got back from a trip to the mountains and it was amazing!
>
> We went hiking every day and even saw a bear from a distance! The views were incredible, especially at sunset. I took loads of photos — I'll show you when we meet.
>
> Let's catch up soon. How about grabbing ice cream this Saturday?
>
> Best wishes,
> Jordan

### 4.2 Email Writing

Emails are similar to letters but more concise. Always include a clear **subject line**.

```
To: [recipient's email]
Subject: [Clear, brief subject]

Dear [Name],

[Main message — keep it focused and polite]

[Closing]

Best regards,
[Your Name]
```

> **Sample — requesting information:**
>
> Subject: Inquiry About Summer Science Camp
>
> Dear Sir/Madam,
>
> I am a Grade 6 student and I am interested in the Summer Science Camp advertised on your website. Could you please send me details about the dates, fees, and registration process?
>
> Thank you for your time.
>
> Best regards,
> Taylor Kim

### 4.3 Notice Writing

A **notice** is a short, formal announcement displayed publicly.

**Format:**

```
[Name of School/Organization]
NOTICE

[Title — brief and clear]
Date: [Date of writing]

[Body — what, when, where, who, why — in 3–4 lines]

[Signature and Designation]
```

> **Sample:**
>
> GREENFIELD MIDDLE SCHOOL
> NOTICE
>
> **Inter-House Sports Day**
> Date: 10 July 2026
>
> All students are informed that the Annual Inter-House Sports Day will be held on 25 July 2026 on the school playground. Events include relay races, long jump, and shot put. Interested students should give their names to their house captains by 15 July. Practice sessions begin on 18 July at 3:30 p.m.
>
> Riya Malhotra
> Sports Captain

### 4.4 Article Writing

An **article** presents information or opinions on a topic for a newspaper, magazine, or school publication.

**Structure:**
1. **Heading** — Catchy and relevant
2. **Introduction** — Hook the reader and state the topic
3. **Body** — 2–3 paragraphs with facts, examples, and opinions
4. **Conclusion** — Summarise and give a final thought

> **Sample — The Importance of Reading**
>
> **Books: Your Best Friends**
>
> Have you ever been completely lost in a story? That feeling — when the real world fades away and you are living inside a book — is one of the greatest experiences reading can offer.
>
> Reading improves vocabulary, strengthens imagination, and builds empathy. When we read about characters from different backgrounds, we learn to understand perspectives that differ from our own. Studies show that regular readers perform better in school and develop stronger critical thinking skills.
>
> In today's world of screens and social media, setting aside even 20 minutes a day for a book can make a significant difference. Whether it is fantasy, biography, science fiction, or mystery, there is a book out there for everyone.
>
> So pick up a book today. You might just discover a whole new world between the pages.

### 4.5 Diary Entry

A diary entry is **personal** — written in first person, sharing thoughts and feelings about events.

**Format:**
- Date (usually top left or top right)
- First person ("I")
- Reflective and descriptive
- Informal, emotional tone

> **Sample:**
>
> *Thursday, 9 July 2026*
>
> Dear Diary,
>
> Today was one of the best days of my life! Our class went on a field trip to the science museum, and I got to see a real space suit up close. It was incredible to think that someone actually wore that while floating in space.
>
> The planetarium show was my favourite part. The whole ceiling turned into a night sky, and I felt like I was actually flying through the stars. I even got to touch a meteorite — it was heavier than I expected!
>
> I wish I could go back every day. Maybe I'll become an astronaut one day. Who knows?
>
> — Alex

### 4.6 Descriptive Paragraphs

A descriptive paragraph paints a **picture with words** using sensory details.

**Techniques:**
- Use the five senses: sight, sound, smell, touch, taste
- Use strong adjectives and vivid verbs
- Include similes and metaphors
- Show, don't just tell

> **Sample:**
>
> The old lighthouse stood tall against the grey sky, its white paint peeling in long strips like bark from a dying tree. Waves crashed against the rocks below, sending sprays of salty mist into the air. The wind howled through the cracks in the wooden door, making a sound like a long, low whistle. Inside, the air smelled of damp stone and old rope. A single beam of light swept across the dark water, cutting through the fog like a golden sword.

### 4.7 Narrative Paragraphs

A narrative paragraph **tells a story** with a clear sequence of events.

**Elements:**
- A beginning (sets the scene)
- A middle (events and conflict)
- An end (resolution)
- Transition words to show order

> **Sample:**
>
> Last Saturday started like any other morning, but it quickly turned into an adventure. I was walking my dog in the park when he suddenly bolted after a squirrel. Before I could react, he had darted across the field and disappeared into a thicket of bushes. I called his name, heart pounding, and searched for what felt like hours. Just as I was about to give up and call for help, a little girl appeared holding Max's leash. She said she had found him sitting by the pond, watching the ducks. I thanked her and walked home with the biggest sigh of relief I had ever breathed.

### 4.8 Five-Paragraph Essay

The **five-paragraph essay** is a structured way to present an argument or analysis.

**Structure:**

| Paragraph | Content |
|---|---|
| **1. Introduction** | Hook + background + thesis statement |
| **2. Body 1** | First main point + evidence/example |
| **3. Body 2** | Second main point + evidence/example |
| **4. Body 3** | Third main point + evidence/example |
| **5. Conclusion** | Restate thesis (differently) + summarise + final thought |

**Sample outline — "Should Students Have Homework Every Day?"**

- **Introduction:** Hook (question or surprising fact) → brief context about the homework debate → **Thesis:** While homework reinforces learning, assigning it every day can be harmful to students' well-being and should be limited.

- **Body 1:** Too much homework causes stress and reduces free time for hobbies, family, and rest.

- **Body 2:** Research shows that excessive homework does not necessarily improve academic performance.

- **Body 3:** Quality matters more than quantity — meaningful assignments are more effective than repetitive worksheets.

- **Conclusion:** Restate thesis → homework should be purposeful and balanced → final thought: schools should focus on the quality of learning, not the quantity of assignments.

### 4.9 Debate Writing

A debate presents **arguments for and against** a topic, followed by a reasoned conclusion.

**Structure:**
1. **Introduction** — State the topic and your position
2. **Arguments FOR** (with evidence)
3. **Arguments AGAINST** (acknowledging the other side)
4. **Rebuttal** — Explain why your position is stronger
5. **Conclusion** — Summarise and state your final opinion

> **Sample — "School Uniforms Should Be Required"**
>
> **Introduction:**
> The question of whether schools should require uniforms has been debated for years. Some believe uniforms promote equality and discipline, while others argue they restrict individuality.
>
> **For:**
> School uniforms create a sense of belonging and reduce bullying related to clothing. When everyone wears the same outfit, students are less likely to be judged based on their family's income. Uniforms also save time in the morning and reduce distractions in the classroom.
>
> **Against:**
> Critics argue that uniforms limit self-expression. Students should be able to show their personality through their clothing. Additionally, uniforms can be expensive for families who struggle financially.
>
> **Rebuttal:**
> While self-expression is important, school is primarily a place for learning. Students can express themselves through art, writing, sports, and extracurricular activities. Financial aid programmes can help families who cannot afford uniforms.
>
> **Conclusion:**
> On balance, the benefits of school uniforms — equality, discipline, and reduced distractions — outweigh the drawbacks. Schools should consider uniform policies while also ensuring support for families in need.

## 5. Literature Elements

### 5.1 Narrative Perspective (Point of View)

The **narrative perspective** tells us **who is telling the story**.

| Point of View | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **First Person** | The narrator is a character in the story ("I", "we") | "I couldn't believe what I saw that night." |
| **Second Person** | The narrator speaks directly to the reader ("you") | "You walk into the room and everything changes." |
| **Third Person Limited** | The narrator is outside the story but follows ONE character's thoughts | "She felt a knot in her stomach as the door opened." |
| **Third Person Omniscient** | The narrator knows ALL characters' thoughts and feelings | "He smiled, but she had no idea what was coming." |

**How to identify:**
1. Look at the pronouns (I/we = first person; he/she/it/they = third person).
2. Determine if the narrator knows only one character's thoughts or everyone's.

**Practice:** What is the point of view?
- "Marcus watched the game nervously. His sister, however, was completely relaxed." → **Third Person Omniscient** (we know both Marcus's and his sister's feelings).

### 5.2 Types of Conflict

**Conflict** is the problem or struggle in a story.

#### Internal Conflict
A struggle **within a character** (mind vs. feelings, choices, fears).
- *Man vs. Self:* A girl must decide whether to tell the truth about a lie she told.

#### External Conflict
A struggle **between a character and an outside force.**

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Man vs. Man** | Character vs. another character | A hero fights a villain |
| **Man vs. Nature** | Character vs. natural forces | A family survives a hurricane |
| **Man vs. Society** | Character vs. rules, norms, or laws | A student protests an unfair school rule |
| **Man vs. Technology** | Character vs. machines or technology | A robot turns against its creator |
| **Man vs. Fate / Supernatural** | Character vs. destiny or magical forces | A cursed prince tries to break the spell |

**Practice:** Identify the conflict:
- A sailor is lost at sea during a storm. → **Man vs. Nature**
- A boy is torn between lying to protect his friend or telling the truth. → **Man vs. Self**

### 5.3 Theme

The **theme** is the **central message or lesson** of a story. It is not the topic — it is what the author wants to say *about* the topic.

**Common themes in literature:**
- Courage and perseverance
- The importance of friendship
- Good vs. evil
- Growing up / coming of age
- The danger of greed
- The power of love
- Identity and self-discovery
- Freedom vs. oppression

**How to find the theme:**
1. Ask: *What did the main character learn?*
2. Look at the conflict and its resolution.
3. Consider recurring symbols, images, or ideas.
4. State the theme as a full sentence.

> **Topic:** Friendship
> **Theme:** True friendship means standing by someone even in difficult times.

**Practice:** Read the description and identify the theme:
- A boy practises piano every day for a year, even when he wants to quit, and finally performs at the recital.
- **Theme:** Perseverance leads to success. / Hard work pays off.

### 5.4 Symbolism

A **symbol** is an object, character, or image that represents a **larger idea or concept** beyond its literal meaning.

| Symbol | Common Meaning |
|---|---|
| A dove | Peace |
| A rose | Love / beauty |
| A chain | Imprisonment / restriction |
| Fire | Destruction or purification |
| Water | Rebirth, cleansing, or life |
| A mirror | Self-reflection / truth |
| A crown | Power / authority |
| Darkness | Fear, evil, or the unknown |
| A road / path | Life journey / choices |

> **Example:** In *The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe*, the White Witch represents evil and tyranny, while Aslan represents goodness and sacrifice. The melting of the snow symbolises the end of oppression and the arrival of spring (hope and renewal).

**Tip:** Not every object is a symbol. An object is symbolic only if it appears repeatedly or is given special attention and carries deeper meaning.

### 5.5 Foreshadowing

**Foreshadowing** is a clue or hint early in a story that **suggests what will happen later**. It builds suspense and prepares the reader.

> **Example:** "The old man warned him never to open the red door. 'Some things,' he whispered, 'are better left locked.'" — This foreshadows that the character will eventually open the red door, and something terrible will happen.

**Types of foreshadowing:**
- **Direct foreshadowing:** The narrator or a character explicitly states a warning or prediction.
  - *"'Be careful,' said the guide. 'The mountain is unforgiving.'"*
- **Subtle (indirect) foreshadowing:** Clues are woven into the setting, dialogue, or actions.
  - *Clouds darkened as the ship set sail.* (Hints at a coming storm.)

### 5.6 Irony

**Irony** is a contrast between what is expected and what actually happens. There are three main types.

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Verbal Irony** | A person says the opposite of what they mean (sarcasm) | "Oh, brilliant!" when someone drops a plate. |
| **Situational Irony** | The opposite of what is expected happens | A fire station burns down. |
| **Dramatic Irony** | The audience knows something the characters do not | In a horror movie, we know the killer is behind the door, but the character opens it anyway. |

> **Situational Irony Example:** A man spends years training to be a lifeguard but cannot swim.
>
> **Dramatic Irony Example:** In *Romeo and Juliet*, the audience knows Juliet is not really dead, but Romeo believes she is and poisons himself.

**Practice:** Identify the type of irony:
- A pilot is afraid of flying. → **Situational Irony**
- Saying "What a beautiful day!" during a thunderstorm. → **Verbal Irony**
- We know the character's friend is secretly plotting against him, but the character doesn't. → **Dramatic Irony**

## 6. Punctuation Review

### 6.1 Period (.)
- Ends a declarative sentence: *The sky is blue.*
- Used in abbreviations: *Mr., Dr., etc.*

### 6.2 Question Mark (?)
- Ends a direct question: *Where are you going?*

### 6.3 Exclamation Mark (!)
- Ends an exclamatory sentence or shows strong emotion: *That was amazing!*
- Use sparingly in formal writing.

### 6.4 Comma (,)
- Separates items in a list: *I bought apples, oranges, and bananas.*
- After introductory words/phrases: *In the morning, I go for a run.*
- Before coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) joining independent clauses: *It was raining, so we stayed inside.*
- Separates adjectives of equal importance: *It was a dark, stormy night.*
- In direct address: *Alex, please close the door.*
- After a transitional word: *However, I disagree.*
- Sets off non-essential information (non-defining clauses): *My sister, who lives in Paris, is a chef.*

### 6.5 Semicolon (;)
- Joins two related independent clauses without a conjunction:
  - *The sun was setting; the sky turned orange.*
- Separates items in a complex list (when items contain commas):
  - *I visited Paris, France; Rome, Italy; and Madrid, Spain.*

### 6.6 Colon (:)
- Introduces a list, explanation, or quotation:
  - *Please bring the following: pens, paper, and a ruler.*
- Between hours and minutes: *7:30 p.m.*

### 6.7 Apostrophe (')
- Shows possession: *Sarah's book* / *the students' classroom* (plural) / *the children's toys* (irregular plural)
- Forms contractions: *don't, can't, it's, they're, I'm*
- **⚠️ Common mistakes:**
  - *its* (possessive, no apostrophe) vs. *it's* (it is)
  - *your* (possessive) vs. *you're* (you are)
  - *their* (possessive) vs. *they're* (they are) vs. *there* (location)

### 6.8 Quotation Marks (" ")
- Enclose direct speech: *She said, "I'll be there soon."*
- Enclose titles of short works: poems, articles, song titles, short stories
  - *I read the article "The Future of Space Travel."*

**Punctuation with quotation marks — American convention:**
- Commas and periods go **inside** the closing quotation mark:
  - *He said, "I love reading."*
- Question marks and exclamation marks go **inside** if they belong to the quote, **outside** if they belong to the whole sentence:
  - *Did she say, "I'm leaving"?* (question mark outside — the question is the whole sentence)
  - *She asked, "Are you coming?"* (question mark inside — the question is in the quote)

### 6.9 Hyphen (-)
- Joins compound words: *mother-in-law, well-known, twenty-one*
- Joins prefix to proper noun: *pre-Columbian*

### 6.10 Dash (— or –)
- Used for emphasis or to set off information:
  - *The one thing I wanted — the only thing — was a peaceful afternoon.*
- Interrupts a sentence more strongly than a comma.

### 6.11 Parentheses ( )
- Add extra or non-essential information:
  - *The concert (which was sold out) was fantastic.*

### 6.12 Punctuation Quick-Reference Table

| Mark | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **.** | Ends a statement | This is fun. |
| **?** | Ends a question | How are you? |
| **!** | Shows strong emotion | Watch out! |
| **,** | Separates, pauses | Yes, I agree. |
| **;** | Joins related clauses | I was tired; I went to bed. |
| **:** | Introduces | Here is the list: |
| **'** | Possession / contraction | Sam's book, don't |
| **" "** | Direct speech / titles | "Hello," she said. |
| **-** | Compound words | part-time |
| **—** | Emphasis / interruption | It was amazing — truly amazing. |
| **()** | Extra information | The dog (a golden retriever) barked. |

### Grammar Rules Cheat Sheet

```
✓ Singular subject → singular verb (The dog runs.)
✓ Plural subject → plural verb (The dogs run.)
✓ Perfect progressive = has/have been + verb-ing
✓ Passive = be + past participle + (by agent)
✓ Reported speech = backshift tense, change pronouns & time words
✓ First Conditional = If + present, will + base
✓ Second Conditional = If + past, would + base
✓ Third Conditional = If + past perfect, would have + PP
✓ Defining relative clause = no commas
✓ Non-defining relative clause = commas, no "that"
```
