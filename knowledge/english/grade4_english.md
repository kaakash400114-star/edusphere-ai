# Grade 4 English Language Arts

## Vocabulary

### Greek and Latin Roots

Roots are the building blocks of words. Knowing common Greek and Latin roots helps you figure out the meaning of unfamiliar words.

| Root | Origin | Meaning | Example Words |
|------|--------|---------|---------------|
| **auto** | Greek | self | autograph, automobile, autobiography, automatic |
| **bio** | Greek | life | biology, biography, biosphere, biodegradable |
| **graph** | Greek | write/draw | graphic, autograph, paragraph, telegraph |
| **tele** | Greek | far/distant | telephone, television, telescope, telegraph |
| **scope** | Greek | look/watch | microscope, telescope, periscope, stethoscope |
| **meter** | Greek | measure | thermometer, speedometer, millimeter, perimeter |
| **dict** | Latin | say/speak | dictionary, predict, dictate, contradict |
| **port** | Latin | carry | transport, portable, export, import, support |

**How to use roots:** When you see an unfamiliar word, break it into its parts. For example, "biography" = bio (life) + graph (write) = a written story of someone's life.

### Prefixes

A prefix is a word part added to the **beginning** of a base word that changes its meaning.

| Prefix | Meaning | Example | New Meaning |
|--------|---------|---------|-------------|
| **pre-** | before | preheat | heat before |
| **pre-** | before | preview | view before |
| **pre-** | before | preseason | before the season |
| **mis-** | wrong/badly | misplace | place wrongly |
| **mis-** | wrong/badly | misread | read incorrectly |
| **mis-** | wrong/badly | misunderstand | understand incorrectly |
| **dis-** | not/opposite | disagree | not agree |
| **dis-** | not/opposite | disappear | not appear / vanish |
| **dis-** | not/opposite | disapprove | not approve |
| **over-** | too much | overcook | cook too much |
| **over-** | too much | overload | load too much |
| **over-** | too much | overjoyed | too happy |
| **sub-** | under/below | submarine | under water |
| **sub-** | under/below | subway | under ground |
| **sub-** | under/below | subtract | pull under (take away) |
| **trans-** | across | transport | carry across |
| **trans-** | across | transplant | plant across (move) |
| **trans-** | across | translate | carry meaning across languages |

### Suffixes

A suffix is a word part added to the **end** of a base word that changes its meaning or part of speech.

| Suffix | Meaning | Example | Part of Speech Change |
|--------|---------|---------|----------------------|
| **-tion** | action or state | education, creation, fiction | Verb → Noun (educate → education) |
| **-tion** | action or state | celebration, information, station | Verb → Noun (celebrate → celebration) |
| **-ment** | action or state | enjoyment, movement, achievement | Verb → Noun (enjoy → enjoyment) |
| **-ment** | action or state | development, excitement, placement | Verb → Noun (develop → development) |
| **-able** | can be done | comfortable, enjoyable, believable | Verb → Adjective (enjoy → enjoyable) |
| **-able** | can be done | breakable, trainable, washable | Verb → Adjective (wash → washable) |
| **-ible** | can be done | visible, horrible, sensible | Verb → Adjective (sense → sensible) |
| **-ible** | can be done | incredible, flexible, responsible | Verb → Adjective (respond → responsible) |
| **-ous** | full of | famous, nervous, dangerous | Noun → Adjective (danger → dangerous) |
| **-ous** | full of | courageous, generous, curious | Noun → Adjective (courage → courageous) |

**Tip:** When adding a suffix that begins with a vowel (like -able), sometimes the final "e" in the base word is dropped (like "notice" → "noticeable" keeps the "e" for pronunciation, but "make" → "makable" becomes "makeable" - though we spell it "makeable" or "mailable").

### Synonyms with Shades of Meaning

Synonyms are words that have similar meanings, but they often have different **shades of meaning** - slight differences in intensity, emotion, or how they are used.

#### "Angry" vs. "Furious" vs. "Irritated"

- **Irritated** - mildly annoyed; a small, lingering feeling. Example: *"She felt irritated when her brother kept tapping his pencil."*
- **Angry** - upset; a general, noticeable feeling of displeasure. Example: *"He was angry when his team lost the game."*
- **Furious** - extremely angry; the strongest of the three; nearly out of control. Example: *"The teacher was furious when the students cheated on the test."*

**Think of it like a volume dial:** irritated is low, angry is medium, furious is loud!

#### More Examples of Shades of Meaning

| Light/Mild | Medium | Strong/Intense |
|------------|--------|----------------|
| peek | look | stare |
| whisper | speak | shout |
| jog | run | sprint |
| chilly | cold | freezing |
| large | huge | enormous |
| sad | unhappy | devastated |

**Practice:** Choose the best word for each sentence:
1. "The dog was _____ when the mail carrier walked past." (mild barking) → *irritated*
2. "My sister was _____ when I accidentally broke her favorite toy." (very upset) → *furious*
3. "The coach was _____ about the team's poor performance." (noticeably upset) → *angry*

### Figurative Language

Figurative language uses words in creative ways - not just their literal meaning - to make writing more interesting and expressive.

#### Alliteration

Alliteration is the repetition of the same **beginning consonant sound** in a group of words.

- *"**P**eter **P**iper **p**icked a **p**eck of **p**ickled **p**eppers."*
- *"The **s**illy **s**nake **s**lithered **s**lowly."*
- *"Five **f**urry **f**oxes **f**ound a **f**eather."*

Alliteration makes writing sound musical and memorable. It is often used in poetry, tongue twisters, and advertising.

#### Onomatopoeia

Onomatopoeia is a word that **imitates the sound** it represents.

- *Buzz* - bees buzzing
- *Crack* - thunder cracking
- *Hiss* - a snake hissing
- *Pop* - a balloon popping
- *Sizzle* - bacon sizzling in a pan
- *Thud* - a heavy book falling
- *Whisper* - wind in the trees
- *Bang* - a door slamming shut

Onomatopoeia helps readers **hear** what is happening in the text.

#### Hyperbole

Hyperbole is an **extreme exaggeration** used to make a point or add emphasis. It is not meant to be taken literally.

- *"I'm so hungry I could eat a horse!"* (You wouldn't really eat a horse.)
- *"It's raining cats and dogs!"* (Animals aren't actually falling from the sky.)
- *"I've told you a million times to clean your room!"* (Not exactly a million.)
- *"This backpack weighs a ton!"* (It doesn't actually weigh 2,000 pounds.)
- *"My legs turned to jelly after that race."* (Your legs are still made of bone and muscle.)

Hyperbole makes writing more dramatic, humorous, and expressive.

#### Personification

Personification gives **human qualities** to non-human things - objects, animals, or ideas.

- *"The wind **whispered** through the trees."* (Wind can't actually whisper.)
- *"The sun **smiled** down on us."* (The sun doesn't have a face.)
- *"The alarm clock **yelled** at me to wake up."* (Alarm clocks don't literally yell.)
- *"The flowers **danced** in the breeze."* (Flowers can't dance.)
- *"The old house **groaned** in the storm."* (Houses don't groan.)

Personification helps readers form vivid pictures in their minds and connect emotionally with descriptions.

## Grammar

### The Eight Parts of Speech

Every word in the English language belongs to at least one of the eight parts of speech.

#### 1. Nouns
A noun is a word that names a **person, place, thing, or idea**.

- **Common nouns:** dog, city, book, happiness, teacher
- **Proper nouns:** Mrs. Johnson, Chicago, *Harry Potter*, Tuesday, the Grand Canyon (always capitalized)

Nouns can also be classified as:
- **Concrete nouns** - things you can see, hear, touch, taste, or smell (apple, thunder, perfume)
- **Abstract nouns** - ideas, feelings, or qualities you cannot touch (freedom, bravery, love, knowledge)

#### 2. Pronouns
A pronoun is a word that **takes the place of a noun**.

- **Subject pronouns:** I, you, he, she, it, we, they (The boy **ran** → **He** ran.)
- **Object pronouns:** me, you, him, her, it, us, them (Give the ball to **me**.)
- **Possessive pronouns:** my, your, his, her, its, our, their (This is **her** book.)
- **Demonstrative pronouns:** this, that, these, those (**This** is the one I want.)

#### 3. Verbs
A verb is a word that shows **action** or a **state of being**.

- **Action verbs:** run, jump, think, write, sing, build
- **Linking verbs:** am, is, are, was, were, be, being, been, seem, become, feel, look, taste (She **is** tall. The soup **tastes** delicious.)
- **Helping verbs:** have, has, had, do, does, did, will, would, can, could, shall, should, may, might, must (She **has** finished. They **will** arrive soon.)

#### 4. Adjectives
An adjective is a word that **describes a noun or pronoun**. It tells which one, what kind, or how many.

- What kind: **red** balloon, **tall** building, **cold** water
- How many: **three** cats, **several** students, **many** books
- Which one: **that** dog, **this** shirt, **the last** cookie

**Articles** are a special type of adjective: **a, an, the**.

#### 5. Adverbs
An adverb is a word that **describes a verb, adjective, or another adverb**. It tells how, when, where, or to what extent.

- How: She sings **beautifully**. He runs **quickly**.
- When: We will leave **soon**. They arrived **yesterday**.
- Where: The bird flew **outside**. She looked **everywhere**.
- To what extent: The test was **extremely** hard. I **almost** dropped it.

Many adverbs end in **-ly**, but not all. Words like *very, too, often, never, always, well, fast, hard* are also adverbs.

#### 6. Prepositions
A preposition is a word that shows a **relationship** between a noun (or pronoun) and another word in the sentence. It often indicates location, time, or direction.

- **Location:** in, on, under, above, between, behind, beside, near, at
- **Time:** before, after, during, since, until, by
- **Direction:** to, from, into, through, across, toward

Example: *"The cat sat **on** the mat."* - "On" shows the relationship between the cat and the mat.

#### 7. Conjunctions
A conjunction is a word that **connects words, phrases, or clauses** together.

- **Coordinating conjunctions** (FANBOYS): For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So
  - *I like apples **and** bananas.*
  - *She was tired, **but** she kept working.*
  - *We can go to the park, **or** we can stay home.*

- **Subordinating conjunctions** (connect an independent clause to a dependent clause - see Sentence Types below): because, although, when, if, while, since, until, before, after

#### 8. Interjections
An interjection is a word or phrase that expresses **strong emotion** and is usually followed by an exclamation mark or a comma.

- *Wow! That's amazing!*
- *Oh no, I forgot my homework!*
- *Yikes, that's a big spider!*
- *Hooray! We won the game!*

Interjections have no grammatical connection to the rest of the sentence.

### Sentence Types by Structure

Sentences are classified by how many clauses they contain. A **clause** is a group of words with a subject and a verb.

#### Simple Sentences
A simple sentence has **one independent clause** (one complete thought). It may have a compound subject or compound predicate, but it is still a simple sentence.

- *The dog barked.* (one subject, one predicate)
- *Maria and Carlos played soccer.* (compound subject, one predicate)
- *The cat climbed the tree and chased a squirrel.* (one subject, compound predicate)

#### Compound Sentences
A compound sentence has **two or more independent clauses** joined by a **coordinating conjunction** (FANBOYS) with a comma, or by a semicolon.

- *The sun was shining, **so** we went to the beach.*
- *She likes to read, **but** he prefers to watch movies.*
- *I finished my homework; I can go outside now.*

#### Complex Sentences
A complex sentence has **one independent clause** and **at least one dependent clause**. A dependent clause cannot stand alone as a complete sentence. It begins with a **subordinating conjunction**.

**Common subordinating conjunctions:**

| Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
|-------------|---------|---------|
| **because** | for the reason that | We stayed inside *because* it was raining. |
| **although** | even though | *Although* she was nervous, she gave a great speech. |
| **when** | at the time that | *When* the bell rang, the students packed up. |
| **if** | on the condition that | *If* you study hard, you will do well on the test. |
| **while** | during the time that | *While* Mom was cooking, I set the table. |

In a complex sentence, when the dependent clause comes **first**, use a comma after it. When the independent clause comes first, **no comma** is needed before the subordinating conjunction.

- ✅ *Because it was raining, we stayed inside.* (comma after dependent clause)
- ✅ *We stayed inside because it was raining.* (no comma needed)
- ✅ *Although she was nervous, she gave a great speech.* (comma after dependent clause)

### Run-On Sentences

A run-on sentence is two or more complete sentences (independent clauses) jammed together **without proper punctuation or conjunctions**.

**Types of run-ons:**

1. **Fused sentence** - two sentences with no punctuation between them:
   - ❌ *The dog barked the mail carrier ran away.*

2. **Comma splice** - two sentences joined only by a comma (no conjunction):
   - ❌ *The dog barked, the mail carrier ran away.*

**How to fix run-ons:**

1. **Add a period** to make two separate sentences:
   - ✅ *The dog barked. The mail carrier ran away.*

2. **Add a comma and a coordinating conjunction** (FANBOYS):
   - ✅ *The dog barked, and the mail carrier ran away.*

3. **Add a semicolon**:
   - ✅ *The dog barked; the mail carrier ran away.*

4. **Add a subordinating conjunction** to make one clause dependent:
   - ✅ *When the dog barked, the mail carrier ran away.*

### Sentence Fragments

A fragment is an **incomplete sentence** - it is missing a subject, a verb, or a complete thought. Fragments cannot stand alone.

- ❌ *Running down the street.* (Missing subject - Who is running?)
- ❌ *The big brown dog.* (Missing verb - What did the dog do?)
- ❌ *Because she was late.* (Missing a complete thought - What happened because she was late?)

**How to fix fragments:**

- ✅ *She was running down the street.* (Added subject)
- ✅ *The big brown dog barked loudly.* (Added verb)
- ✅ *Because she was late, she missed the bus.* (Added independent clause)

### Subject-Verb Agreement

The subject and verb in a sentence must **agree in number** - if the subject is singular, the verb must be singular; if the subject is plural, the verb must be plural.

**Singular subject → singular verb:**
- *The cat **runs** quickly.*
- *She **plays** the piano.*
- *The student **studies** every night.*

**Plural subject → plural verb:**
- *The cats **run** quickly.*
- *They **play** the piano.*
- *The students **study** every night.*

**Tricky cases:**

1. **Compound subjects joined by "and"** → plural verb:
   - *Tom and Jerry **are** friends.*
   - *The dog and the cat **chase** each other.*

2. **Compound subjects joined by "or" or "nor"** → verb matches the subject **closer to it**:
   - *Neither the cat nor the dogs **were** outside.* (verb matches "dogs" - closer)
   - *Neither the dogs nor the cat **was** outside.* (verb matches "cat" - closer)

3. **Don't be fooled by prepositional phrases!** The subject is NEVER in a prepositional phrase:
   - ✅ *The box of chocolates **is** on the table.* (subject = box, singular)
   - ❌ *The box of chocolates **are** on the table.* (incorrect)

4. **Collective nouns** (group words like *team, class, family, group*) usually take a **singular** verb:
   - *The team **is** ready for the game.*
   - *My family **lives** in Texas.*

### Tense Consistency

Verb tenses tell **when** an action happens. Keeping the same tense throughout a paragraph or passage is called **tense consistency**.

| Tense | Form | Example |
|-------|-------|---------|
| **Present** | base form / base + s | She walks to school. He reads daily. |
| **Past** | base + -ed (or irregular) | She walked to school. He read the book. |
| **Future** | will + base form | She will walk to school. He will read tomorrow. |

**Inconsistent (wrong):**
❌ *Yesterday, we went to the park and play soccer. We have a great time.* (Mixes past and present.)

**Consistent (correct):**
✅ *Yesterday, we **went** to the park and **played** soccer. We **had** a great time.* (All past tense.)

**Rules for tense consistency:**
- Stay in one tense unless there is a real reason to change (like a flashback or time shift).
- If you are telling a story in the past, keep everything in the past.
- If you are describing general facts, keep everything in the present.

### Quotation Marks for Dialogue

Quotation marks show the **exact words** a character or person says.

**Rules:**

1. **Opening quotation mark before the first spoken word; closing mark after the last:**
   - *"I'm going to the store,"* Mom said.

2. **Capitalize the first letter** of a direct quote if it is a complete sentence:
   - She said, *"We should leave now."*

3. **Commas and periods go INSIDE the quotation marks** (in American English):
   - *"I love reading,"* said Maya. ✅
   - *"I love reading",* said Maya. ❌

4. **Place a comma** between the speaker tag and the quote:
   - Maya said, *"Let's study together."*
   - *"Let's study together,"* Maya said.

5. **Start a new paragraph** every time a new character speaks:
   - "Did you finish your homework?" asked Jake.
   - "Not yet," Maria replied. "I need help with math."

6. **Use single quotation marks** for a quote within a quote:
   - Dad said, *"I heard the coach say, 'Great job!' after the game."*

### Commas

Commas help clarify meaning and separate ideas. Here are the key uses for 4th grade:

#### 1. Commas in a Series (List)
Use commas to separate three or more items in a list. Place a comma before the conjunction (this is called the **Oxford comma** and is the standard in American English).

- *I bought apples, bananas, **and** oranges.*
- *She likes reading, drawing, **and** singing.*

#### 2. Commas After Introductory Words or Phrases
Use a comma after an introductory element at the beginning of a sentence.

- *Yes, I would like some water.*
- *In the morning, we have math class.*
- *After lunch, we went to recess.*
- *Wow, that was a great game!*

#### 3. Commas for Direct Address
Use commas to set off the name of the person being spoken to (direct address).

- *Marcus, please pass the salt.*
- *I think, Mom, that we should go.*
- *Thank you, Mrs. Lee, for the help.*

#### 4. Other comma uses:
- **Between adjectives** (when they describe the same noun equally): *It was a dark, stormy night.*
- **In compound sentences** (before FANBOYS): *She was tired, but she kept running.*
- **In dates and addresses:** *December 25, 2025, was a snowy day.*

## Reading

### Text Structure

Authors organize their writing in different ways called **text structures**. Recognizing the structure helps you understand the author's purpose and the main ideas.

#### Cause and Effect
The author explains **why** something happened (cause) and **what happened as a result** (effect).

- **Signal words:** because, so, since, as a result, therefore, consequently, due to, led to
- **Example:** *"Because the bridge collapsed during the storm (cause), the town had to find a new route for deliveries (effect)."*

**Clue questions:** Why did this happen? What was the result?

#### Compare and Contrast
The author shows how two or more things are **alike** (compare) and **different** (contrast).

- **Signal words:** similarly, likewise, also, both, however, but, on the other hand, in contrast, different from, unlike
- **Example:** *"Both dolphins and sharks live in the ocean (compare). However, dolphins are mammals that breathe air, while sharks are fish with gills (contrast)."*

**Clue questions:** How are they the same? How are they different?

#### Problem and Solution
The author presents a **problem** and explains one or more **solutions**.

- **Signal words:** problem, solution, issue, challenge, resolve, solve, fix, addressed, overcome
- **Example:** *"The school had a problem with litter on the playground. To solve it, the student council started a recycling program and placed more trash cans around the yard."*

**Clue questions:** What is the problem? How was it solved?

#### Chronological Order (Sequence)
The author presents events in the **order in which they happened** (time order).

- **Signal words:** first, next, then, after that, finally, meanwhile, soon, before, during, earlier, later
- **Example:** *"First, the scientist gathered her materials. Next, she mixed the chemicals in a beaker. Then, she carefully heated the mixture. Finally, she recorded her observations."*

**Clue questions:** What happened first? What happened next? What happened last?

#### Description
The author describes a **person, place, thing, or idea** by giving details, characteristics, and sensory information.

- **Signal words:** for example, such as, characteristics, features, for instance, specifically, including
- **Example:** *"The Grand Canyon is a massive gorge in Arizona. It is over 277 miles long, up to 18 miles wide, and more than a mile deep. Its layered bands of colorful rock reveal millions of years of geological history."*

**Clue questions:** What is being described? What details help you picture it?

### Summarizing with SWBST

A good summary is brief and captures the most important ideas. The **SWBST** method helps you write strong summaries.

| Letter | Stands For | Question to Ask |
|--------|-----------|-----------------|
| **S** | Somebody | Who is the main character or subject? |
| **W** | Wanted | What did the character want or need? |
| **B** | But | What was the problem or conflict? |
| **S** | So | What did the character do about it? |
| **T** | Then | How did it end? What was the resolution? |

**Example - *Charlotte's Web* by E.B. White:**

- **S** (Somebody): Wilbur the pig
- **W** (Wanted): wanted to stay alive and not be sent to slaughter
- **B** (But): he was a runt pig being raised for meat
- **S** (So): his friend Charlotte the spider wrote messages in her web to make people think Wilbur was special
- **T** (Then): Wilbur was saved, and Charlotte's babies were born before she died

**Summary:** Wilbur the pig wanted to stay alive, but he was being raised for slaughter. His friend Charlotte the spider wrote messages in her web to convince people Wilbur was special. Because of this, Wilbur was saved, and Charlotte's babies lived on after she died.

### Point of View

Point of view (POV) tells **who is telling the story**.

#### First Person
The narrator is **a character in the story** and uses pronouns like **I, me, my, we, us**.

- *"I couldn't believe what I saw when I opened the door. My heart raced as I stepped inside."*
- The reader only knows what the narrator thinks and feels - you cannot see into other characters' minds.

#### Third Person
The narrator is **outside the story** and uses pronouns like **he, she, it, they, the character's name**.

- *"Sarah walked into the room cautiously. She noticed the strange painting on the wall."*
- Third-person limited: the narrator follows one character's thoughts and feelings.
- Third-person omniscient (all-knowing): the narrator knows what ALL characters think and feel.

**How to determine POV:** Look at the pronouns. If the story uses "I" or "me," it's first person. If it uses "he," "she," or character names, it's third person.

### Symbolism

A **symbol** is an object, character, figure, or color that represents an **abstract idea or meaning** beyond its literal appearance.

- **A dove** = peace
- **A red rose** = love
- **A storm** = conflict or danger
- **A crown** = power or authority
- **Spring** = new beginnings or hope
- **Darkness** = fear or the unknown

**In literature:**
- In *The Wizard of Oz*, the **Emerald City glasses** symbolize how people's perceptions can be altered by deception.
- In many stories, a **journey or path** symbolizes a character's personal growth or the passage from childhood to adulthood.

**How to find symbols:** Ask yourself: *"What object keeps appearing in the story? Does it seem to represent a bigger idea?"*

### Foreshadowing

**Foreshadowing** is a clue or hint that the author drops early in a story to **suggest what will happen later**. It builds suspense and prepares the reader.

- *"The sky grew dark and the wind picked up. The leaves on the trees trembled."* → This foreshadows a storm coming.
- *"She locked every door and checked each window twice before going to bed."* → This foreshadows that something dangerous might happen.
- *"He hadn't studied for the test, but he wasn't worried. 'It'll be fine,' he told himself."* → This foreshadows that he will likely do poorly.

**Foreshadowing vs. Flashback:**
- **Foreshadowing** - hints about what WILL happen (forward-looking)
- **Flashback** - shows what already happened in the past (backward-looking)

### Characterization

**Characterization** is the way an author reveals a character's personality and traits.

#### Direct Characterization
The author **directly states** what the character is like.

- *"Julia was the kindest girl in the class, always sharing her supplies and helping others."*
- *"Marcus was known for his terrible temper."*

#### Indirect Characterization
The author **shows** the character's personality through:
- **Speech** - What the character says and how they say it
- **Thoughts** - What the character thinks and feels
- **Effects on others** - How other characters react to them
- **Actions** - What the character does
- **Looks** - How the character dresses or appears

**Example of indirect characterization:**
*"Tom knocked over Lucy's pencil case and kept walking without apologizing. When the teacher asked who did it, he looked away and whistled."*

→ We can infer that Tom is **careless** and **not very honest** without the author saying so directly.

**STEAL method for indirect characterization:**
- **S**peech - What does the character say?
- **T**houghts - What does the character think?
- **E**ffect on others - How do others react?
- **A**ctions - What does the character do?
- **L**ooks - How does the character look?

## Writing

### Research Writing

Research writing involves gathering information from sources and presenting it in your own words.

#### Note-Taking
Good researchers take organized notes so they can find information easily later.

**Strategies:**
- **Use a graphic organizer** (like a T-chart, web, or outline) to organize facts.
- **Write down the source** (title, author, website) for every piece of information.
- **Write in your own words** - do NOT copy sentences from the source.
- **Record only the most important facts** - don't write down everything.

**Example note card:**
| Topic: | Solar System |
|--------|-------------|
| Fact: | Jupiter is the largest planet - it is so big that over 1,300 Earths could fit inside it. |
| Source: | *Space for Kids* by Jane Smith, page 24 |

#### Paraphrasing
**Paraphrasing** means restating someone else's ideas **in your own words** without changing the meaning. It is different from copying (plagiarism).

**Original text:** *"The honeybee is one of the most important pollinators in the world. Without honeybees, many of our favorite foods, including apples, almonds, and blueberries, would not grow."*

**Good paraphrase:** *"Honeybees play a vital role in pollination. Many foods we enjoy, such as apples and blueberries, depend on these bees to grow."*

**Bad paraphrase (too close to original):** *"The honeybee is an important pollinator in the world. Without them, many foods like apples, almonds, and blueberries wouldn't grow."* (This is still too similar - it's plagiarism.)

**Steps to paraphrase:**
1. Read the original text carefully.
2. Cover the text and write what you remember in your own words.
3. Check your version - is it different enough in wording while keeping the same meaning?
4. If it's too similar, try again with different words and sentence structures.

#### Citing Sources
When you use information from a source, you must **give credit** to the original author. This is called **citing your source**.

**For a book:**
- (Author's Last Name, page number)
- Example: (Smith, 24)

**For a website:**
- (Website Name)
- Example: (National Geographic Kids)

**For an article:**
- (Author's Last Name)
- Example: (Johnson)

**Why cite?** It gives credit to the person who did the work, and it makes your writing more trustworthy because readers know where you found your facts.

### Persuasive Essays

A persuasive essay tries to **convince the reader** to agree with your opinion on a topic.

#### Structure of a Persuasive Essay

**1. Introduction**
- **Hook:** Grab the reader's attention with a question, fact, or bold statement.
- **Claim (Thesis):** State your opinion clearly. This is the main argument of the whole essay.
- **Example:** *"Every student in our school should have at least 30 minutes of recess every day."*

**2. Body Paragraphs (Reasons + Evidence)**
Each body paragraph presents **one reason** that supports your claim, backed by **evidence** (facts, examples, statistics, expert opinions, personal experience).

- **Reason 1:** Physical health benefits
  - Evidence: *Studies show that children who get regular physical activity have stronger bones and healthier hearts.*
- **Reason 2:** Better focus in class
  - Evidence: *Research from the American Academy of Pediatrics found that recess improves students' concentration and classroom behavior.*
- **Reason 3:** Social and emotional development
  - Evidence: *Recess gives children a chance to practice teamwork, resolve conflicts, and make friends.*

**3. Counterargument (Optional but Strong)**
Acknowledge the other side's opinion, then explain why your position is still stronger.

- *"Some people argue that students need more time for academics. However, research shows that students actually perform better academically when they have regular breaks for physical activity."*

**4. Conclusion**
- Restate your claim in different words.
- Summarize your main reasons briefly.
- End with a call to action or a powerful closing statement.
- *Example:* *"Recess is not a luxury - it is a necessity. Students need physical activity, social interaction, and mental breaks to thrive. Our schools must make sure every child gets the daily recess they deserve."*

#### Persuasive Techniques (Ethos, Pathos, Logos)
- **Ethos (Credibility):** Use reliable sources and expert opinions to build trust.
- **Pathos (Emotion):** Appeal to the reader's feelings with stories, vivid language, and emotional examples.
- **Logos (Logic):** Use facts, statistics, and logical reasoning to make your argument.

### Narrative Writing with Developed Characters

A narrative is a **story** - real or imagined. In 4th grade, you should write stories with **well-developed characters**, a clear plot, and descriptive details.

#### Developing Characters
- Give your characters **specific traits** (brave, curious, shy, humorous) and show these traits through their **actions, dialogue, and thoughts**.
- Give your characters **motivations** - reasons for what they do.
- Give your characters **flaws or challenges** - no one is perfect, and flaws make characters interesting.
- Show how characters **change or grow** over the course of the story.

#### Story Structure
- **Beginning (Exposition):** Introduce the characters, setting, and the normal world before the conflict.
- **Middle (Rising Action + Climax):** The main character faces a problem or conflict. The tension builds until the **climax** - the most exciting or turning point.
- **End (Falling Action + Resolution):** The problem is solved (or not), and the character has changed in some way.

#### Using Dialogue
- Use dialogue to reveal character personality and move the plot forward.
- Use correct punctuation: quotation marks, commas, and tags (said, asked, whispered, exclaimed).
- *"I can't believe we have to walk home in this rain!"* Jake grumbled, pulling his jacket tighter.

### Revising and Editing

**Revising** and **editing** are different - and both are essential steps in the writing process.

#### Revising (Making It Better)
Revising means improving the **ideas, organization, and content** of your writing.

- **Add** missing details, examples, or descriptions.
- **Remove** parts that are off-topic or unnecessary.
- **Replace** weak words with stronger ones ("said" → "exclaimed," "nice" → "extraordinary").
- **Move** sentences or paragraphs to a better order.
- **Combine** short, choppy sentences into more complex ones.

**Revising questions to ask yourself:**
- Does my introduction hook the reader?
- Do I have enough details and evidence to support my ideas?
- Is my writing organized in a logical order?
- Does my conclusion wrap things up effectively?
- Are my characters believable and well-developed?

#### Editing (Making It Correct)
Editing means fixing the **grammar, spelling, punctuation, and capitalization** errors.

**Editing checklist:**
- ☐ Every sentence starts with a capital letter.
- ☐ Every sentence ends with the correct punctuation mark.
- ☐ Subject and verb agree in every sentence.
- ☐ Commas are used correctly (series, introductory elements, dialogue, etc.).
- ☐ Quotation marks are used correctly for dialogue.
- ☐ Commonly misspelled words are checked.
- ☐ Verb tenses are consistent throughout.
- ☐ No run-on sentences or fragments remain.
- ☐ Possessives use apostrophes correctly (dog's vs. dogs').

**The Writing Process (Review):**
1. **Prewriting** - brainstorm, plan, organize
2. **Drafting** - write your first version
3. **Revising** - improve ideas and content
4. **Editing** - fix grammar and mechanics
5. **Publishing** - share your final piece
